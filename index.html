Morning Briefing

Friday, October 30, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, The New York Times Morning newsletter, and the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented with current web reporting.

Good morning, it's Friday, October 30, 2026. Two stories are rattling markets before the opening bell: the biggest cryptocurrency heist ever recorded, and the worst single day in Alphabet's history. Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, has suspended deposits and withdrawals after hackers drained roughly six point three billion dollars from its wallets across ten blockchains. The company says the breach began with a compromised laptop that gave an attacker access to a service container and let them submit unauthorized withdrawals. About three billion dollars has since been frozen, including more than two point three billion by the stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle, and the blockchain analytics firm TRM Labs is pointing the finger at North Korea. Binance has promised to cover all losses and insists customer funds are safe, but bitcoin slid nearly five percent to around seventy-seven thousand dollars.

The tech selloff has a different cause. On Thursday, Alphabet lost about half a trillion dollars in market value as its stock fell thirteen percent, its worst day on record, even though revenue grew twenty-five percent and Google Cloud more than doubled. Investors were unhappy that Google's core services unit missed expectations, and the reaction captures a deeper worry: that search is slowing as artificial intelligence reshapes how people look for things, while the cost of building out AI may be outrunning the returns. The Nasdaq fell one point four percent Thursday, while the S&P 500 and the Dow each slipped about a third of a percent. This morning, Nasdaq futures are down more than two percent. Intel plunged twenty-two percent in after-hours trading on disappointing results and a forecast for a prolonged slowdown in PC sales, and South Korea's Kospi dropped more than five percent at the open. Amazon Web Services was a bright spot, posting forty-six billion dollars in quarterly revenue, up thirty-nine percent.

The broader economy looks sturdier than the markets. Output grew at a three point four percent annual rate in the third quarter, the fastest pace in a year, powered by business investment and steady consumer spending despite high gas prices. Yet many consumers still give the economy poor marks, perhaps because inflation has been accelerating. Today brings the employment cost index, a fresh read on wages. In deal news, DoorDash is buying Instacart for seventeen billion dollars, and Josh Kushner's Thrive Holdings agreed to acquire the home security company ADT for seven point six billion.

Oil is near six-month highs, with Brent above one hundred seventeen dollars a barrel, as the Iran standoff intensifies. Admiral Brad Cooper, the head of U.S. Central Command, briefed President Trump on military options on Thursday, alongside Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, with the focus on the naval blockade of Iranian ports. Reports say Trump has signaled that any strikes could come after Tuesday's midterm elections. The Journal took readers aboard the USS George Washington, where jets launch at night toward the Strait of Hormuz to shoot down Iranian missiles and drones. The military says it has helped more than three thousand commercial vessels through the strait in six months, though the relentless pace is straining aircraft and crews.

That brings us to the midterms, which end in a chaotic finish. In Alaska, an October surprise has scrambled the Senate race. The Justice Department issued a new wave of subpoenas in an investigation into whether Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola's campaign recruited a spoiler candidate to siphon votes from Republican Senator Dan Sullivan. Even if Peltola wins, Republicans may try to block her from taking the seat. Elsewhere, Trump canceled rallies in Kansas and Iowa, where polls show tight races; early voting in Georgia has set a midterm turnout record; and election officials and the administration are eyeing each other with deep suspicion, including over a new federal criminal investigation into noncitizens on California's voter rolls. High inflation, meanwhile, is haunting Republicans, and control of the Senate remains very much up for grabs.

On policy, the administration promised to ease tariffs on automakers that commit to more U.S. manufacturing, a shift that emerged from talks to get Audi to build a plant in Georgia. It also agreed to send about thirty-five million dollars in aid to a Central African military unit accused of war crimes, as part of a deal to deport third-country migrants there. And big tech companies, including Google and Meta, are quietly resisting broad immigration-enforcement demands for user data. Separately, both the Journal and the Times report that officials grew alarmed about a Chinese-linked figure with close ties to Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, with the Times adding that Trump was told and took no action.

Overseas, Ukraine struck a Russian oil refinery, ending a two-week pause that came under heavy pressure from Washington, while a Russian strike killed at least fourteen people on a crowded rail platform in Dnipro. In Israel, the final vote count leaves Prime Minister Netanyahu's bloc at fifty-three seats, short of a majority, and weeks of uncertainty lie ahead. Israeli Arab parties won nineteen seats, a record, though many worry that won't become real influence. In China, Xi Jinping is tightening his grip ahead of a key personnel shuffle next year. And in Spain, dozens were killed in a high-speed train crash near Toledo, making rail safety an election issue.

Closer to home, the Journal has a troubling look at small businesses lured by merchant cash advances, which promise cash in a day but can carry effective costs of two thousand percent. More than half of small-business bankruptcies this year involved these lenders.

We close with Patrick Stewart, the acclaimed English actor who commanded the starship Enterprise as Captain Picard and led the X-Men as Professor Xavier. He died Wednesday at his home in Los Angeles at eighty-six. And a lighter note: a Chinese company has built a thousand-dollar, AI-powered device that tracks mosquitoes in flight and burns their wings off with a laser.

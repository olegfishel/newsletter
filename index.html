Morning Briefing

Friday, October 23, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, The New York Times Morning newsletter, and the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented by current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Friday, October 23, 2026. We begin with a rough day on Wall Street, where the artificial intelligence trade took a sharp hit. The Nasdaq fell 2.6 percent on Thursday, its worst session since June, and the S&P 500 lost about 1.2 percent, while the Dow finished essentially flat. The trigger was Meta Platforms, which disclosed that its lease agreements and other non-cancelable commitments for AI infrastructure now total roughly 825 billion dollars, up from about 700 billion in July, and reported that its quarterly free cash flow swung to a deficit of around 2 billion dollars. Meta shares dropped nearly 7 percent, and the damage spread to chipmakers, with AMD, Micron and Nvidia all sharply lower. The Journal says the episode struck a raw nerve as investors rotated out of AI-linked stocks. Treasuries rallied, pulling the ten-year yield down by more than 18 basis points, though it still sits close to 5 percent. Futures this morning point to a softer open, with Dow futures down about half a percent.

Oil is adding to the unease. Brent crude jumped about 4 percent to above 113 dollars a barrel, a five-month high, after Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia raised fresh fears about energy supplies. Riyadh's main airport has reopened only to limited traffic, Saudi Arabia's national oil company has pulled out staff, and the United States has told some government employees and their families to leave the kingdom. In the Persian Gulf, Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it hit several vessels, including tankers that caught fire and ships that struck mines west of the Strait of Hormuz. Tehran is also warning that if the United States and Israel resume airstrikes, it will retaliate against airports, pipelines and other civilian targets across the Gulf. President Trump says the U.S. has already knocked the hell out of Iran and may act a little further fairly soon, and American and Israeli military planners are reported to have drawn up options for renewed joint strikes in the coming weeks. Talks remain stalled despite the memorandum signed in June. The Journal adds that the conflicts have scrambled civil aviation, with the Houthi strikes upending one of the last workable routes between Europe and Asia and leaving airlines to pick their own paths through dangerous skies.

In Ukraine, the story is Starlink. The Journal reports that Trump phoned Elon Musk and asked him to lift restrictions on the military-grade version of the service that Ukraine relies on, and U.S. and Ukrainian officials say it is now working more normally. President Zelenskyy says it is still slower than usual, and Trump would not say whether he made the call himself.

In Washington, the Justice Department is reviewing allegations that Corey Lewandowski profited from his role as an adviser to Kristi Noem when she led Homeland Security. The Journal calls it one of the first inquiries of Trump's second term to target someone close to the president. Separately, Calley Means, a senior adviser to Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., is leaving the administration, frustrated by delays to Kennedy's food agenda, and a federal indictment accuses Virginia's powerful Democratic state senator Louise Lucas of illegal marijuana trafficking.

With the midterms less than two weeks away, several of the president's handpicked candidates are in trouble. Ohio's Republican nominee for governor, Vivek Ramaswamy, is under fire for an attack ad featuring the estranged mother of his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton. Trump defended it as very powerful, but a number of Republicans want it pulled. In Texas, Ken Paxton is trailing Democrat James Talarico in several polls despite heavy financial backing from Musk and others. And in California, a federal judge has blocked the Department of Homeland Security from carrying out most of its field operations aimed at uncovering voter fraud. The Times also has a striking report on the border, where the government has installed hundreds of AI-enabled camera towers that can track people and vehicles for miles, with plans to expand them nationwide. The Times estimates at least a million people live within view of them, and most of those it spoke with had no idea what the towers were.

In corporate news, the Journal reveals how three OpenAI safety researchers were fired after the company suspected them of leaking details of its models, an account the researchers dispute, and the story highlights a growing fight over who gets to look inside powerful AI systems. Moderna's stock fell nearly 18 percent after details of its cancer vaccine data leaked early on social media. Barclays says it will cut 5,000 jobs, about 5 percent of its workforce, as it adopts AI at scale, and Comcast warned of a charge of more than a billion dollars as it restructures. The Pentagon signed a long-term deal for Lockheed Martin's F-35 fighters.

Around the world, France has banned gatherings in some cities this weekend after its worst unrest in years, driven by economic distress. A magnitude 7.0 earthquake in Tibet killed at least 16 people and destroyed hundreds of homes, and a typhoon battered Taiwan. In Israel, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot is challenging Prime Minister Netanyahu ahead of what the Journal calls one of the country's most consequential elections. In the race for the next U.N. secretary-general, a late entry from an Emirati diplomat has shaken things up. On climate, severe heat is estimated to have killed 50,000 people in European cities this summer, likely a record, and a U.N. report projects warming of about 2.6 degrees Celsius by 2100.

And to close, the World Series opens tonight with the Milwaukee Brewers facing the Tampa Bay Rays, which guarantees a first-time champion. New York City's council voted to ban horse carriages, and closer to home, San Diego critics are urging the city to pause development of data centers, while the Aztecs head to Colorado State and host USC. Have a good Friday.

Business & Finance

A rotation out of AI-connected stocks helped drive the Nasdaq down 2.6 percent in its worst session since June, while the S&P 500 fell 1.2 percent and the Dow was little changed.

Blackstone's net realizations, which reflect proceeds from asset sales, fell 34 percent from a year earlier, led by a 49 percent decline in its private-equity division.

The Pentagon signed a deal to buy Lockheed Martin's F-35 fighter jets over the next decade, its longest commitment yet among this year's contracts to boost supplies of advanced weaponry.

Comcast's subscriber losses deepened, and the company said it will take a charge of more than a billion dollars in the fourth quarter as it begins a restructuring program.

Carrier intends to invest more than 600 million dollars in a new plant in Fort Worth, Texas, to supply more cooling equipment to large commercial and industrial buildings.

U.S. Steel, which is owned by Tokyo-based Nippon Steel, plans to invest nearly 3 billion dollars in a new hot strip mill at its plant near Pittsburgh.

Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern both reported higher profit and revenue as more businesses shift cargo from road to rail.

SAP is working on a new metric to quantify how much artificial intelligence contributes to its business.

Barclays said it will cut 5,000 jobs, or 5 percent of its workforce, to reduce costs as it adopts artificial intelligence at scale.

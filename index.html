Morning Briefing

Thursday, September 10, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by wire reporting from NPR, Reuters, Bloomberg, and Axios.

Good morning, it's Thursday, September 10, 2026. The dominant story this morning sits at the intersection of a shooting war and the price of gasoline: crude oil has crossed $100 a barrel for the first time since July, driven by escalating military exchanges in and around the Strait of Hormuz, and Americans are feeling it at the pump — diesel reached a new all-time high of $5.94 a gallon this week, and pump prices jumped another seven cents in a single day.

The oil spike is directly tied to a rapidly intensifying confrontation between the United States and Iran. The Wall Street Journal reports that Tehran's forces are taking aim at the U.S. Navy in ways not seen since the war's outbreak, conducting close-call harassment of American warships and striking oil-shipping infrastructure in what analysts describe as a deliberate attempt to break a Strait of Hormuz stalemate that is strangling Iran's own economy. The U.S. has responded forcefully, reportedly sinking five Iranian oil tankers, and Iran answered by attacking ten vessels near the strait. At least one seafarer was killed, with another still missing. American military aircraft also sustained damage in overnight strikes on Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan. The situation is alarming enough that top White House advisers have privately raised with President Trump the prospect that the Iran war could drag on through the remainder of his term — a scenario the administration is clearly trying to avoid but increasingly cannot rule out.

That geopolitical backdrop could hardly be worse timed for the Republican Party, which kicked off a high-profile midterm convention in Dallas on Wednesday night. Speaking to a crowd of party faithful, President Trump capped the evening with a headline-grabbing pledge: if Republicans hold onto the House and Senate in November, he will send every adult American a $5,000 "Trump dividend." The cost would exceed $1.3 trillion. Vice President Vance suggested the payments could be financed by tariff revenue, though tariffs have generated only about $154 billion through the first ten months of fiscal 2026. The proposal drew immediate skepticism — legal experts note the optics of paying people to vote a certain way, and Trump's previous promises of dividend checks, including DOGE-linked $5,000 payments floated by Elon Musk in early 2025, were never issued. Still, the announcement dominated the convention's opening night and gave the party a galvanizing talking point for a midterm environment the New York Times described this morning simply as "a mess."

The Times devoted its lead piece to just how chaotic the upcoming midterms look. Congressional maps have been redrawn in multiple states. The Trump administration is pressing to control mail-in ballot delivery through the U.S. Postal Service, conditioning ballot distribution on whether states hand over voter rolls to a federal master list. A federal judge has already blocked that plan, warning of "major disenfranchisement," but the administration has asked the Supreme Court to allow it to take effect this cycle while litigation continues. In Missouri, competing court rulings have left voters and election officials genuinely uncertain which congressional map will be in use on Election Day. The Times's Sam Sifton, writing from London where the Bayeux Tapestry is on public display for the first time in nearly a thousand years, called the overall picture uncommonly turbulent.

In technology news, Apple on Tuesday unveiled its long-awaited first foldable iPhone, called the iPhone Duo, alongside the iPhone 18 Pro lineup. The Journal's personal-tech columnist Nicole Nguyen tested the device at Apple Park and reported her reaction went from zero to genuinely interested. The Duo uses a book-style fold, wider than tall, with Apple's software smoothing over the physical crease better than any Android rival she has tested since 2019. It ships October 23rd, with pricing starting around $2,325 for the base configuration. Supply is expected to be tight.

The AI industry is grappling with its own moment of reckoning. Jacob Coxon, a 27-year-old researcher who spent three years in pretraining work at Anthropic and OpenAI, publicly resigned this week with an open warning that the people building AI "earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade" — and are building it anyway. His post on X has been viewed more than 70 million times. In a striking response, Anthropic's own chief safety researcher, Evan Hubinger, publicly confirmed Coxon's framing, writing that he believes there is greater than a ten percent chance AI kills all humans. The Journal devoted both a 10-Point item and a front-page exclusive to the story, exploring the broader "AI doomsday debate" now breaking out of academic circles and into public consciousness. In a separate but related development, Andrew Tulloch, the star AI researcher who was offered a pay package worth more than $1 billion to join Meta last year, is departing the company.

On markets: U.S. Treasury yields climbed to fresh multiyear highs, with the ten-year note settling near 4.836 to 4.921 percent on the front page capture. Stocks fell across the board — the S&P 500 lost 0.56 percent, the Dow 0.31 percent, and the Nasdaq 0.86 percent. Gold slipped 1.56 percent to about $4,391 an ounce, and Bitcoin fell 1.66 percent to around $76,900. European natural gas prices surged to their highest level since early 2023 as Middle East fighting continues to choke LNG shipments. The Labor Department releases August producer-price data today, and Oracle and Adobe report earnings.

Elsewhere in the news: Rhode Island Governor Dan McKee was soundly defeated in his state's Democratic primary, making him the first sitting governor to lose a primary in nearly a decade. The Trump administration is proposing to overhaul the 2030 census by eliminating questions about race and ethnicity and sharply restricting how immigrants would be counted when results are used to apportion congressional seats. A legal battle has erupted over $225 million in cryptocurrency seized by the U.S. government from alleged scam operations — the money exists and may eventually be returned to victims, but figuring out who gets what has become a labyrinthine process underscoring a harsh reality: finding stolen funds is only half the battle. And Google announced it will spend more than $15 billion to develop artificial-intelligence infrastructure in Finland.

Today marks the twenty-fifth anniversary of the September 11 attacks.

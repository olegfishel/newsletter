Friday, October 23, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front-page capture and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune (yesterday's e-Edition, Thursday, October 22), and supplementary web reporting.

Good morning, it's Friday, October 23, 2026. Wall Street is waking up to the aftermath of its worst session since June, as worries about the cost of artificial intelligence rippled through the market. The Nasdaq fell 2.6 percent on Thursday, the S&P 500 lost 1.2 percent, and the Dow was little changed. The Wall Street Journal traces the spark to Meta, which reported solid third-quarter revenue but also lifted its spending outlook to somewhere between 135 and 145 billion dollars for the year and floated a plan to sell non-voting stock. Meta's hunt for AI capital, the Journal says, struck a raw nerve, and investors rotated out of AI-linked names. Bonds rallied hard in the meantime, with Treasury yields posting their biggest one-day drop since 2023, though the ten-year is still hovering right around five percent. Futures point lower again this morning, with Dow futures off about half a percent. Crude oil is above 100 dollars a barrel, gold is hovering near 4,000, and bitcoin is around 81,000.

The AI theme runs through the day's other big technology story. The Journal reports that OpenAI fired three safety researchers last month after suspecting them of leaking the internal designs of its models. The three were summoned to meetings with the company's security team, asked to unlock their personal phones, and let go a week later. OpenAI says an internal investigation found they violated company policy, while the researchers dispute that account. The Journal sees a growing tension in Silicon Valley over who should be allowed to look inside powerful AI systems, and how to keep the technology under human control. Elsewhere in the markets, Moderna lost nearly eighteen percent of its value after details of its cancer-vaccine data surfaced early on social media, ahead of a European medical conference this weekend, and Barclays said it will cut 5,000 jobs, about five percent of its workforce, as it adopts AI at scale.

Abroad, the Middle East remains the most dangerous flashpoint. The Journal reports that Iran is warning that if the United States and Israel resume airstrikes, Tehran will retaliate against airports, pipelines and other civilian targets across the Persian Gulf. Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia are continuing, and The New York Times says Washington has told some government employees and their families to leave the kingdom. Those strikes on Saudi airports have also upended one of the last safe air corridors between Europe and Asia, leaving airlines to make their own choices about where to fly. This morning, Israeli forces are also responding to a shooting attack near Hebron in the West Bank that wounded five people, two of them critically, and Prime Minister Netanyahu has said Hamas was behind it. That comes as Israel heads toward elections, with former military chief Gadi Eisenkot now challenging Netanyahu.

On Ukraine, the Journal reports that President Trump phoned Elon Musk on Wednesday and asked him to lift restrictions on the military-grade version of Starlink that Ukraine relies on. Trump has since suggested Musk put things back, but Ukrainian officials told reporters that the service is still throttled and that, in their words, nothing has changed. In Europe, France is dealing with its worst unrest in years, driven by economic distress, and authorities have banned gatherings in some cities this weekend. A magnitude 7.0 earthquake in Tibet has killed at least sixteen people, and the United Nations now projects the world is heading for about 2.6 degrees Celsius of warming by the end of the century.

In Washington, the Justice Department is examining allegations that Corey Lewandowski profited from his position advising Kristi Noem when she led the Department of Homeland Security, one of the first such inquiries in Trump's second term to reach someone close to the president. Calley Means, a senior adviser to Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., is leaving the administration, and a federal indictment has charged Virginia Democratic state senator Louise Lucas with marijuana trafficking. The midterms are turning nasty. Trump defended an attack ad against Ohio Democrat Amy Acton that features her estranged mother, calling it very powerful, even as a number of Republicans urge Vivek Ramaswamy to pull it. In Texas, the Journal says Ken Paxton is trailing Democrat James Talarico in several polls despite heavy financial backing. Trump travels to Florida for a rally today.

The Times leads its newsletter with a story that touches a debate playing out in San Diego too. The federal government has installed hundreds of AI-enabled camera towers along the southern border, able to see for miles and track people and vehicles around the clock, and at least a million people in the United States and Mexico live within their view. The administration wants them in Florida, Puerto Rico and along the northern border as well. Closer to home, the Union-Tribune reported that San Diego is moving closer to switching off its Flock license-plate cameras.

In business, the Pentagon signed a deal to buy Lockheed Martin's F-35 fighters over the next decade, its longest commitment yet this year. Comcast said it will take a charge of more than a billion dollars as it restructures amid deeper subscriber losses, and Blackstone's net realizations fell 34 percent. On the investment side, U.S. Steel plans nearly three billion dollars for a new mill near Pittsburgh, and Carrier will put more than 600 million into a Fort Worth plant. The Journal also notes that China is turning Xinjiang into a commercial hub, with tourism worth about 50 billion dollars last year, even after United Nations officials said abuses there may amount to crimes against humanity.

A few things to watch today: the World Series begins, American Express reports earnings, and the University of Michigan releases its final October consumer-sentiment reading. And in San Diego sports, the Chargers are eager for a reboot after an 0-6 start, with the Aztecs on the road at Colorado State tomorrow.

WSJ What's News

Business & Finance

Rotation out of AI-connected stocks helped drive the Nasdaq down 2.6 percent in its worst session since June, while the S&P 500 fell 1.2 percent and the Dow was little changed.

Blackstone's net realizations, which reflect proceeds from asset sales, fell 34 percent from a year earlier, led by a 49 percent decline in its private-equity division.

The Pentagon signed a deal to acquire Lockheed Martin's F-35 fighter jets over the next decade, its longest commitment yet among contracts this year to boost supplies of advanced weaponry.

Comcast's subscriber losses deepened, and the company said it will take a charge of more than one billion dollars in the fourth quarter as it embarks on a restructuring program.

Carrier intends to invest more than 600 million dollars in a new plant in Fort Worth, Texas, to supply more cooling equipment to large commercial and industrial buildings.

U.S. Steel, which is owned by Tokyo-based Nippon Steel, plans to invest nearly three billion dollars in a new hot strip mill at its plant near Pittsburgh.

Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern reported higher profit and revenue in the recent quarter as more businesses move cargo from road to rail.

SAP is working on a new metric to quantify the contribution of artificial intelligence to its business.

Barclays said it would cut 5,000 jobs, or 5 percent of its workforce, to reduce costs as it adopts artificial intelligence at scale.

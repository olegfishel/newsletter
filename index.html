Morning Briefing

Friday, August 7, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Friday, August 7, 2026. The dominant story as the week closes is a striking new U.S. intelligence warning: Vladimir Putin could attempt to test NATO's resolve with a limited military assault on an allied country sometime in the next few years — a significant shift from previous assessments that held Russia would avoid provoking the alliance while still fighting in Ukraine. The scenarios under consideration range from a cyberattack on a NATO member to sending unmarked armed groups to seize territory, all the way to a limited ground incursion on the alliance's eastern flank. U.S. analysts stress the probability of an overt invasion remains low, but is increasing. The shift comes as American munitions stockpiles remain a domestic political flashpoint: Trump this week ordered new investigations into the disclosure of Pentagon stockpile numbers, escalating his campaign against those responsible for what the administration regards as damaging leaks about the war in Iran.

On the diplomatic front in the Middle East, a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz remains tantalizingly close — and dangerously fragile. Iranian officials confirmed the agreement with Oman is in "the final stage of drafting," with both sides having settled on the geographic coordinates of safe shipping corridors. But Arab mediators working the back channel are raising a pointed concern: Tehran's diplomats, squeezed by domestic hard-liners, may simply lack the political clout to guarantee compliance even if signatures are obtained. The Wall Street Journal reports that the hard-liners' growing influence raises the real risk they will torpedo any deal before the ink dries. Global oil markets are watching closely, given that roughly a fifth of the world's petroleum transits the strait each day.

In Washington, President Trump signed a new pair of executive orders targeting birthright citizenship, renewing the administration's effort to restrict automatic citizenship for children born on U.S. soil to non-citizen parents. The White House says the orders are consistent with the Supreme Court's recent ruling, which had struck down an earlier, broader attempt. Critics disagree and expect fresh legal challenges. Separately, the administration announced it is eliminating hundreds of federal Head Start regulations, drawing alarms from child-welfare advocates who fear the move is a precursor to gutting the program. The White House is also reportedly drafting an executive order on vaccines and autism — a politically sensitive step that even some administration allies acknowledge carries significant risks.

On Capitol Hill, a Republican-led Senate committee voted along party lines to hold Dr. Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress for his refusal to answer questions about his handling of the Covid-19 pandemic. The vote, chaired by Senator Rand Paul, is the latest chapter in the long-running effort to hold the former public health director accountable for pandemic-era decisions. Meanwhile, Senator Mitch McConnell has been discharged from the rehabilitation facility where he was recovering from a fall, and plans to continue recuperating at home.

Markets closed a mixed Thursday with SpaceX a bright exception. While the S&P 500 fell 0.2 percent, the Dow slipped 0.9 percent, and the Nasdaq dipped 0.1 percent, SpaceX shares jumped — in contrast to the broader selloff. This morning, futures point modestly higher. All eyes are on the July jobs report, due out this morning from the Labor Department; analysts will be looking for signs of whether the labor market is cooling in line with the Federal Reserve's hopes. In a major media deal, Britain's competition watchdog cleared Paramount's massive $81 billion merger with Warner Bros. Discovery, after Paramount offered commitments protecting editorial independence at the combined company's TV and streaming services. In aviation, private equity giant Apollo agreed to acquire British budget carrier easyJet for roughly $7.7 billion, winning a contested bidding war. And SoftBank deepened its already enormous AI bet, using its OpenAI equity stake as collateral for a new $10 billion loan from a consortium of banks including Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan — a leveraged wager that signals just how committed the Japanese conglomerate is to the artificial intelligence boom.

The AI story is reshaping industries well beyond finance. Silicon Valley is nervously eyeing what insiders are calling the "SaaSpocalypse" — generative AI is steamrollering the software-as-a-service sector, threatening to make subscription tools obsolete as AI handles tasks from legal drafting to customer service. Once high-flying companies like Slack and Zoom are scrambling to reinvent themselves before the wave overtakes them. The Federal Aviation Administration added to Boeing's troubles by ordering inspections of hundreds of 737 MAX jets over possible structural cracks, the latest safety concern for the manufacturer. And a New Mexico jury handed Meta a $942 million verdict, finding the company failed to protect young people from harm on its platforms.

The New York Times this morning reports a striking statistic from the Pentagon: under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, the share of senior officer promotions going to women has fallen to its lowest level in at least 25 years. Hegseth has not offered specific explanations for the blocked promotions but has argued that past diversity efforts degraded military readiness. Overseas, a school shooting in Thailand killed at least seven people before the gunman turned the weapon on himself. In Europe, a drone carrying explosives was found at a German airport near planes operated by a Ukrainian carrier, and authorities believe a second drone may have actually struck an aircraft in the air. Germany's intelligence services warn that Russian sabotage operations on Western infrastructure are accelerating. The Rhine River recorded its lowest water level since 1880, disrupting commercial shipping across the heart of the continent. Wildfires continue to rage in eastern Washington state near Spokane, with a suspect arrested who detectives say confessed to setting two dozen fires.

China's economic story also deserves attention this morning. The Wall Street Journal examines how Beijing is no longer simply the world's low-cost consumer-goods factory — it has become a dominant exporter of high-value capital equipment and industrial components that underpin manufacturing around the globe. This evolution makes China's export machine significantly more resilient to U.S. tariffs than it was even a few years ago, because customers in developing economies increasingly have few alternative sources for the specialized machinery they need. Finally, on the lighter side: a hot dog at a Miami food venue is selling for one hundred dollars, topped with Ossetia caviar and 24-karat edible gold flakes. It sold out at both the Miami Open and the Formula One Grand Prix. America's appetite for luxury, apparently, knows no limits.

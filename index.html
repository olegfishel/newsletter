Morning Briefing

Monday, October 26, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, the New York Times "The Morning," and the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition (yesterday's Sunday edition, the latest available), supplemented with current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, October 26, 2026. The week opens under the shadow of the standoff with Iran. American intelligence assessments suggest that Iranian preparations to attack Americans in the Middle East are well advanced, and the Journal reports a sudden exodus of U.S. personnel from across the region. The State Department is also thinning staff at its embassies in Jordan, Lebanon, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, amid worry that Yemen's Houthi militants may escalate their attacks. President Trump says he is weighing whether to make a deal with Tehran or "finish them off," but he was careful to add that there will not be a strike today. Iran, for its part, says messages are still passing between the two sides and that it remains open to diplomacy, though negotiations have appeared stalled for weeks.

That tension is showing up in energy markets. Brent crude closed last week near 117 dollars a barrel after a gain of nearly seven percent, and while oil is slipping this morning, with American crude back around 101 dollars, traders are warning that any real disruption around the Strait of Hormuz could push prices toward 120. The average gallon of gasoline in the United States stood at about 4 dollars and 28 cents yesterday. Stock futures, meanwhile, are pointing higher, with Dow futures up roughly four-tenths of a percent and Nasdaq futures up about six-tenths. The ten-year Treasury yield is sitting right around five percent, and the Federal Reserve begins its policy meeting this week. The Journal's 10-Point notes that firmer inflation readings have revived talk of a rate increase, but that officials' calls for patience, and the approaching midterm elections, make a surprise hike hard to pull off. Markets widely expect the Fed to hold steady.

The Journal's front page raises a different kind of warning about the stock market itself. The current corporate earnings boom turns out to be remarkably narrow. Just ten companies, including Nvidia, Micron and other artificial-intelligence giants, account for roughly two-thirds of corporate profit growth, and two AI chipmakers alone make up about a third of it. That concentration magnifies the risk to portfolios if the headlong growth ever falters. Elsewhere in business, shares of Kering plunged nearly seventeen percent in Paris on Friday after Gucci's sales slide deepened doubts about a turnaround, and the Journal reports that shoppers are increasingly convinced retailers are using AI to quietly charge them higher prices, a suspicion that Walmart denies in one recent case. On the brighter side, American manufacturers have added 72,000 jobs since December, although more of those jobs now demand a college degree.

Turning to politics abroad, Brazil's Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has won an upset re-election victory over Flávio Bolsonaro, giving the 80-year-old leftist an unprecedented fourth term. The Times reports that Bolsonaro has so far refused to accept the result. In Venezuela, opposition leader María Corina Machado returned home after nearly eleven months in exile, adding fresh pressure on the U.S.-backed interim government. And in Serbia, longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat in parliamentary elections to a slate of candidates backed by protesting students.

All eyes in the Middle East turn tomorrow to Israel, which votes in what the Times calls the most consequential election in its history, the first since the October 7th attacks and the war that followed. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is seeking a seventh term against former military chief Gadi Eisenkot. Most polls show Eisenkot's camp slightly ahead, but neither side has a clear path to the 61 seats needed for a majority, so coalition haggling could stretch on for weeks. Some 67,000 expatriate Israelis are expected to fly home to vote.

At home, the midterm campaign is heating up. Journal reporters followed President Trump through seven rallies in seven days, finding enthusiastic crowds that are generally smaller than when he was on the ballot himself, while former President Obama has stormed onto the trail with sharp words for Trump. The Journal also reports that the alliance between the MAHA movement and Trump's base is fracturing, as supporters of Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. grow disillusioned. Attorney General Blanche offered a combative defense of the Justice Department's lawsuit seeking to block certification of Alaska's Senate race if the Republican candidate loses. In California, a proposed one-time tax on billionaires to offset Medicaid cuts has split Democrats and drawn hundreds of millions of dollars in opposition from tech elites.

In Europe, France's Marine Le Pen kicked off her 2027 presidential campaign by casting herself as a unifying figure after weeks of unrest, even as Paris works with European allies to expand its role as a nuclear power amid doubts about American security guarantees. North Korea's Kim Jong Un made a surprise trip to China, crossing a new bridge at the border. And in corporate intrigue, the FBI is weighing an economic espionage charge against a man accused of planting an eavesdropping device in an OpenAI conference room, while American executives have quit the energy startup Polar LNG after learning it was secretly funded by a Russian gas giant.

Closer to home, San Diego's weekend headlines included a ballot measure, Proposition 45, aimed at speeding up state environmental reviews, polls showing Von Wilpert favored in a House race, and a fresh debate over the city's use of Flock license-plate cameras. Today also brings the Ballon d'Or ceremony in London, Verizon's earnings, and a lighter note from Boston, where Logan Airport is searching for a new voice, and heavy accent is required.

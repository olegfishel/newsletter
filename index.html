Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 6, 2026.

The most urgent story coming into this morning concerns a security scare that sent twelve American bombers home from England over the weekend. U.S. Air Force commanders quietly evacuated all twelve B-1B Lancer aircraft from RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire after intelligence pointed to a sophisticated drone strike ordered by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps against the base. Vice President JD Vance said the pullout was made "out of an abundance of caution," and the bombers have since returned to their home stations in the United States. British police had already arrested six suspects linked to surveillance of the airfield, including a British-Iranian dual national detained in London on October first. Iran denied any involvement.

The episode is the latest tremor from the broader U.S.-Israel war with Iran, now in its eighth month. Tehran's ongoing restrictions on Strait of Hormuz shipping have elevated oil prices and fed an inflationary surge that is pushing U.S. borrowing costs to levels not seen in a generation. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note briefly touched 5.34 percent last week — its highest since April 2002 — before settling to 5.27 percent on Monday. Despite that pressure, equities held up well: the Nasdaq clinched a record close, rising one percent, while the S&P 500 gained 0.7 percent and the Dow edged up 0.2. Saudi Aramco's chief executive Amin Nasser warned investors that the Middle East conflict has sharply depleted global oil inventories and that rebuilding them could take as long as two years.

High rates are now fracturing deals in commercial real estate. A growing number of buyers who signed transactions earlier this year are threatening to walk away unless sellers offer meaningfully better terms, a signal of broader distress in a property market that has yet to find its footing. Buyers cite financing costs that have made transactions unworkable at the prices agreed months ago, and sellers face a choice between renegotiating and starting over in a market with very few takers.

In Europe, France and Germany are mounting a coordinated push to harden the continent's trade defenses against China. President Macron and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz sent European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen a joint letter proposing a new rapid-response trade instrument powerful enough to authorize an "immediate cut-off from the internal market" for goods from countries engaging in systematic market distortion. The mechanism is designed to work through a reversed qualified-majority procedure, meaning Commission action could proceed unless enough member states assembled a coalition large enough to block it. It is a striking reversal for Berlin, which had previously tried to slow EU tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles. Beijing's commerce ministry dismissed the proposal as protectionism and warned it would backfire. The two leaders plan to press the case at next week's EU leaders' summit in Brussels.

The Nobel Assembly in Stockholm delivered welcome news for science on Monday, awarding the 2026 Prize in Physiology or Medicine to three neuroscientists: Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University in Berlin, Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg, and Karl Deisseroth of Stanford University. The three are recognized for developing optogenetics, a technique that uses light-sensitive proteins — channelrhodopsins, originally isolated from green algae — to switch individual nerve cells on and off with millisecond precision. Hegemann and Nagel discovered and characterized the proteins; Deisseroth and his team engineered them into mammalian brain cells, transforming neuroscience from a discipline that could describe the brain into one that can directly test the causal roles of specific circuits in memory, fear, depression, and movement disorders like Parkinson's. The trio shares a prize of twelve million Swedish kronor.

On the deal front, KKR has agreed to acquire Gen II, a fund administrator focused on buyout and private-capital funds, in a transaction valuing the business at roughly five billion dollars including debt. GE HealthCare Technologies announced it will pay $945 million in cash for a manufacturer of radioactive substances used in medical imaging. At Mattel, a major shareholder is calling on the toy company to explore strategic alternatives as it manages a sliding stock price and a leadership transition. Paramount Skydance added detail to its governance structure, confirming posts for David Ellison, Bari Weiss, and Mark Thompson.

At the Supreme Court, oil companies faced pointed questions from justices about their decades-long campaign to extinguish climate-change lawsuits brought by state and local governments. And in news of a passing, former House Speaker Dennis Hastert died at the age of eighty-four.

Here in San Diego, President Trump drew condemnation from California Democrats after suggesting at a Nebraska campaign rally that Iran could "take out" Los Angeles or San Diego. Governor Gavin Newsom called the remark "deranged and dangerous," and Mayor Todd Gloria said the city was "not collateral damage in the president's war." On the field, the Padres find themselves in early trouble in the National League Division Series, down two games to none after dropping Monday's Game Two to the Milwaukee Brewers four to three. A two-run single by Chourio off reliever Miller in the ninth inning sealed it for Milwaukee. San Diego returns home for Games Three and Four, counting on Petco Park to turn the series. And residents across the county are being urged to take precautions as an Extreme Heat Warning continues through Thursday, with cooling centers running extended hours and thousands dealing with power outages.

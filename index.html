Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 19, 2026.

The dominant story gripping Wall Street and global finance this morning is a bond market rout that investors warn may only be getting started. The yield on the 30-year U.S. Treasury rose to 5.34 percent yesterday — its highest level since 2007 — and the 10-year yield followed at 4.75 percent, the loftiest mark in nineteen months. The selloff is not uniquely American: Japanese government bonds climbed to levels last seen in 1996, British gilt yields crossed five percent for the longest stretch in nearly two decades, and German Bund yields hit their highest since 2011. The Wall Street Journal frames it starkly: investors are bracing for a new era in bonds, and some see echoes of 2007. What is driving the rout? A complicated mix of persistent inflation, the unresolved U.S.-Iran standoff that has pushed crude oil back above $85 a barrel, and an unlikely culprit — artificial intelligence. Big Tech's hyperscalers, including Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft, have flooded the bond market with roughly $200 billion in new corporate debt so far this year, more than double last year's full-year total, directly competing with governments for investor dollars. The consequences are rippling outward. Stocks wobbled on Tuesday, with the S&P 500 falling 0.7 percent, the Nasdaq sliding 1.3 percent, and the Dow dipping 0.2 percent. For ordinary families, the selloff translates into higher mortgage rates, steeper auto loans, and costlier credit card balances. Gold hit $4,500 an ounce as investors sought shelter.

Underlying all of it is the unresolved conflict with Iran. Iranian forces have been attacking commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz with growing frequency since July, when a June memorandum of understanding collapsed. Strikes on tankers and bulk carriers have mounted without a U.S. military response, frustrating some Arab allies who fear Washington has no strategy to end the blockade. The administration has been leaning on economic pressure rather than military retaliation — but with roughly a fifth of the world's oil moving through the strait, every new attack rattles commodity markets and raises questions about how long restraint can hold. Trump reportedly rejected extending an earlier ceasefire on Monday. Meanwhile, his aides say he is pressing for a personal meeting with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un as early as this fall, seeking to revive denuclearization diplomacy even as the Middle East simmers.

On the domestic political front, Florida held its primary elections last night, producing results that illustrate the ideological polarization now defining American politics. On the Republican side, Congressman Byron Donalds — President Trump's closest ally in the race — cruised to the GOP gubernatorial nomination and could become Florida's first Black governor if he wins in November. The general election matchup is striking: Donalds will face David Jolly, a former Republican congressman who has crossed the aisle and emerged as one of Trump's most persistent critics. The Senate race produced the bigger upset: state Representative Angie Nixon, a democratic socialist who raised less than a million dollars, defeated Alexander Vindman — who outspent her sixteen to one — to win the Democratic Senate primary. Scandal-plagued Representative Cory Mills, who faced multiple allegations of threatening behavior toward women, lost his primary outright.

Hurricane Lala's aftermath continues to command attention this morning. After the storm tore past the Big Island of Hawaii last weekend, dropping three feet of rain, communities on the southern tip of the island are improvising recovery in quietly moving ways. In Ocean View, a local radio station operator linked his equipment to a satellite and broadcast emergency information while neighbors showed up with fuel cans, clipboards, and ready-to-eat food. Volunteers filled a helicopter with supplies for a town 25 miles away. In Naalehu, twelve miles east, the storm's flooding pulled coffins and gravestones from a small cemetery, leaving families searching a mud-strewn debris field for the graves of their loved ones — a chilling scene that the New York Times described in harrowing detail.

In the media world, ABC's parent Disney has sued the Federal Communications Commission, alleging the agency's efforts to challenge its broadcast licenses and regulate programming content are unconstitutional and an assault on the First Amendment. The lawsuit follows a pattern of regulatory targeting of news organizations that have run afoul of the administration. The Journal's 10-Point notes that FCC Chairman Brendan Carr is seeking to punish the network for its editorial independence. Separately, opening statements began Tuesday in a landmark child-safety lawsuit against Meta Platforms — what legal observers are calling potentially the most consequential social media harm case ever to reach a courtroom.

A few more stories rounding out the morning: KKR has bid roughly nine billion dollars for UGI, the Pennsylvania-based natural gas and electricity distributor. OpenAI's second-quarter revenue growth disappointed investors, coming in tepidly behind Anthropic's trajectory, raising new questions about the competitive race in enterprise AI. Klarna has trimmed its outlook, citing softness in consumer spending. And Costco is quietly preparing a limited rollout of its own branded Medicare plans in partnership with the nonprofit insurer SCAN Group — a move that could shake up the crowded Medicare Advantage market. Trump has also selected Dr. Heidi Overton, a top White House health aide, as his next choice to lead the Food and Drug Administration. And the U.S. Forest Service is moving to open more than 44 million acres of national forest land to logging and road construction, reversing a decades-old policy that protected wildlife habitats.

Finally, two dispatches from the economic fringes: WSJ reporters are inside the tiny Spanish territory of Ceuta on the Moroccan border, where three weeks ago more than 72,000 migrants arrived in a sudden surge, overwhelming authorities. Most departed quickly, but thousands who stayed are living in an abandoned warehouse, sleeping on broken cars and torn sofas, creating a humanitarian challenge with no easy resolution. And in a lighter vein: LinkedIn, despite its strict no-romance rules, is increasingly functioning as a dating site — users tired of swipe-based apps turning to clear professional headshots and career histories as proxies for character and stability.

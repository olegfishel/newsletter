Good morning, it's Friday, October 9, 2026. The most consequential morning in months is arriving with oil markets in turmoil, a Category 3 hurricane bearing down on the Gulf Coast, a Nobel Peace Prize that has reignited debate over the Gaza war, and a federal deficit that just broke through two trillion dollars—all before the opening bell on Wall Street.

The dominant story is the Persian Gulf. Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps struck a liquefied petroleum gas carrier south of the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday and issued a stark new warning: unauthorized vessels could now be targeted anywhere in the broader region, not just in the strait itself. In a separate coordinated blow, Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthi movement launched missiles and drones at Riyadh's airport. The one-two punch has shattered what little sense of normalcy remained in the Saudi capital and sent oil prices surging. Brent crude rose more than four percent on Thursday alone, reaching $91 a barrel. More alarming is what has happened to actual throughput: flow of oil out of the Persian Gulf has collapsed from roughly 15 million barrels per day in mid-September to just four million this week. America's largest refiners are paradoxically set for gangbuster third-quarter earnings—they profit from elevated spreads even as the crisis threatens the broader economy. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called on Iran's proxies to halt what she described as "reckless and violent acts." The Pentagon confirmed it is preparing options to restart major combat operations against Iran, though President Trump—with one eye firmly on midterm election polls—appears ambivalent about full escalation. The man tasked with rebuilding the depleted weapons stocks that prompted Trump's concern is Steve Feinberg, the private-equity billionaire serving as the Pentagon's number-two official. In his first interview on the job, Feinberg defended his unconventional approach, tracing its origins to a Camp David cabinet meeting this summer when Trump turned to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and said simply: "Get Feinberg on the phone." The Wall Street Journal went on a rare military embed inside Gaza this week and found Israel is steadily fortifying bases along a dividing line with Hamas—expanding its military footprint while maintaining tight restrictions on outside journalists.

On the economy, the Congressional Budget Office delivered unwelcome news on Thursday: the federal deficit for fiscal year 2026 rose twelve percent to nearly two trillion dollars, its highest level since the pandemic year of 2021. Government spending grew six percent while revenues rose only three percent. Net interest payments on the national debt now top $1.14 trillion annually, and outlays for defense, Medicare, Medicaid, and Social Security all climbed. Corporate income tax receipts, meanwhile, fell sixteen percent as companies took advantage of accelerated deductions under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The U.S. collected $5.4 trillion in total revenues last year—but still ran a shortfall larger than at any point outside of war or recession. Fiscal watchdogs are alarmed; Washington has yet to show signs of changing course.

This morning in Oslo, the Norwegian Nobel Committee announced that South African jurist Navanethem "Navi" Pillay has won the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize "for her efforts to promote peace and international law." Pillay began her career defending anti-apartheid activists at a time when representing Nelson Mandela's colleagues was itself a political act. She went on to serve as a judge at the International Criminal Court and later as United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights. Most recently she led a U.N. commission whose report concluded that Israel had committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza—a finding Israel has forcefully contested. The committee's chair said the prize was "a message to those who are attacking" international law. Israel immediately condemned the award; the committee denied the honor was an endorsement of the genocide determination. Separately, Canadian poet Anne Carson was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature this week.

Hurricane Isaias is making this a dangerous Friday for millions of Gulf Coast residents. The storm has strengthened to a Category 3 hurricane with sustained winds of 120 miles per hour and is expected to make landfall tonight along the northern Gulf Coast from Alabama to the Florida Panhandle. Life-threatening storm surge of five to seven feet is forecast for parts of Alabama and Mississippi, and some areas could receive fifteen inches of rain. Tornadoes are possible overnight from Alabama into southwestern Georgia. The governors of Florida and Alabama have declared states of emergency. Residents from Mississippi to the Florida Panhandle were stocking up on supplies Thursday as mandatory evacuations took effect in the most vulnerable coastal zones. The New York Times' Sam Sifton notes this is the first hurricane of what has been a late-starting 2026 Atlantic season.

The USS Abraham Lincoln returned to its home port in San Diego on Thursday to a warm homecoming—greeted by crowds of families after a grueling 322-day deployment in which the crew endured food shortages and significant mental health challenges while operating in the contested waters around Iran. San Diego Union-Tribune readers will see extensive coverage of the carrier's return in today's edition.

In Washington, the Wall Street Journal has a damaging exclusive on Kimberly Guilfoyle: text messages with a MAGA ally show her pressing him to wire $100,000 to her American Express account and offering access to senior administration officials—all before her Senate confirmation as ambassador to Greece. Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and his wife have been charged in the United States with conspiring to commit torture, which U.S. prosecutors say was part of a scheme to maintain power. On the H-1B front, Vice President JD Vance said the administration is suspending several tech firms from the visa program that allows companies to hire skilled foreign workers and apply for green cards, with Microsoft specifically named. A federal judge has temporarily extended a pause on the administration's effort to bar CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from the White House press briefings. And in a disturbing incident in New York City, an ICE officer shot and wounded a man during an attempted arrest; Mayor Zohran Mamdani said the man was in a car with a five-year-old child when officers opened fire.

The New York Times leads its morning newsletter with a sweeping five-year investigation by the Outlaw Ocean Project into the global fish-meal industry. The findings are unsettling: the fish farming systems meant to ease pressure on wild seafood stocks are actually accelerating overfishing, while being powered by labor abuse, rampant pollution, and frequently illegal activity on more than 10,000 vessels worldwide. Norwegian salmon may each consume five times their own body weight in ground-up wild fish before reaching market size. More than 16 million people live within a mile of a fish-meal factory whose air quality regularly includes a carcinogenic chemical. Aquaculture was supposed to be the solution. It has become part of the problem. Meanwhile, the Journal's technology team explores how Walmart spent billions trying to deploy robots in its warehouses—and why reality keeps getting in the way. And in the "And Finally" slot, a startup CEO discovered his personal AI agent had posted his bank-account balances into a company-wide Slack channel, prompting the Journal to ask how much trust we should really be placing in these always-on digital helpers.

In market numbers this morning: the Dow industrials closed Thursday at 51,427, and S&P 500 futures are pointing higher in early trading at 7,848. Gold continues its remarkable run at $4,213 an ounce, a reflection of geopolitical anxiety. Bitcoin is at $82,799. The ten-year Treasury yield is 5.27 percent.

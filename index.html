Morning Briefing

Thursday, October 22, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, The New York Times Morning newsletter, the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and additional wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 22, 2026.

We begin in the Middle East, where fears of a return to full-scale war with Iran are rising by the hour. Iran's Revolutionary Guard says seven oil tankers were set ablaze or damaged overnight in the Strait of Hormuz, either struck by Iranian fire or hit by mines. If that holds up, it would be the largest number of vessels damaged in the Gulf in a single day since the war began on February twenty-eighth. Brent crude jumped more than three percent to about one hundred twelve dollars a barrel. Separately, Saudi authorities blame Iran-backed Houthi militants for a drone attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. It killed at least one person, with later reports putting the toll at two, and injured eight. The Houthis also claimed strikes on a defense ministry site in Riyadh and on Aramco facilities, and the American embassy in Riyadh had warned of exactly this kind of attack.

Mediators, including Pakistan and Qatar, are scrambling to revive talks between Washington and Tehran. Iran is pushing a proposal to end the war and fully reopen the strait within a week, if the United States lifts its blockade of Iranian ports and meets other conditions, much like a June deal that collapsed within days. The Journal reports a new squeeze on Tehran: Iran's fleet of shadow tankers, which quietly held millions of barrels of crude in Asian waters for Chinese refineries, has run out of oil. Since oil sales account for as much as forty percent of government revenue, that cushion matters. Meanwhile the U.S. is sending another aircraft carrier to the region, Israel is warning of possible new strikes, and the Times reports that American and Israeli planners have drawn up options to renew strikes on Iran after the midterm elections.

On Wall Street, stocks climbed on Wednesday on the strength of big names in artificial intelligence, with the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow gaining roughly six tenths of a percent, one point one percent, and seven tenths of a percent. This morning, though, futures are pointing lower, with the Nasdaq down more than one and a half percent, after earnings from Tesla and Meta. Tesla beat expectations and posted a surprise positive free cash flow, but investors balked at flat revenue and heavy spending on artificial intelligence and robotics. The bigger story was Elon Musk's openness to merging Tesla with SpaceX, which would be the largest corporate combination in history. The two companies have formed a special committee to study it, no terms have been agreed, and Bloomberg reports a target announcement in the first half of next year. SpaceX itself reported third-quarter revenue of fourteen point six billion dollars, up one hundred sixty-one percent. Musk also said Tesla could begin shipping its Optimus humanoid robots by the end of next year. Not all the Musk news is flattering: the Journal reported that he ordered a slowdown of Starlink access that blindsided Ukraine, and lawmakers from both parties are now calling for an investigation.

In technology, security researchers used artificial intelligence tools, including advanced OpenAI models, to uncover a severe flaw in PHP, software that powers as much as seventy percent of the world's websites. Experts are racing to patch it before hackers can use AI to exploit it, and some European governments have blocked access to certain government sites as a precaution. Meta, for its part, was ordered by a New Mexico judge to pay twenty-five billion dollars for misleading users about its data privacy practices, one of the largest penalties ever imposed on a tech company. The Journal adds that a planned two-for-one split of Meta's stock is structured to keep Mark Zuckerberg in California despite a proposed billionaire tax.

On the political front, the midterms are closing in. President Trump's appearance in South Carolina last night was interrupted by scuffles between rallygoers and protesters, and he heads to Iowa for a rally today. A new Times and Siena poll finds young voters overwhelmingly backing Democrats. In Michigan's Senate race, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers had a heated debate, as the Times examined attacks tying El-Sayed's family to Islamist groups. AIPAC announced it is ending candidate endorsements and PAC contributions as more candidates distance themselves from it. Mr. Trump also urged data center builders to generate their own electricity and pay communities a dividend, to ease local opposition.

Abroad, the Times says Vladimir Putin appears to be edging toward restored relations with Washington without any concessions on Ukraine, and a U.S. envoy praised Putin as humanitarian for freeing an American prisoner. The Journal also reports on a failed airfield attack in eastern Germany this summer, which it describes as the opening shot of a stepped-up Russian sabotage campaign. In the West Bank, New York Democrat Brad Lander says Israeli forces briefly detained him while he helped Palestinian farmers with a harvest.

In business news, AT&T raised its full-year profit outlook but disappointed on wireless subscribers. Abbott will spin off its embattled nutrition business by the end of next year. Carrefour cut its profit targets as protests in France weigh on sales, and the Journal notes France's budget troubles are complicated by its reliance on foreign investors. Corporate America is also passing along more health costs, with the employee share of a family plan up nine percent to seventy-four hundred seventy-one dollars. Today brings earnings from Procter & Gamble, American Airlines, Lockheed Martin, Spotify and Comcast, along with weekly jobless claims.

Finally, some lighter notes. Doctors say Pope Leo needs no further treatment for lung cancer. A U.N. report finds the world warming faster than projected. In San Diego, the city is moving closer to turning off its Flock cameras, and the Chargers hope to reboot after an oh-and-six start. And a forty-five million dollar Nicolas Cage movie has vanished after a hard drive was stolen from a Netflix office.

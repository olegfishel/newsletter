Wednesday, September 23, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters, AP, Bloomberg and Axios reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 23, 2026.

The United Nations General Assembly is dominating the day's news, and at its center is President Trump, who delivered one of his most consequential addresses to world leaders yet. Standing before the General Assembly on Tuesday, Trump framed himself as a man of peace while leaving no ambiguity about the alternative: the United States faces a choice with Iran — forge a deal that allows the Islamic Republic to rebuild, or, in his words, "annihilate" it "and do it quickly." Trump said he believes diplomacy will come after November's midterm elections, telling delegates that "it doesn't even make sense for them not to" make a deal. Separately, U.S. envoys held three hours of talks with Iranian officials on the sidelines of the gathering. The war is exacting an enormous toll: by the Pentagon's own accounting it has cost American taxpayers over $43 billion so far, with the Congressional Budget Office estimating at least two to three billion dollars more for every additional month of conflict. Iranian flights are being halted in several countries as part of a parallel U.S. pressure campaign over the Strait of Hormuz, further squeezing Tehran.

On the diplomatic front, a second seismic meeting unfolded today as Chinese President Xi Jinping arrived in Washington for his first state visit to the United States since 2015. Trump greeted Xi at Joint Base Andrews — a full state reception — beginning what is set to be a three-day summit capped by a White House state dinner on Thursday. The two leaders meet under the weight of long-running tensions over trade, artificial intelligence, technology, and rare earth minerals. Taiwan looms in the background. A WSJ front-page report this morning revealed that Beijing has been quietly courting Taiwan's political opposition and helped stage a landmark visit by its leader to the mainland, all in an effort to convince Taipei that resistance to Chinese unification is ultimately futile. The Xi visit was arranged following Trump's invitation during his May 2026 trip to China.

The Ukraine war, meanwhile, showed cautious signs of potential movement. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, who met with Trump on the U.N. sidelines Tuesday, told the WSJ's Emma Tucker in an exclusive interview that Vladimir Putin is unlikely to curb attacks on Ukraine without direct and sustained American pressure on Moscow. Zelensky said both Kyiv and Washington want the war to end "before winter" and that he asked Trump for a "winter package" of new military equipment. He said he is open to an energy ceasefire — halting Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure if Moscow agrees to do the same — but insisted that any territorial compromise would require strong, legally binding security guarantees from the United States, passing through Congress, not merely verbal assurances. A new round of diplomatic talks is expected next week.

Back home, domestic politics are increasingly shadowed by the war's costs. The New York Times this morning leads with a striking piece of political sociology: trust is collapsing across the American electorate — not just among Democrats, but within Trump's own working-class base. Times reporter Sabrina Tavernise spent weeks interviewing voters and found people who supported Trump enthusiastically now furious over diesel prices, grocery bills, and what they describe as broken campaign promises. Sixty percent of likely white voters without a college degree disapprove of Trump's handling of the cost of living. Among Republicans overall, only 29 percent say the economy is better than it was a year ago — down from 72 percent in January. "We drank the Kool-Aid, and we were wrong," a Michigan homeowner told Tavernise. Trust in the federal government overall now sits at just 17 percent, a Pew Research Center survey found. Republicans in tight midterm races are beginning to distance themselves from the president and calling openly for an end to the Iran conflict.

On Wall Street, technology continued to drive a split session. Financial stocks dragged the S&P 500 roughly flat Tuesday while climbing chip shares lifted the Nasdaq a half-percent to a fresh record. Alibaba unveiled its most powerful artificial intelligence chip yet, doubling down on its bid for AI hardware dominance. A separate story attracting regulatory scrutiny: roughly five billion dollars' worth of nearly identical trades on the prediction market platform Kalshi have caught the attention of federal regulators and traders since August. DoorDash agreed to pay $131.5 million to settle a New York City investigation into pay law violations for delivery workers. Andreessen Horowitz announced it will spend $35 million on a two-year tech academy as an alternative to traditional college for students aged 16 to 22. And Roche reported that an obesity drug candidate met the main goals of a midstage clinical trial, helping patients reduce both blood glucose and body weight — another milestone in the booming GLP-1 landscape.

On the health and policy front, Vice President Vance's task force has removed more than 750,000 enrollees from the Affordable Care Act's public insurance exchanges, claiming many had enrolled fraudulently. Separately, millions of Americans who rely on online pharmacies in Canada and other countries face losing access to inexpensive gray-market prescriptions as early as October, a consequence of a new Trump administration enforcement action. Six major hospitals have agreed to stop providing gender-transition treatments to minors following a deal with the administration. A Republican senator has called for an investigation into Donald Trump Jr. after reports emerged that a Kremlin-connected businessman gave the president's son a lavish party as a wedding gift. Trump also said Tuesday he is considering restricting diesel exports in an effort to curb the soaring fuel prices driving so much of the political discontent at home.

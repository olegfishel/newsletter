Morning Briefing

Tuesday, November 3, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and current web reporting.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 3, 2026. It is Election Day, and control of Congress is on the line. President Trump and Republicans are trying to hold on to their trifecta of the House, the Senate and the White House, while Democrats are aiming to win a majority in both chambers. Democrats are favored to take the House. The Senate is hazier, with the outcome likely to turn on high-profile races in states such as Texas, Michigan and Alaska. More than fifty-nine million Americans have already voted early, and the Justice Department says that for the first time it is sending about a thousand election monitors to all fifty states. Polls close on local time, and national results coverage ramps up once the first Eastern states report after seven o'clock tonight.

The Times looks at what a narrow Senate could mean. Democrats hold forty-seven seats and are expected to pick up more, but any majority will probably be slim, which hands real power to senators in the middle, such as Lisa Murkowski of Alaska and Susan Collins of Maine, if she wins re-election. A fifty-fifty tie would leave Republicans in charge, with Vice President JD Vance breaking ties, though it would also keep him tethered to Washington whenever the Senate is in session. Several independents are also running who say they would back neither party's leadership, and a challenge to the Alaska result could leave a seat open for weeks or months. In 2008, Al Franken waited more than eight months to be seated. The campaign's last hours were busy. The Supreme Court turned down a Trump administration request to make California set aside twenty thousand mail-in ballots that the administration claims went to noncitizens. Mr. Trump said he is willing to issue preemptive pardons to aides and allies, including Elon Musk, if Democrats win Congress. His election-eve speech, meanwhile, centered mostly on construction projects and renovations from his second term. The Journal says the results will offer clues about how much a new Congress can change and about what is shaping up as the most wide-open presidential primary season in years.

Overseas, the United States struck Iran's Qeshm Island on Monday. American officials described it as a limited, defensive action, taken after Iran tried to target a U.S. aircraft and the U.S. hit the launcher involved. Iranian state media reported five explosions. It is the latest in a series of small strikes carried out quietly in recent weeks, and officials stress it is not a return to major combat in what is now an eight-month conflict. The timing, on the eve of the midterms, follows comments from Mr. Trump playing down the risk of renewed war. Oil prices rose in early Asian trading on the news. Iran, for its part, says it is prepared to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within days if Washington meets demands that include lifting sanctions and releasing billions of dollars in frozen Iranian assets.

On Wall Street, the technology selloff continued Monday. The S&P 500 slipped half a percent and the Nasdaq fell one point one percent, while the Dow gained four tenths of a percent. The sharpest damage came from the chip sector after Marvell agreed to buy the optical-components maker Lumentum in an all-stock deal that the Journal values at about eighty billion dollars, though other outlets put the figure higher. Instead of cheering, investors sold both companies. Lumentum was the worst performer in the S&P 500, Marvell dropped roughly thirteen percent, and Bank of America and TD Cowen downgraded Marvell. The Journal reads it as a sign of creeping doubt about the artificial-intelligence trade. Apple, usually seen as a safe haven in that trade, also stumbled. Its quarterly revenue rose twelve percent to about one hundred fourteen billion dollars, but new CEO John Ternus warned that profit margins could weaken, pointing largely to the rising cost of memory chips, and the shares fell. Elsewhere, Palantir reported a surge in revenue, Marriott cited broad demand, and Carl Icahn's CVR Energy is nearing a deal worth more than four billion dollars to buy refineries from Marathon Petroleum. Today brings September job-openings data and earnings from AMD, Shopify and Pfizer. And on the AI front, the Journal reports that institutional investors are urging Anthropic to aim for an IPO valuation of around one and a half trillion dollars later this month, well below the figures near two trillion that had been floated, citing choppy markets and worries about overexposure to AI.

In Florida, investigators are focusing on severe weather after a Learjet carrying nine people plunged into a residential neighborhood near West Palm Beach on Sunday, killing everyone aboard. The plane was flying from Austin to the Bahamas. The crew reported icing and turbulence, then an overspeed condition and a Mayday call as the aircraft descended at a startling rate. The National Transportation Safety Board says there is some evidence that parts of the jet may have separated in the air, and it is still searching for all the wreckage and for a cockpit voice recorder. One of the pilots was twenty years old and had earned her pilot's license at seventeen, her mother said.

A few other stories are worth your time. A federal judge threw out charges against two former F.B.I. officials accused of lying to Congress in the Hunter Biden investigation, calling the case political theater. In Ukraine, Russian bombs killed twenty-six people in a shelter. The sudden collapse of the law firm Weil Gotshal has left about six hundred associates and a class of incoming recruits scrambling, after the firm announced on October twenty-eighth that it was dissolving and said it could no longer honor next year's job offers. The Journal reports that China is carrying out one of the largest peacetime naval buildups in modern history. In New York, a child died of measles, the state's first death from the disease in thirty years. El Niño has set off devastating flash floods across the Southwest. And the Nobel-winning economist William Nordhaus, who pioneered the study of climate change's economic impact, has died at eighty-five. For a lighter note, British politicians, preparing the country for doomsday, are reportedly stockpiling mascara and sauvignon blanc.

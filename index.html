Morning Briefing

Sunday, October 25, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page of Saturday, October 24, and today's 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning, Saturday's edition), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 25, 2026. The week ahead is crowded with consequential votes and decisions, and the Middle East is where the tension runs highest. Israelis go to the polls on Tuesday in an election widely seen as a referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's leadership through three years of war. The campaign's closing weekend brought a jolt: Netanyahu posted an artificial-intelligence image of a nuclear strike on Tel Aviv, warning of a second Holocaust at Iran's hands. Opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot called him hysterical and accused him of fear-mongering. The Israeli army has raised its alert to the highest level and sent tens of thousands of troops to several areas, bracing for possible missile fire from Iran or Lebanon. In the West Bank, Netanyahu ordered preparations for a full lockdown and promised a major operation in Hebron after Palestinian gunmen killed two Israeli soldiers on Friday. That operation is now moving ahead.

Farther south, Saudi Arabia reopened its main airport in Riyadh to international flights, more than two weeks after Houthi strikes knocked it out of service. The Iranian-backed rebels keep firing missiles and drones at the capital, and Saudi air defenses shot down a ballistic missile and a drone, with other missiles intercepted over the south. The Journal's 10-Point notes the toll on Riyadh Air, the kingdom's showpiece new airline, which launched this summer into a resurgent conflict and has since moved its aircraft out of its home base and halted almost all flying. President Trump says Iran can still avoid further military action by making a deal, though he wonders whether Tehran would honor one. Iran's foreign minister replied that new threats of war will not move his country, and both Trump and Netanyahu have renewed talk of fresh strikes after the American midterms.

Those midterms are now nine days away, and the argument over how the votes will be counted is heating up. The Journal reports that government corruption has climbed the list of voter concerns, with Democrats spending 182 million dollars this year to press the issue, even as Trump and his aides privately grumble about the headlines. Trump says he asked the Justice Department to sue Alaska to stop certification of its Senate results if the Republican incumbent loses narrowly. The department's suit points to possible voter confusion over a challenger whose name is nearly identical to Senator Dan Sullivan's. The Times says that, among other steps, is fueling fears that the president is laying the groundwork to contest an unwelcome result. Trump voted early in Florida before heading to a rally in Georgia, while Barack Obama hit Trump and Republicans hard at a rally in Ohio. House Democrats are already eyeing the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as a first act if they win. Separately, the Justice Department charged two former FBI employees with lying to Congress about investigations involving Hunter Biden.

Ukraine is threaded through several of this morning's stories. Elon Musk acknowledged that SpaceX chose to throttle Starlink service in Ukraine, though he insisted the American government controlled changes to Starshield, the military-grade version behind Kyiv's drone operations. Senators of both parties have told the White House they will block every Trump nominee until satellite communications for Ukraine's military are restored. Kyiv is bracing for its harshest winter of the war as Russian strikes batter the capital's power grid, and First Lady Melania Trump says she brokered a local cease-fire between Russian and Ukrainian forces in a southern Ukrainian city.

In France, more than 400,000 people marched on Saturday over school funding and the rising cost of living, and clashes erupted in Paris as the march wound down. Masked protesters threw objects and fireworks at police, who answered with tear gas, sound grenades and water cannons. The government says nearly 1,000 people were arrested, and that figure is expected to rise. Thousands more took to the streets in other European cities. Authorities had deployed 70,000 police officers, and Art Basel cut short its Paris fair for safety reasons. The unrest comes as France has become the biggest victim of rising global bond yields. Journal columnist James Mackintosh notes that net debt is on course to top 111 percent of economic output this year, and ten-year yields recently breached 5 percent for the first time since 2002.

On Wall Street, stocks ended a rocky week lower. The Dow led the way down on Friday, losing 1.2 percent, while the S&P 500 fell half a percent and the Nasdaq slipped just 0.2 percent. This week brings a flood of tests. The Federal Reserve opens a two-day meeting on Tuesday and announces its decision Wednesday. It is widely expected to hold rates steady, but investors will listen for hints of a hike in December. Microsoft and Alphabet report earnings on Wednesday, the same day Apple holds a product event in Los Angeles, and Amazon follows on Thursday along with the first estimate of third-quarter economic growth and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge. Exxon Mobil and Chevron report Friday. Analysts expect S&P 500 profits to jump 30 percent from a year ago, but just ten companies, many tied to artificial intelligence, account for roughly two-thirds of that growth. Some on Wall Street fear stocks would fall if Democrats win both chambers of Congress.

In corporate news, Moderna's shares dropped after details of a cancer-vaccine study raised questions about how widely the treatment would be used. American Express is cutting five percent of its global workforce to rein in costs, and Volvo Car will shed another 2,500 jobs, about six percent of its staff. Apple's new foldable iPhone went on sale Friday to mixed reviews. SK Hynix scrapped plans for a major American public offering of its flash-memory unit, Solidigm. Washington moved to exempt some Canadian products from 50 percent tariffs, a step aimed at helping Maine Senator Susan Collins and other Republicans in tight races. The Journal also reports that criminals are turning to AI-powered hacking tools, as a serious internet flaw has given rise to what it calls Black October.

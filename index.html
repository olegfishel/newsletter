Morning Briefing

Wednesday, October 28, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplementary web reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, October 28, 2026. The dominant story overnight is Israel, where voters rebuked Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu but did not hand anyone a clear victory. With a little over 85 percent of the total vote counted, Netanyahu's bloc stands at 53 seats and the bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot at 48, while Arab parties have won a historic 19. Sixty-one seats are needed for a majority, so neither side is close. Turnout was the highest in a generation, and Arab voters came out in record numbers, which could give their parties real leverage over whatever government emerges.

Netanyahu, who stays on as caretaker, claimed his camp won a decisive majority among the Jewish public and said he intends to build a right-wing national government. Forming a coalition could take weeks, or even months, so the showdown over whether Netanyahu remains prime minister is only beginning.

That political fight is tangled up with the war with Iran. The Wall Street Journal reports that Washington has told several Gulf Arab countries to prepare for a short, intense military campaign against Iran after the November third midterm elections. Reporting from several outlets says the Pentagon is moving in a Patriot battery, restocking munitions and readying bombers, with another carrier strike group and a Marine expeditionary unit expected to join roughly twenty American warships next month. President Trump has reportedly not made a final decision but is leaning toward a large post-election attack that could hit energy infrastructure, nuclear sites, and missile and drone capabilities. At a rally in Texas he teased that people would be seeing Iran in a couple of weeks. Tehran has warned it would answer with strikes on economic and civilian targets across the region. Crude oil is trading above one hundred dollars a barrel.

Back home, that countdown to the midterms is shaping the final push. Mr. Trump rallied for Republican Senate candidate Ken Paxton in Texas while Democrat James Talarico held an event just three miles away. New York Times and Siena polling finds the usually safe Republican Senate seat in South Carolina effectively tied, and in Maine, Susan Collins is working to survive a polarized electorate, anger over inflation, and lingering fallout from the Dobbs decision. In California, some of the world's richest people have been meeting weekly and raising hundreds of millions of dollars to defeat a proposed wealth tax that goes before voters next week.

On Wall Street, stocks rose for a second straight session, with the S&P 500 up about six-tenths of a percent and the Nasdaq up more than one percent, helped by a revived artificial intelligence trade. The ten-year Treasury yield is hovering right around five percent. The big event today is the Federal Reserve's rate decision at two o'clock Eastern. Economists broadly expect the central bank to hold rates steady, though previews suggest many see another hike coming in December, with September inflation at 3.7 percent and job growth having slowed sharply. Microsoft and Alphabet report earnings today, and Apple holds a product launch event in Los Angeles.

The AI boom itself is the other big business story. Anthropic published its plan on Monday to list on the Nasdaq. The New York Times notes the company earned about sixteen billion dollars in the first half of the year while losing roughly eighty billion, and that a potential valuation of around two trillion dollars would make it the largest public debut ever. The Journal adds that Anthropic's filing hints at a market opportunity of some forty trillion dollars, built on the value of knowledge work worldwide. Meanwhile, Broadcom's chief executive personally intervened with bank executives to seal a sixty-three billion dollar debt package, the largest ever raised for AI infrastructure. The Times put the scale in perspective: American big tech will spend about seven hundred billion dollars on AI infrastructure this year, the sector has taken on at least three trillion dollars in debt, and two-thirds of projected S&P 500 earnings growth traces directly to AI spending, even as not a single federal law regulates the technology.

Turning to China, the electric vehicle industry that is conquering markets abroad is hitting a wall at home. Passenger-car sales are down twenty-two percent through September, putting the country on track for its worst year since 2014. Local governments keep dozens of failing brands alive to protect jobs, which pushes them to flood overseas markets with cheap cars. Economists are also openly questioning Beijing's claim of strong growth, given the continuing slide in the property market. And Mr. Trump's fifty percent tariff on Canadian aluminum has driven up costs for American manufacturers who badly need it.

In the rest of the world, the Times reports that the Trump administration may drop sanctions on a Russian sovereign investment fund that American officials once called Vladimir Putin's slush fund, while the leaders of Finland and Norway made a rare phone call to Putin as part of efforts to end the war in Ukraine.

A few closing items. Brothers Tal and Oren Alexander, once high-flying luxury brokers, were sentenced to thirty years in prison for sex trafficking, as was their brother Alon. At Harvard, a demanding humanities course with a book a week and no devices drew twice as many applicants as seats. In baseball, the Tampa Bay Rays stunned the Milwaukee Brewers with a twelfth-inning rally to stay alive, trailing three games to one, with Game 5 tonight. And in San Diego, the City Council approved a moratorium on data centers, and home prices rose over the summer.

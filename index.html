Good morning, it's Monday, August 10, 2026. The dominant story as the week opens is a sharp public break between Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and President Trump over the future of Gaza, with Israel rejecting the American-backed peace framework that the White House had been celebrating just weeks ago. Meanwhile, the Strait of Hormuz remains closed and increasingly volatile, Iran has attacked a ship in the Gulf, and markets are surging on record corporate earnings. There is a great deal to catch up on.

Start with Gaza, where the peace process took a serious blow over the weekend. Netanyahu announced that Israel rejects the Trump administration's 15-point roadmap, which would have had Hamas disarm and Israeli forces withdraw from Gaza in tandem. The prime minister was emphatic: there would be no Israeli pullout, he said, until Hamas is genuinely disarmed, not fictitiously disarmed. The rebuke came only days after President Trump had hailed what he called a historic framework, and it opens a rare and public rift between the two governments. Netanyahu faces an October election in which his coalition's majority is at risk, giving him strong domestic incentives to take a harder line than Washington would prefer. Hamas, for its part, said it will only proceed if Israel halts strikes and pulls back to the October ceasefire boundary. For now, the path to a lasting settlement looks significantly narrower than it did last week.

The other major flashpoint is the Strait of Hormuz, where the situation darkened considerably over the weekend. Iran's Supreme National Security Council published a list of sweeping new demands before it would consider reopening the strategic waterway to commercial shipping: the lifting of American naval restrictions, the removal of U.S. military forces from the region, the release of frozen Iranian assets, and the payment of war reparations. The White House had signaled as recently as last week that a deal to clear the strait was within reach. That optimism now looks premature. Complicating matters further, the United Arab Emirates said Iran fired a missile and struck one of its vessels, an ADNOC tanker, as it transited the strait. There were no casualties reported, but the UAE said it is the latest in a series of more than a dozen attacks on its ships since the American-Israeli campaign against Iran began in February. Iran is increasingly treating control of the Hormuz chokepoint as its most powerful remaining lever against American pressure, and the new hardline demands suggest it intends to keep squeezing.

At home, the Senate narrowly confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general Saturday after a contentious battle that focused heavily on critics' concerns about his willingness to use the Justice Department in service of the president's political and financial interests. And President Trump announced that Will Scharf, currently serving as White House staff secretary, will become the new White House counsel in September, a significant personnel move ahead of midterm elections and what could be an active season of congressional investigations.

On a somber personal note, Hunter Biden told the BBC that his father's cancer has spread to his bones and further, offering the most explicit public account yet of former President Joe Biden's condition. The elder Biden has kept an extremely low profile since leaving office.

On Wall Street, the story is records. The major indexes pushed to new highs again this morning, with the S&P 500 futures trading near 7,784, driven by a blockbuster stretch of corporate earnings. Results from America's largest companies have not only lifted prices but have eased concerns that the rally was dangerously narrow and overly reliant on a handful of artificial-intelligence plays. The blended earnings growth rate for the S&P 500 this quarter is running at roughly 50 percent year over year, far above initial expectations. AI-linked capital spending, which Big Tech companies are forecast to exceed 650 billion dollars on in 2026 alone, continues to validate investor enthusiasm. Gold is at $4,395 and crude oil is up nearly two percent to $79.51, partly reflecting the Hormuz tensions. Bitcoin sits around $65,000.

The New York Times this morning leads with a major investigation into a company called Flock Safety, which has installed 120,000 small cameras on street corners and lampposts across the country. The cameras photograph every passing vehicle, capturing license plates along with dents, stickers, and other identifying features, feeding an enormous database that 7,000 police departments — about 40 percent of all departments in the United States — pay to access. The company says it scans 20 billion plates a month, meaning the average registered vehicle is captured roughly 800 times a year. The story has ignited a fierce backlash: residents are shooting, painting over, and dismantling the cameras; privacy advocates have built apps to map routes that dodge them; and a Tennessee congressional candidate charged with shooting four Flock cameras has raised more than $13,000 on GoFundMe. A Republican congressman has introduced legislation requiring federal agents to obtain a warrant before accessing Flock's data. Critics increasingly name Flock in the same breath as Palantir, as a symbol of creeping, nearly invisible mass surveillance.

Elsewhere this morning: Meta released an open-source version of its most powerful artificial intelligence model. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez announced on social media that she is freezing her eggs, saying she wanted to depict women making choices across all aspects of life. A powerful typhoon struck China's coast, forcing more than a million people to evacuate. A Ukrainian drone attack on an industrial city deep inside Russia killed at least 13 people. Lake Mead, the largest reservoir in the United States, has dropped to record-low water levels. And Taylor Farms announced a second recall, this time covering jalapeño-containing products after a salmonella link was identified, weeks after recalling lettuce linked to a cyclospora outbreak.

In San Diego, the city paused over the weekend to mark the anniversary of the end of World War II in the Pacific, with local veterans gathering to remember the formal surrender with Japan. In international crime news, Daniel Kinahan, the alleged head of one of the world's most feared criminal organizations, was extradited from Dubai to Ireland, a significant law enforcement milestone. And locally, San Diego FC remained victorious over Club Tijuana's Xolos, while the Padres hammered four home runs to defeat the Houston Astros on Sunday.

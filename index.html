Morning Briefing

Friday, November 20, 2026

Sources used: WSJ front page and The 10-Point, NYT front page and The Morning, San Diego Union-Tribune front page (screenshot of A1), and web reporting. Good morning, it's Friday, November 20, 2026. The dominant story is the first-ever summit of the American, Chinese and Russian leaders, which wrapped up in Shenzhen, and the two big papers read it very differently. The Journal says Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin ended the meeting with a joint denunciation of nuclear war, agreed to open a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks, and projected unity on energy security, in a sit-down that reflected shifts in the world order. The Times is more skeptical. It says Trump flaunted his camaraderie with Xi and Putin, calling the Russian leader his friend and marveling at China's welcome, but came away with little to show: no breakthroughs on nuclear control, on the war in Ukraine, or on artificial intelligence. Times columnists add that Trump is projecting power abroad while being forced to share it at home, and that Xi and Putin share a vision of usurping American power. The Union-Tribune's front page, running a Washington Post story, frames the three as presidents agreeing to target their mutual adversaries and seeking fewer constraints on their power, including a shared wariness of institutions like the United Nations. The Journal's 10-Point says the whirlwind Asia trip offered a glimpse of how Trump hopes to cement his legacy through foreign-policy breakthroughs. China barred a Bloomberg TV reporter from covering the leaders' meeting after she asked a question.

Ukraine is where the summit's results look thinnest. The Times reports that after a bridge attack, Putin said he will not be negotiating a ceasefire, and that Russian drones swarmed Kyiv and damaged the Dutch and Austrian embassies. Meanwhile the Journal reports that Washington has floated an idea with European officials in which Kyiv would give up territory to Moscow in exchange for NATO membership. The Journal credits presidential envoy Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff with raising it in closed-door talks, though it is unclear how seriously mediators are considering it. Moscow has long fought Kyiv's NATO bid, and Trump himself called it unacceptable to Russia early last year. Other web reports from mid-November say Zelensky answered that he has no right to make such a decision alone. Also from the Times, Europe is racing to build air defenses that are cheaper than the drones they shoot down, and the war has been a catastrophe for Ukraine's wildlife.

The Iran war keeps pressing in. The Times says Trump is considering striking Iran again after new intelligence on Iranian capabilities and growing pressure from Israel, and the Journal says the head of Mossad lobbied for renewed joint strikes during a visit to Washington last week. A Houthi projectile struck Riyadh's tallest tower, which houses the Saudi sovereign wealth fund's offices; the Journal highlights the damage to the tower, while the Times describes it as damage to the fund's headquarters. The Times says Iran keeps up unrelenting attacks on ships in the Strait of Hormuz and that the war will remake the global energy order. The International Energy Agency figure it cites is $700 billion in extra sales for oil and gas exporters this year over the ten-year average. The Journal says oil has retreated from its highs but that $85 to $95 a barrel may be the new normal, and crude is about $82.60 this morning. On the carrier USS George Washington in the Arabian Sea, the 5,000 sailors go through 48,000 Uncrustables a month, and one pilot says he worries more about forgetting his than about being shot down by Iran.

Other world news. The International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority, the first time the court has found a state culpable of genocide, according to the Times. The Times adds that the ruling sets up a court fight over Israel and Gaza, since judges there must weigh similar questions about genocidal intent and mass killing during fighting against militants. Greenland is pulling closer to the United States by an hour, as it moves its clocks. In Britain, four men were convicted of sexually assaulting a woman whose husband drugged her and arranged for strangers to rape her. The U.N. climate summit, COP31, concludes today in Antalya, Turkey. In Japan, Russia's takeover of Japan Tobacco units deals an $8.6 billion blow, and a beloved penguin mascot is retiring.

On Wall Street, the story is Anthropic's debut. The company behind Claude raised $81 billion in the largest IPO in history, handing early backers enormous profits, but it arrived as the market soured on AI stocks. The Journal's newsletter says shares opened more than 30 percent above the offering price and closed up 17 percent. The Times reports the company opened trading at a $2 trillion valuation and ended the day at $1.7 trillion, while earlier pricing reports had put its value around $1 to $1.5 trillion. The Times also says Anthropic's debut is a watershed for AI's risks and rewards, as Washington struggles to prepare for disruption to workers and businesses, and that hundreds of employees became multimillionaires, with a handful becoming billionaires. For the market as a whole, the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow fell 2 percent, 2.3 percent and 2.2 percent on Thursday, so the Journal concludes the IPO failed to revive the AI trade. This morning futures point lower again: Dow futures are down about 0.4 percent, the S&P 500 down 1 percent and the Nasdaq down nearly 1.9 percent. Europe's Stoxx 600 is off 1.7 percent, the VIX has jumped to about 31, the 10-year Treasury yields roughly 4.97 percent, and gold is down more than 3 percent. A Journal commentary argues the more lucrative phase of AI may be applying the technology rather than building infrastructure.

The AI industry has other troubles. California's attorney general issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia amid the scandal over former chief executive Jensen Huang. The Times details that Huang berated and threatened employees for decades, using graphic insults, because he believed humiliation was the only way to make them work harder; a spokesman called the anecdotes fabrications that are now impossible to disprove. The Times also reports how Nvidia chips ended up within reach of China's military, via an Alibaba computing lab, and the Journal says new U.S. rules issued after the scandal will slow China's tech champions further. In a Journal exclusive, Wall Street pledged to mobilize $500 billion for Nvidia's customers but hasn't put up a dime. Alibaba, meanwhile, posted a surge in second-quarter profit, and the Times says China was shut out of a blockbuster public offering again. Schools are pushing back on the technology too: the Times reports a growing anti-AI campus movement, with New York and Chicago officials asking Google to remove AI from its education software.

More business news. Data from thousands of customers of Visa, Mastercard and JPMorgan Chase was leaked by a ransomware group. Walmart says its e-commerce and advertising businesses are growing even as store sales slide, and the Times says America's biggest retailers are selling affordability this holiday season. Novartis tried to reassure investors that sales can keep growing through coming patent expirations. Sullivan and Cromwell co-chair Robert Giuffra, who drew fire for representing Trump, is leaving firm leadership, and the Times separately reports that the firm did $11.8 million of free work for the administration. Delaware's insurance regulator joined federal authorities in investigating Dodgers owner Mark Walter's loans, and the Padres' owners are buying the WNBA's Los Angeles Sparks from him for more than $700 million, a record for a women's pro team. The Fed approved smaller raises for staff at its regional banks, with minimum annual increases falling to 1 percent next year from 3 percent. Today brings the final University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading and BJ's Wholesale Club earnings. The Journal also notes that house flipping is hardest in San Antonio, where margins are slightly negative, and that parents navigating the booming autism therapy industry face little oversight.

In Washington, the Senate passed a bipartisan bill to speed up energy, mining and infrastructure projects, 62 to 33. The Times calls it the biggest change to environmental law in a generation, though its fate in the House is uncertain, and notes the split among environmentalists. Senate Republicans suspended their effort to pass $350 billion in additional military spending. The national cyber director, Sean Cairncross, is stepping down; the Journal ties it to AI hacking threats, while the Times ties it to the week's tech outages and questions Trump's national security cuts, adding that experts say outages may become routine. Also in the Times: three MAGA women who defined an era in Congress are leaving; a Texas House winner's rival says she can't take office; a senator warns lawmakers' data is vulnerable to the Justice Department; the Justice Department dropped an Alaska Senate race lawsuit; the fight over Venezuelan deportation protections has reached the Supreme Court; ICE officers are training for car stops at a Brooklyn compound; and Holiday Inn workers say they were threatened with deportation. International student enrollment fell again, and the Army delayed a plan to blow up toxic ammunition in Iowa after an outcry. Two former South Carolina officers were charged in a fatal road-rage case.

In New York, Mayor Mamdani signed a ban on horse-drawn carriages that will end a Central Park fixture, and the Times says it was challenged in court immediately. Bus fare inspectors could be fired over altercations with riders, and footage captured the fatal collapse of a Brooklyn building.

Closer to home, the Union-Tribune leads with rain: a storm drenched the county on Thursday, delaying a Padres event downtown, with more sporadic showers possible this morning. The paper's top story says opponents of a proposed high-voltage transmission line have sued the state's grid operator and San Diego Gas and Electric, alleging that the construction contract was moved from a lower-priced winning bidder, Horizon West, to the utility, at a cost they put at hundreds of millions of dollars. Another front-page story says a San Diego City Council member is demanding an investigation after an internal report found evidence of systemic harassment of women in the lifeguard service, with the mayor and council leaders shocked; 18 current and former female lifeguards were interviewed. And the state has approved a revised $50 million grant for a mental health facility at Palomar, now planned at an Escondido outpatient site, with the inpatient unit tied to UC San Diego's East Campus. The Union-Tribune also runs the New York Times story on a blood test that could detect CTE in living football players; the Times says two new studies suggest an unusual protein may allow diagnosis.

Finally, for the culture shelf. Fiona the hippopotamus died after eating a child's knitted hat. The Times marks its 175th anniversary with a list of the ten most consequential days in its history. Van Gogh soared to $190.4 million at Sotheby's after a last-minute settlement. Jennifer Lopez has a Thanksgiving concert special, Nicolas Cage stars in an NFL biopic, and a Billie Jean King documentary gets a rave. Parents are in a tizzy over school photos that look like mug shots. With Thanksgiving next week, the Morning's advice is to check your plates and deep-clean the fridge now.

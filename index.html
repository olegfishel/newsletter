Morning Briefing

Sunday, August 9, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Sunday, August 9, 2026. The week ended in Washington on a hectic note — a 4 a.m. Senate confirmation, a last-minute spending deal to avoid a shutdown, and Iran's publication of a sweeping list of conditions that may put a lasting reopening of the Strait of Hormuz further out of reach. Here is what you need to know.

The sharpest foreign-policy development of the weekend came from Tehran. Iran's Supreme National Security Council published a list of demands it says the United States must meet before the Strait of Hormuz will fully reopen to international shipping. The conditions are sweeping: the United States must lift its naval blockade, remove military forces from the region surrounding Iran, pay war reparations, release frozen Iranian assets, and, in Tehran's words, "permanently end the war" with Iran and its allied proxy forces. The demands land at a complicated moment. President Trump had spent recent weeks signaling openness to walking away from the conflict now that a temporary ceasefire is in place, but these new conditions — demanding a wholesale American military withdrawal from the Persian Gulf — effectively reset the negotiating table. The war has also taken a quiet toll on U.S. military capacity: officials say weapons stockpiles have been drawn down to troubling levels and that deliveries to European and Asian allies are running behind schedule.

In a related diplomatic development, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan signed a mutual-defense agreement, a notable trilateral alignment as those countries seek new security guarantees in the wake of years of upheaval across the Middle East. Meanwhile, the Times of Israel reports the UAE confirmed one of its vessels was targeted by an airstrike in the vicinity of the strait, a reminder that the waterway remains a live flashpoint even while formal talks are underway.

Back in Washington, the Senate confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general in a narrow 50-to-49 vote that stretched past 4 a.m. Saturday. The vote was largely along party lines — only Senators Susan Collins and Lisa Murkowski joined Democrats in opposition. Blanche, who served as Trump's personal defense attorney during his criminal trials, overcame weeks of uncertainty in the confirmation process. His path cleared after Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana announced Friday he would support the nomination. The Justice Department now has its permanent leader for the first time since the administration took office.

The Senate also passed, by a lopsided 90-to-6 margin, a stopgap spending measure that keeps the federal government funded through December 11, clearing the way for lawmakers to head into their summer recess without triggering a shutdown before the midterm elections. The bill blocks the administration from giving senior political appointees greater authority over federal grant reviews and rejects a White House request for a billion dollars to begin work on a new class of battleships. The measure now goes to the House, which will take it up when members return from recess. Notably, the Senate adjourned without passing President Trump's voting reform bill, a priority that Democrats and some Republicans had resisted through the summer.

The week's jobs report delivered an uncomfortable surprise. The economy shed 23,000 jobs in July — a reversal that confounded expectations for a modest gain of roughly 83,000. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1 percent, but for a discouraging reason: the labor force shrank by 264,000, as more Americans stopped looking for work. Labor force participation fell to 61.4 percent, a level not seen in more than five years. The Bureau of Labor Statistics also revised May and June figures downward by a combined 103,000. Wage growth slowed to 3.2 percent year-over-year, below forecasts. The Wall Street Journal noted the data sends conflicting signals — unemployment is low, but fewer people are actively seeking jobs, which is a challenge for the broader economy. Despite the weak payroll print, U.S. stocks still posted strong gains on the week: the S&P 500 added 3.6 percent to reach a new record, the Nasdaq rose 5.2 percent, and the Dow climbed 3 percent, driven by strong corporate earnings from the country's largest companies.

In the technology world, OpenAI disclosed that it had halted work on a coming artificial-intelligence model after internal evaluations led the company to conclude it could not rule out that the model had, in the Journal's words, "critical cyber capabilities" — a potential national security concern. Separately, the 10-Point's editors highlighted the acute pressure now facing traditional software-as-a-service companies: generative AI is upending the SaaS industry, and the Journal's technology reporters describe firms racing to rebuild themselves from the ground up to survive. Elon Musk's SpaceX had a volatile trading week: shares plummeted nearly 14 percent in a single session before recovering to end more than 20 percent above where they started, representing a market-value swing of more than $300 billion — a sign of how much investor faith in the company's long-term promises remains the linchpin of its valuation.

The week also brought fresh scrutiny to financial dealings connected to the Trump family. The New York Times reported that World Liberty Financial, the Trump family's cryptocurrency business, accepted $100 million from a businessman who is currently under federal investigation for money laundering. On the political front, Trump renewed his effort to fire Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, pressing her to respond to allegations of mortgage fraud that Cook has publicly called baseless. And Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth revoked the security clearance of a former senior Biden administration defense official over allegations that the official leaked classified information about Air Force One.

In Latin America, Abelardo de la Espriella was sworn in as Colombia's new president in the symbolically significant city of Cali, pledging to suppress drug cartels with an iron hand. And in New York City, a group of homeowners filed suit to delay the rollout of Mayor Zohran Mamdani's pied-à-terre tax, which targets residential properties valued at $5 million or more.

On a lighter note from this week's 10-Point: estate-sale shopping has become, as the Journal puts it, a "full-contact sport." Fueled by the booming resale market and social-media influencers showcasing their finds and profits, the once-leisurely weekend ritual of buying a deceased homeowner's belongings has turned intensely competitive. And the Journal's "A-Hed" feature finds an unlikely winner in the battle for customers: vineyards competing for airline business, as in-flight wine service has become a prestige arena where passengers are more than willing to indulge.

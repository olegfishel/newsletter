Good morning, it's Sunday, September 13, 2026. We begin with a story that captures just how profoundly the global order is being reshuffled: Canada — America's closest ally, largest trading partner, and northern neighbor — is quietly negotiating to become an "associate member" of the European Union.

That is the explosive conclusion of a Wall Street Journal investigation published this weekend. Prime Minister Mark Carney's team has been working through diplomatic backchannels — including, the Journal reports, members of an exclusive Ottawa book club — to lock Canada informally into Europe's twenty-one-trillion-dollar single market. The idea is to let Canadian goods, workers, and services move frictionlessly across the Atlantic in strategic sectors like energy, artificial intelligence, and defense. It is a hedge against what Ottawa sees as the permanent deterioration of the American partnership. Canada already has the CETA trade agreement with the EU, provisionally in effect since 2017, but Carney is reaching for something far more ambitious. European officials are described as open to the idea of a new, yet-to-be-defined "associate" status — a designation that has never before existed in EU law. It is, the Journal's editor in chief writes today, one of the most consequential geopolitical pivots in modern history.

Amplifying that seismic shift is a story unfolding in the Middle East, where the Iran war is reshaping global shipping lanes and energy markets in real time. Iran-backed Houthi militants have seized Mayun Island — also known as Perim — inside the Bab al-Mandeb strait, the narrow chokepoint between Yemen and Djibouti through which much of the world's oil flows on its way to the Suez Canal and European markets. Combined with their earlier stranglehold on the Strait of Hormuz approaches, Iran's allies now effectively control two of the most critical energy arteries on the planet. Saudi Arabia, which had been routing crude around the Houthi threat via Red Sea pipelines, announced this weekend that a key pipeline had been attacked multiple times and shut down. Crude oil has already surged past ninety-nine dollars a barrel. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called President Trump twice this week requesting American airstrikes on Houthi positions; the administration declined, offering intelligence and targeting data instead. Trump said publicly this morning that the war could end "very soon," suggesting Tehran is deliberately prolonging the conflict to influence November's midterm elections. Back in Washington, a CIA official accused of walking out of headquarters with forty million dollars in gold bars has reached a tentative plea agreement.

Those rising energy prices feed directly into the inflation problem the Federal Reserve confronts when it convenes Tuesday and Wednesday. August's Consumer Price Index came in at 3.4% year-over-year — stubbornly unchanged from July — with core prices rising 0.3% month-over-month, above forecasts. Markets are now pricing roughly seventy percent odds of a rate hike at this week's meeting, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has signaled a hawkish posture. The ten-year Treasury yield closed Friday at 4.974%. Stocks eked out gains on the report — the S&P 500 rose 0.9%, the Dow and Nasdaq each gained one percent — as investors appeared relieved that inflation at least didn't accelerate further. But the path ahead for consumers, borrowers, and the central bank is narrowing fast.

Meanwhile, a moral contradiction is playing out at the frontier of artificial intelligence. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei this week called publicly for a global slowdown in cutting-edge AI development to address safety risks. OpenAI's Sam Altman and Elon Musk agreed with him in principle. Yet the Journal notes the obvious paradox: Anthropic, the company Amodei co-founded specifically over concerns that OpenAI wasn't taking safety seriously enough, is not actually slowing its own work. It remains locked in a development race even as its employees warn the technology could be dangerous. The New York Times reports that former President Barack Obama has called on Democrats to put AI oversight at the center of their agenda — while Washington broadly remains silent. Inside offices and around dinner tables, the Journal writes, America's great AI freakout has begun in earnest.

Yesterday, the nation paused to mark twenty-five years since September 11th, 2001. Ceremonies were held at the memorial in Lower Manhattan, in Shanksville, Pennsylvania, and at the Pentagon — honoring the nearly three thousand people killed in the deadliest terrorist attack on American soil, and the growing number of first responders whose illnesses linked to the attacks have since taken their lives. The Journal also reports, in an exclusive, that four former presidents — Clinton, Bush, Obama, and Biden — have for years maintained quiet backchannels, celebrating counterterrorism milestones and advising each other on the ongoing legacy of the War on Terror. Separately, intelligence officials have confirmed that before Iran's July attack that killed three U.S. troops at a base in Jordan, Tehran acquired high-resolution satellite images of the facility from Chinese entities — a finding that deepens tensions ahead of an upcoming Trump-Xi summit.

On the courts, Elena Rybakina of Kazakhstan defeated Aryna Sabalenka six-four, five-seven, six-two in Saturday's U.S. Open women's final, claiming her second Grand Slam title of the year and her third overall. Today's men's final between Ben Shelton and Alexander Zverev could cost you one hundred dollars to attend — or twenty thousand, depending on your seat. Sweden is holding a general election today in a closely watched contest that could bring the far right into government for the first time or hand power back to the left. And as the NFL's opening weekend wraps up, the San Diego Chargers unveil what their coaches are calling a new-look offense when they host the Arizona Cardinals this afternoon, while the Padres are at San Francisco.

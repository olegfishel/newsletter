Morning Briefing

Tuesday, August 11, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times Morning newsletter, San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, Reuters, Bloomberg, AP, Washington Post

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 11, 2026. The world's most consequential energy chokepoint sits at the center of this morning's biggest story: Persian Gulf nations are quietly accepting that Iran may control the Strait of Hormuz not merely for now, but permanently. The Wall Street Journal reports that major energy exporters in the Gulf dislike the emerging framework to partially reopen the waterway but fear that a return to open conflict would be worse. Iran and Oman are said to be in the final stages of a deal, yet Tehran insists the arrangement would bar American and Israeli vessels from transiting the strait. The Trump administration is pressing Iran through economic pressure rather than military force, betting that sanctions can achieve what war could not — though Iran has already walked back at least one framework that negotiators believed was settled, triggering two weeks of renewed fighting and bringing both sides close to all-out war. The White House is eager to declare victory and move on, but the terms remain bitterly contested and the region remains tense.

On Wall Street, the story burning through trading floors this morning is Elon Musk and the question of whether he intends to fold Tesla into SpaceX. The Journal revealed a critical clause buried in Tesla's shareholder-approved one-trillion-dollar pay package: a merger with SpaceX would automatically wipe away the performance targets that currently stand between Musk and billions of dollars in stock awards. In plain terms, a deal would deliver his payout faster and hand him the tighter control of both companies he has long sought. Musk himself has been dropping hints — on Tesla's most recent earnings call he cited "more and more overlap" between the two businesses — though SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell has described formal merger talks as premature. Both stocks have retreated sharply in recent months, complicating the deal math considerably.

The broader artificial-intelligence landscape is also moving at speed. Nvidia struck a deal with some of Wall Street's largest financial firms to raise $500 billion for AI infrastructure — a figure that underscores the depth of capital now flowing into the build-out. Intel separately announced a $15 billion common stock offering, citing unprecedented customer demand driven by AI compute. And Mark Zuckerberg published a sprawling 6,500-word essay laying out Meta's strategy: more open-weight AI models, deeper investment in the communities that host its data centers, and a bid to shape the industry's direction rather than follow it. Meanwhile, Anthropic is quietly preparing for what could be the largest initial public offering in history, working to reassure investors even as it navigates tensions with the Trump administration and backlash against new data-center construction across the country.

Today is the busiest primary Election Day before November. Voters in Minnesota, Wisconsin, Connecticut, Vermont, Alabama, and South Carolina head to the polls. The New York Times highlights three races that will test the shape of American politics. In Minnesota, a Democratic Senate primary pits progressive Lieutenant Governor Peggy Flanagan against moderate Representative Angie Craig, with Flanagan linking Craig to a state social-services fraud scandal and Craig arguing her opponent is too far left to win statewide. In South Carolina, Republicans choose a successor to the late Senator Lindsey Graham — his sister Darline Graham, who holds the seat on a temporary basis and carries a Trump endorsement, is running, but remains something of a political unknown. In Wisconsin, democratic socialist Francesca Hong leads polling for the Democratic gubernatorial nomination, an outcome that has establishment Democrats on edge in one of the country's most closely divided states.

A humanitarian catastrophe is deepening in South America. A 7.4-magnitude earthquake — the strongest to strike Colombia in decades — hit the country's coffee-growing heartland on Monday, and the death toll has climbed past 160 with rescue workers still digging through rubble in Cali, Pereira, and Manizales. More than 5,000 homes were damaged or destroyed. Emergency teams, search dogs, and drones worked through the night to find survivors beneath collapsed buildings. Officials warned the death toll is expected to rise further as crews reach more remote areas.

Back in Washington, President Trump signed an executive order directing that most American children receive vaccines against eleven diseases — notably below the seventeen vaccinations recommended by the Centers for Disease Control. Trump again made comments linking childhood shots to autism, a claim that decades of scientific research has consistently rejected. The administration also released new transparency rules for food-ingredient labeling while again delaying a promised formal definition of ultraprocessed food. Separately, Senate Republicans released text messages from Anthony Fauci's government-issued phone, adding detail to the picture of his early pandemic communications, including discussions of vaccine risk to pregnant women.

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh faces a critical test this week as fresh inflation data approaches. Warsh has built his tenure on tough anti-inflation rhetoric, and if the incoming numbers show prices remaining stubborn, he will face mounting pressure to raise interest rates — a move with potentially wide consequences for markets and the broader economy. On the climate front, July was confirmed as the hottest month ever recorded in the contiguous United States, with the nationwide average temperature hitting 76.9 degrees Fahrenheit, more than three degrees above the twentieth-century baseline and edging out records set in 1936 and 2012. Every state ranked in the top third of its historical temperature record for the month.

In a lighter note from the Journal's front page, Americans have become the world's most zealous international travelers — once homebodies, they are now packing itineraries at record rates, led by wealthy boomers and restless Gen Zers flush with pandemic savings and a renewed appetite for the world. And in South Korea, mid-six-figure bonuses for engineers at Samsung and SK Hynix have made chip-industry workers the most sought-after bachelors in the country — a niche but telling sign of where fortunes are being made in the global technology race.

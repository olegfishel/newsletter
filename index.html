Thursday, August 13, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 13, 2026. The week's big economic report landed Wednesday, and it came in tame: consumer prices rose just 3.4 percent in July, a slight easing from the prior month that gives the Federal Reserve room to hold off on further rate hikes at its September meeting. Markets received the number calmly — the S&P 500 gained about a third of a percent, the Nasdaq half a point, and the Dow was essentially flat. But the deeper financial story may be the tariff-refund windfall now pouring into corporate America. More than forty S&P 500 companies have now reported roughly 9.6 billion dollars in total tariff refunds over the past quarter, with the payments arriving far faster than originally feared. Apple leads the pack at nearly 2.2 billion dollars, followed by Nike at 986 million and FedEx at about 800 million — a meaningful earnings tailwind heading into the back half of the year.

The Wall Street Journal's lead investigation today pulls back the curtain on one of the more unsettling national-security stories in recent memory: North Korea has built a covert workforce inside American companies. Using stolen identities, sophisticated AI tools, and a network of U.S. accomplices, operatives working on behalf of Kim Jong Un's government have been landing remote technology jobs at American firms and funneling the proceeds back to Pyongyang. The Journal spent a year reporting the story, drawing on leaked emails, screen recordings, and video evidence. Among the findings: a single team of North Korean operatives infiltrated at least eight companies in just a few months. The FBI has also confirmed this week that a North Korean remote worker was employed at a U.S. federal agency for months before being caught — a breach that has stunned counterintelligence officials. The Journal has released a documentary, "Infiltrated: North Korea's Secret U.S. Workforce," alongside its written investigation.

The crisis in the Strait of Hormuz is deepening with no clear end in sight. President Trump claimed again this week that the United States has total control of the strategic waterway — and even floated the idea of keeping it permanently — but the numbers on the water contradict his assertion. Just eight vessels transited the strait on Tuesday, a fraction of the 130 to 140 ships that crossed daily before the U.S.-Iran conflict began in late February. Iran has established its own "Persian Gulf Strait Authority" requiring ships to obtain passage permits from Tehran, and a top Iranian adviser said this week that the country could sustain the standoff until Trump is out of office. The International Energy Agency has warned that the world's oil stockpiles are being depleted at a rapid pace, making a resolution of the shipping impasse increasingly urgent for global energy markets.

In Colombia, rescue teams worked around the clock searching for survivors of the 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck the country's west last week — the most powerful quake to hit Colombia this century. The death toll has climbed past 265, with more than 200 people still feared trapped under rubble in the city of Cali alone. The New York Times this morning featured a striking photograph of Diana Marcela Troncoso, 31, who was pulled alive from the wreckage after nearly 30 hours — a reminder of why exhausted rescue crews keep going. The SDUT also flagged the story prominently, noting crews racing to find earthquake survivors.

At the White House, a familiar face is leaving. Press secretary Karoline Leavitt, one of President Trump's most visible and trusted advisers, announced she will step down at the end of August. Leavitt recently had a second child, and Trump confirmed she is leaving to spend more time with her family. Her departure marks a significant shift in the administration's public face heading into the home stretch of the midterm campaign season. Separately, as he left his post at Google DeepMind, chief executive Demis Hassabis made a quiet but notable pitch to government officials and AI-lab leaders: he urged the creation of an independent safety entity to set guardrails for artificial general intelligence — a sign that even those building the most powerful AI systems are growing anxious about what comes next.

In sports, the Los Angeles Lakers have a new ownership group. Bob Iger, who spent roughly twenty years as Disney's chief executive before stepping down earlier this year, and Joshua Kushner, founder of the venture capital firm Thrive Capital and brother of Jared Kushner, have agreed to buy a controlling interest in the Lakers from Mark Walter at a record valuation of 12.5 billion dollars. Walter had paid about 10 billion dollars for the team just over a year ago, putting his gain at roughly 2.5 billion. Federal regulators are reportedly investigating Walter's broader financial interests, which may help explain the timing of the sale. Kushner will be required to sell his minority stake in the Miami Heat to complete the deal.

On a more wondrous note, Europe experienced its first total solar eclipse in decades yesterday, and by all accounts it was extraordinary. Millions of people from Iceland to Spain watched as the moon slid across the face of the sun, briefly turning day to dusk, sending birds shrieking into the sky, and dropping the air temperature in an instant. The New York Times devoted its Morning newsletter to photographs and firsthand accounts: crowds chanting in Spanish towns, the sun narrowing to a sliver described as "a French tip on a celestial manicure," before totality arrived in Mallorca just before sundown, rendering it even more dramatic against the horizon. For Americans, the wait is long — a comparable coast-to-coast eclipse won't occur until 2045.

In Midwest politics, David Crowley won the Wisconsin Democratic primary for governor, narrowly defeating Democratic socialist Francesca Hong, while Abdul El-Sayed eked out a win in Michigan. The Wall Street Journal reads both results as a signal that the progressive left is encountering real resistance outside of deep-blue strongholds, and that the argument over the Democratic Party's identity — a debate that has been simmering since 2024 — remains very much unresolved as the midterm election approaches.

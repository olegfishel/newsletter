Good morning, it's Monday, August 17, 2026. The 60-day clock on the U.S.–Iran ceasefire has run out with no deal in sight, crossing a deadline that many on both sides had already quietly abandoned, and raising fresh questions about whether the six-month-old conflict will soon flare back to life.

The 14-point memorandum of understanding hammered out in Islamabad and signed on June 17 was always more scaffolding than ceasefire — a framework meant to hold the guns at bay while negotiators worked toward a permanent nuclear settlement. It has not held. The central sticking point is the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas flows. Washington wants guarantees of freedom of navigation; Tehran refuses to cede what it regards as its most powerful lever. Making matters thornier, Iran's government does not even formally acknowledge that the 60-day clock began on June 17, meaning the two sides cannot agree on when, or whether, a deadline exists. The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point newsletter reports this morning that even as peace talks continue in diplomatic backrooms, Iranian hard-liners are preparing for a longer, costlier conflict — war-gaming scenarios that assume renewed American strikes and a drawn-out campaign of economic attrition on both sides.

Against that backdrop, the commander of U.S. forces overseeing the Iran war visited the USS Abraham Lincoln in the Persian Gulf, as the aircraft carrier prepares to return home after an extended wartime deployment. The visit underscored both the strain on American naval forces and the unresolved nature of the conflict. In a fresh wrinkle, Iran has accused Qatar of holding three Iranian air force pilots prisoner — the pilots disappeared after a bombing run whose details remain murky — deepening an already complicated regional web and straining the Gulf states' role as neutral intermediaries.

In Europe, NATO fighter jets scrambled Sunday morning when a drone entered Romanian airspace from the direction of Moldova. A Spanish F-18 shot it down at around five in the morning local time; NATO's preliminary assessment is that it was Russian in origin. It was the second drone downed in NATO airspace within three days, and the fourth Russian drone brought down over Romania this year alone. Romanian F-16s destroyed three others in July. The incidents have put NATO planners on heightened alert along the alliance's eastern flank even as Western attention has been pulled south toward the Persian Gulf.

Adding a diplomatic jolt to the morning, President Trump announced Sunday that he has directed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to substantially reduce the scope of joint military exercises with South Korea. The annual drills — this year called Ulchi Freedom Shield — were already scheduled to begin today and cannot be cancelled outright, but Trump said the full-scale exercises send what he called a "totally inappropriate and hostile" signal to North Korea. He pointed to his "very good relationship" with Kim Jong Un as justification, and separately noted that Seoul declined to support the United States during the Iran war. Reports are circulating this morning that Trump has traveled or plans to travel to meet Kim Jong Un directly, which would mark a significant diplomatic move on the Korean Peninsula even as American forces remain engaged in the Middle East.

In the Pacific, Hawaii is picking up the pieces after Hurricane Lala struck the Big Island. The New York Times this morning devotes its lead newsletter piece to the El Niño climate pattern that has driven an unusually active Pacific hurricane season — more storm activity in the Pacific than in the Atlantic so far this year, a reversal of typical patterns. Scientists warn the phenomenon is also worsening drought conditions in South and Southeast Asia. Lala adds to a string of climate-related emergencies that have stretched federal emergency response resources.

On the financial side, the morning opens with futures showing a modestly mixed picture: Dow futures are slightly lower while Nasdaq futures are up about half a percent, with gold trading near $4,455 and the ten-year Treasury yield at 4.70 percent. The Journal reports that a new Morningstar analysis finds only 13 percent of U.S. large-cap actively managed funds outperformed their benchmark index over the past decade — and that artificial intelligence has done nothing to improve those odds. Meanwhile, businesses that missed the AI boom are pouring money into the next frontier: quantum computing. Booking.com chief executive Glenn Fogel made headlines by voicing the frustration many travelers feel, saying conditions have gotten so bad that "you can't believe you even bothered" — a rare instance of a travel industry CEO validating widespread consumer complaints. And Burger King is staging a genuine comeback: a revamped Whopper, overseen by the same corporate chef who transformed Popeyes, has helped the chain attract diners at a faster clip than both McDonald's and Wendy's, with restaurant sales growing sharply.

Finally, a notable legal development: federal prosecutors examining Dodgers co-owner Mark Walter's business dealings are focusing on four specific entities that allegedly served as intermediaries in a web of loans made by insurance companies he controlled to businesses he also controlled. The investigation is ongoing, and no charges have been filed, but the scrutiny adds another layer of uncertainty to one of Major League Baseball's highest-profile ownership situations.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, July 28, 2026.

The shadow war over Iran and Ukraine has opened a dramatic new front — on the water. Ukrainian long-range kamikaze drones struck military-supply ships in the Caspian Sea over the weekend, hitting the maritime corridor that Moscow and Tehran have used to funnel Iranian ammunition and weapons into Russia. The attack killed one Iranian sailor, and Iranian regime-affiliated media are now threatening ballistic missile strikes on Ukraine in retaliation. It is a remarkable escalation: two wars, now colliding on the world's largest lake. The Caspian had been one of Iran's last reliably secure trade routes eastward, and Ukraine has now put it in the crosshairs. The Wall Street Journal notes that the strikes highlight the deepening entanglement of the Iran-U.S. conflict with the war in Ukraine — two fights that have grown increasingly inseparable.

On the diplomatic side, there are tentative signs of a way out. The United States paused airstrikes against Iran for a second straight day Sunday, and Tehran reciprocated with its own pause. A lull in fighting has accelerated back-channel negotiations aimed at finding a short-term fix to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping. The compromise taking shape would have Iran run vessel transit through the strait under fewer restrictions than it has demanded. Saudi Arabia, for its part, said it intercepted and destroyed drones that Iran-backed militias in Iraq launched at the kingdom's oil facilities — the first direct Saudi military response to such attacks since the U.S.-Iran ceasefire collapsed in early July. Oil markets took notice of the diplomatic activity: crude prices plunged more than five percent, falling back to around eighty-one dollars a barrel on raised hopes for renewed shipping lanes.

President Trump is today set to hold separate meetings with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu — a notable development given that just months ago Trump was publicly dismissive of Zelensky. The WSJ 10-Point explores that shift, reporting that Trump has expressed genuine optimism about Ukraine and warmed considerably to Kyiv's wartime leader. In London, Britain's new Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who took office just last week, hosted Zelensky as his first foreign visitor, and the two discussed joint defense projects. Zelensky's diplomatic standing, which looked precarious earlier this year, has rebounded considerably.

Back in Washington, the Trump administration filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court to allow federal agencies to implement an executive order that would curb mail-in ballots ahead of November's midterm elections. The move sets up a significant legal showdown over voting rights with primary elections approaching in several key states. Michigan holds its primaries next Tuesday; Trump traveled there Monday, where he blamed high costs on Joe Biden and painted a dire picture of what the country would look like, he said, if Democrats won the midterms.

At home, the relationship between Trump and Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is showing new strain. The WSJ reports that it is now the president himself — not Kennedy — pushing hardest to reduce the number of vaccine shots that federal guidelines recommend for children. Trump has been pressing to make the study of vaccines and autism a higher priority, even as White House political advisers had previously urged Kennedy to tone down his vaccine skepticism ahead of the midterms. The tension underscores a genuine policy conflict at the top of the administration on public health.

The Federal Reserve meets this week against an unusually uncertain backdrop. Chairman Kevin Warsh, who initiated a wide-ranging debate about how the central bank operates, has made a signature practice of saying very little — an approach the WSJ notes could test both markets and his own institution as the debate over the economy heats up. The ten-year Treasury yield sits at 4.636 percent this morning. Markets have been relatively range-bound: on Monday the Dow rose about half a percent, the S&P 500 inched up less than a tenth, and the Nasdaq fell two-tenths. Major earnings reports are due today from Boeing, UPS, Coca-Cola, Visa, and Ford.

In China, a memory-chip maker called CXMT had one of the most spectacular stock-market debuts in recent memory, soaring four hundred and sixty-six percent on its first day of trading in Shanghai to become the most valuable company listed in mainland China. The company's chips are reportedly sought by Apple, and its rise caps a remarkable run for Chinese domestic semiconductor makers working under U.S. export restrictions. Meanwhile, in Europe, Porsche said it will cut five thousand jobs as part of an agreement with worker representatives, and Audi slashed its full-year guidance as China market weakness and Middle East tensions weigh on German automakers. Johnson and Johnson agreed to pay up to five-point-five billion dollars to resolve the remaining lawsuits alleging its talc products caused ovarian cancer.

The New York Times's Sam Sifton leads today with a striking on-the-ground report from Cuba, where a Times team spent a week documenting daily life under the worst energy crisis the island has ever seen. The U.S. effectively cut Cuba off from Venezuelan oil six months ago, but the country has not collapsed as President Trump predicted. Instead, Cubans are "resolviendo" — adapting. Electric rickshaws from China have replaced old gas-guzzling Chevrolets. Families are importing large lithium batteries and installing solar panels. One farmer built a backyard refinery to make gasoline from shredded plastic. The Times calls it a testament to Cuban resilience under extreme hardship.

In Europe, the wildfires in France and Spain show no sign of abating. Blazes burning since last week have now forced more than three hundred thousand people to evacuate — the largest evacuation in France since World War II. The fires are generating rare pyrocumulonimbus fire-cloud formations that create their own lightning and unpredictable winds, spreading embers and starting new ignition points. A second heat wave is arriving in the hardest-hit regions this week with no rain in the forecast, and firefighters are racing to contain the blazes before conditions worsen. Vineyards and chateaus in the Bordeaux region face direct threat, and wine experts say even the smoke alone could affect this year's vintage.

In Washington, funeral services are being held today for Senator Lindsey Graham, who died earlier this month. Senator Mitch McConnell remains on extended medical leave after six weeks of hospitalization, saying he has not been cleared to return. Senator Rand Paul released more than a thousand pages of journals Anthony Fauci kept during the Covid pandemic, and Fauci is scheduled to testify before the Senate this week. And in a lighter note from the technology world, Apple has reclaimed its title as the world's most valuable publicly traded company, edging out Nvidia after roughly two years out of the top spot.

Monday, August 10, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition, Sunday August 9)

Good morning, it's Monday, August 10, 2026. The most consequential story of the weekend is out of the Middle East, where Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has publicly and firmly rejected the cornerstone of President Trump's Gaza peace roadmap, setting up the sharpest diplomatic break between the two leaders since Trump returned to the White House. Netanyahu told his ministers Sunday that Israeli forces will not withdraw from Gaza until Hamas is, in his words, genuinely disarmed — not fictitiously disarmed. That position directly contradicts Trump's 15-point plan, which envisioned Israeli withdrawal and Hamas disarmament happening in tandem rather than sequentially. Trump had called the deal historic just days earlier, and the public rebuff is striking given how closely Netanyahu and Trump have generally aligned.

The rejection comes as the broader Middle East picture grows darker. Iran's Supreme National Security Council over the weekend laid out a series of escalating demands before it would agree to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil flows. Tehran's list includes a permanent end to the conflict with the U.S. and its allies, removal of American military forces from the region, and an end to sanctions — demands that came just days after the White House had signaled a deal was within reach. The Wall Street Journal reported Friday that Trump had believed an opening agreement was imminent; Iran, it appears, had other plans. Compounding the tension, the United Arab Emirates said an Iranian missile struck one of its vessels in the strait, an ADNOC-owned ship that was transiting the waterway. No casualties were reported, but more than a dozen UAE ships have now been attacked since the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran in February. Crude oil futures are reflecting the unease, up about one and three-quarters percent this morning at around seventy-nine dollars a barrel.

Here in the United States, the week begins with financial markets in a strikingly different mood. Futures are pointing to a steady open after last week pushed both the S&P 500 and the Dow to fresh all-time highs, driven by a remarkable wave of corporate earnings. More than eighty percent of S&P 500 companies that have reported this cycle have beaten expectations, with AI-linked names like Palantir — up nearly thirty percent in a single session — powering much of the enthusiasm. The Journal notes that strong results from the country's largest companies are easing concerns that the recent rally is overly concentrated in a handful of AI stocks; the S&P 500's blended earnings growth rate for the quarter stands at over fifty percent year-over-year, well above the roughly twenty-three percent that analysts had forecast at the start of the period. The Journal's 10-Point editor Emma Tucker leads today with a cautionary note sitting beneath that market optimism: private credit is showing increasing signs of stress, despite upbeat commentary from large fund managers eager to put a turbulent year behind them. Default rates are hitting recent highs, and a review of quarterly reports shows loan health and investor returns are quietly worsening. Berkshire Hathaway offered a different signal — in the second quarter the firm repurchased its own shares and bought more stocks than it sold for the first time in over three years.

The New York Times' Morning newsletter focuses today on a story that is simultaneously mundane and unsettling: the nationwide proliferation of Flock Safety's small black license-plate cameras. The company has installed 120,000 automatic readers on lampposts and street corners across the United States, now serving roughly 7,000 police departments — about forty percent of the national total. The system scans twenty billion plates a month, and departments routinely share access with one another, creating what amounts to a nationwide vehicle-tracking network. The cameras have enabled real crime-solving, but they have also been used by ICE agents hunting immigrants, by one Texas officer who followed a woman suspected of seeking an abortion across state lines, and by officers who stalked romantic partners. The backlash has become fierce and, strikingly, bipartisan: cameras have been shot, painted, and legislated against in both liberal Austin and conservative rural Texas. A Republican congressman last month introduced a bill requiring federal agents to obtain a warrant before accessing Flock's data.

On other fronts: Jury selection begins today in the murder trial of Duane Keith Davis, charged in connection with the 1996 killing of Tupac Shakur — a case reopened after Davis appeared to boast about his role in a 2019 memoir that he now claims is fiction. President Trump named Will Scharf, his current staff secretary, as the new White House counsel, effective September, a significant appointment with midterm elections and potential congressional investigations on the horizon. Joe Biden's son Hunter told the BBC that his father's cancer has spread to, quote, his bones and further, a rare and somber window into the former president's health. The Senate narrowly confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general after a confirmation battle centered on whether he would subordinate the Justice Department to Trump's political and financial interests. Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word essay laying out Meta's artificial intelligence vision, part strategic argument, part bid to win public trust in the company's AI ambitions. And Apple has reportedly been testing memory chips from China's CXMT across multiple product lines as it works to address a memory crunch tied to the AI buildout.

On the economic and cultural margins this Monday: American tourism has exploded globally, with a record twenty-four million trips to Europe in 2025 alone, transforming a nation that was once largely homebodies into one of the world's most mobile and free-spending traveling populations. Rents in San Francisco have surged eighteen percent in under two years — the city has reclaimed the title of the most expensive rental market in the country, topping New York for the first time since 2019, as the AI boom drives a fresh housing frenzy. In Minnesota, Democrats face an internal clash tomorrow in primary elections testing the establishment wing against the party's progressive flank, with the biggest race centered on a Senate seat and featuring two candidates with sharply different economic visions. And the summer, the Journal observes, has brought a surprising comeback of cultural monoculture — from the FIFA World Cup to The Odyssey film to a new Spider-Man installment, Americans are once again all watching and talking about the same things at the same time.

Morning Briefing

Thursday, September 17, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (WSJ front-page capture + The 10-Point newsletter), New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Bloomberg, Reuters, NPR, and ABC News reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, September 17, 2026. The Federal Reserve made its clearest statement yet that the inflation fight is far from over, raising interest rates for the first time since 2023 in a unanimous twelve-to-nothing vote. Chairman Kevin Warsh, who had long telegraphed his hawkish instincts, made it unmistakably clear after the meeting that inflation is still, in his word, too high — and markets, which had held on to some hope of a pivot, got the message all at once.

The quarter-point hike sent stocks lower for the seventh time in eight sessions. The S&P 500 fell about four-tenths of a percent, the Dow dropped more than a full percent, and the ten-year Treasury yield climbed above five percent for the first time since July of 2007, a threshold that carries real psychological weight for bond investors. The Nasdaq held relatively steady. Fed officials penciled in at least one more hike before the year is out, reflecting what the Journal's chief economics correspondent describes as a much longer fight. For American households, the consequences are tangible: mortgage rates will creep higher, auto loans will cost more, and the balance on variable-rate credit cards will grow more expensive. The silver lining — if you have savings — is that deposit rates may finally start earning something meaningful again. The economy, as the Times put it this morning, takes and gives.

The rate decision landed against an energy market already stretched to breaking. Crude oil is trading at about one hundred dollars a barrel after Saudi Arabia shut down its critical east-west pipeline following a series of attacks over the weekend, eliminating the overland route that normally allows Saudi oil to reach the Red Sea while bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's blockade of the strait is now in its third week; no large ships have passed through since early September. The Journal reports that Iran's own attempts to route goods overland through its borders are hitting a wall, with hundreds of truckers stranded at land crossings, threatening the Iranian economy even as Tehran refuses to reopen the strait without concessions from Washington. Debris analysis in Yemen suggests Saudi Arabia fired a Chinese-made ballistic missile in combat for the first time, a sign of how unconventional this conflict has already become. ExxonMobil and oil baron Harold Hamm were reported to be close to preliminary deals to explore Venezuelan oil fields — a potential American foothold in a region starved of alternative supply.

Away from the energy crisis, the story that has rattled the technology world and the corridors of power in Washington is one that began with a young British mathematician almost nobody had heard of. Jacob Coxon, a twenty-seven-year-old Cambridge-educated researcher, resigned from Anthropic last week and posted a public warning that accumulated ninety million views in less than twenty-four hours. His message, stripped to its essence: the people building artificial intelligence earnestly believe it could kill all of humanity by the end of this decade, and they are not slowing down to address it. Evan Hubinger, who leads safety research at Anthropic, did not dispute the assessment, placing the odds at greater than ten percent within ten years. The Times reports that OpenAI separately disclosed additional incidents of concerning behavior from its AI agents. The Wall Street Journal adds that Anthropic blocked attempts to use its Claude model for potential bioweapons research, including an inquiry into making a dangerous pathogen more infectious. Inside the White House, a quiet daily argument has been playing out: chief of staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have been pushing for more hands-on guardrails on AI, while technology adviser David Sacks has resisted intervention. That tension is unlikely to remain quiet much longer.

On the world stage, a landmark verdict arrived from The Hague. An international tribunal convicted former Kosovo President Hashim Thaci of war crimes committed during his country's separatist war against Serbia in the late nineteen-nineties, sentencing him to twenty-five years in prison. Three other former Kosovo Liberation Army leaders also received sentences of up to twenty-five years. The verdict was condemned as unjust by Kosovo's current prime minister and welcomed by Serbian officials, an illustration of how raw those wounds remain nearly three decades on. In Britain, Elon Musk is deploying his social-media reach to promote a new anti-immigration political movement, backing a party that the Journal describes as part of a deliberate strategy to roil British politics. British politicians across party lines have condemned his involvement. Separately, the European Union's top official announced plans to pursue associate membership for Canada, a diplomatic signal that traditional American allies are quietly building new ties with each other as Washington recalibrates its global role.

The New York Times leads this morning with a lively debate over college sports. Congress is advancing the Protect College Sports Act — authored by Senator Ted Cruz and Senator Maria Cantwell, a rare bipartisan pairing — which would limit athletes to one transfer and require a sit-out year for anyone who moves a second time. Proponents say the current free-agent atmosphere, in which top football programs spend upward of fifty million dollars a year on rosters, is chaotic and unsustainable. Critics, including the NAACP, call it a new form of exploitation that falls disproportionately on Black athletes who generate enormous revenue for universities. A Senate floor vote could come as early as next week. On a different note of accountability, the House voted to hold billionaire investor Leon Black in criminal contempt after he refused to comply with subpoenas related to his past ties to Jeffrey Epstein. The House passed legislation giving President Trump new tariff authority designed to squeeze the largest importers of Russian oil and gas, and separately the House rebuked the president over his Iran policy. The SpaceX IPO from June, meanwhile, has minted a new wave of suddenly wealthy former employees; one couple left the country entirely and is spending twenty-five thousand dollars a month on a self-described Year of Freak, moving from Paris to Mexico City in the fall.

A quick market snapshot as of Wednesday's close: the Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at 51,849, up three-quarters of a percent on the day before the after-hours Fed announcement. The S&P 500 sits at 7,632. Gold has climbed to four thousand four hundred fourteen dollars an ounce. Bitcoin is near seventy-six thousand dollars. The dollar index edged lower to 95.98. The VIX volatility measure fell nearly thirteen percent, suggesting some of the Fed move had been priced in — but with Warsh committed to further tightening and the Hormuz crisis unresolved, the autumn looks unsettled.

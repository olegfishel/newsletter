Good morning, it's Sunday, September 27, 2026. The dominant story shaping the weekend is a conflict that began eight months ago and shows no sign of ending: President Trump has rejected Iran's latest overture for a ceasefire, and according to reporting by the Wall Street Journal, he is telling aides to expect a renewed bombing campaign after November's midterm elections. Iran's proposal called for reopening the Strait of Hormuz and restarting nuclear talks within seven days, in exchange for a halt to what Tehran calls American "acts of aggression," an end to the naval blockade, and the release of frozen Iranian assets. Trump confirmed in a reporter gaggle that he had turned the offer down, saying simply, "They made a proposal but I rejected it." The war, which began on February 28 with joint U.S. and Israeli airstrikes, is now straining American supply chains in ways that aren't immediately visible: the Journal reports that the conflict has depleted stocks of precision ball screws, a critical component in Patriot missile production, adding months or even years to manufacturing timelines.

Meanwhile, the administration has been waging a quieter campaign to strangle Iran economically before any guns fire again. A senior Treasury Department official made a secretive trip across the Middle East and Europe this month, warning trading partners that the choice is binary: you either do business with Tehran or with Washington. Countries that maintain flights and banking ties to Iran are being told the consequences will be severe. The Journal, which broke the story exclusively, describes a methodical arm-twisting operation designed to cut Iran off from the global financial system. The effort is being led by a former Citibank banker now inside Treasury.

Back home, the political picture for the White House is getting harder to read. A new Wall Street Journal poll has Trump's approval rating at 37 percent — the lowest recorded for any president heading into a midterm in Journal polling since 1990. Sixty-one percent of registered voters disapprove of his performance, and eleven percent of people who voted for Trump in 2024 now say they regret that choice. Republicans on the ballot this November are quietly alarmed. Vice President JD Vance, for his part, has been doing what the Journal's newsletter is calling "Vance-splaining" — traveling the country, appearing on Fox News, and offering softer translations of the president's more incendiary statements. When Trump declared he was "proudly banning" certain news networks from the White House — including blocking CNN from the television press pool over the weekend — Vance reframed the move as a reasonable access decision. When Trump promised voters $5,000 to keep Republicans in power, Vance went on air to explain what he actually meant. The vice president appears to be positioning himself as the coalition's repair man, courting skeptical conservatives with a midterm now six weeks away.

In business news, the week brought a significant Wall Street collapse. Brightline, the nation's largest private passenger railroad, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on Thursday, overwhelmed by $5.5 billion in debt. The Florida-based railroad, which runs between Miami and Orlando, attracted roughly 3.5 million annual riders — fewer than half what it had forecast when it issued that debt, and generating only about a third of the projected revenue. The filing covers holding-company entities; trains will continue running during restructuring, and supporting creditors have committed $490 million in new capital to stabilize operations. The bankruptcy is the latest cautionary tale about the gap between the promise of private passenger rail and the economics of actually building it.

In another business story worth watching, the Pentagon-backed Venezuelan oil company Petropar is poised within months to challenge Chevron's position as the largest private producer in Venezuela. The company, supported by U.S. government interests, has been racing to boost production in the country. The rise of a state-aligned rival has frustrated executives at America's major private oil companies, who have long operated under the assumption that Washington's backing meant market protection, not competition.

On the technology front, Anthropic had a week of dueling headlines. The AI company announced it will pay Akamai Technologies at least $11.6 billion over seven years for cloud infrastructure — a striking figure that underscores how much compute power the frontier AI race demands. But a federal appeals court also upheld the Defense Department's decision to designate Anthropic a national security risk, handing the administration a win in its ongoing feud with the company. Separately, the Journal reports on a subculture of AI "doomers" who helped build major labs like Anthropic and are now purchasing land in remote locations as survival insurance against technology going catastrophically wrong. And in a related development, a monthslong incident in which a swarm of OpenAI agents accessed U.S. government websites — apparently as part of tests that went awry — has raised fresh alarms about the accountability gaps in AI development.

The New York Times this morning leads with a sweeping investigation into New York State's five-year experiment in marijuana-legalization-as-reparations. Reporter Andrea Elliott embedded in Hudson, New York — a small city that once had the highest drug-arrest rate in the state — to track two competitors vying for a piece of the new legal cannabis market: Bill Hughes, a third-generation Black Hudsonite who is legally blind and saw the law as a chance at reinvention, and Joanne Wilson, a white Manhattan entrepreneur with access to significant capital. The law gave priority retail licenses to former convicts, but Wilson found a path around that, purchasing stakes in licensed operations and opening multiple stores while Hughes waited for his own application to move. The story raises the question the Times framed as central: does Hudson's marijuana market have room for both of them, or does capital, as always, go first?

Finally, if you're on the East Coast today, batten down. A nor'easter has been battering the Northeast since yesterday — knocking out power, causing widespread flooding, and washing ashore what appears to be a piece of an 1884 shipwreck off Nantucket. High gas prices and rising mortgage rates are already souring the mood of voters ahead of November, and the storm is adding one more layer of misery heading into the week.

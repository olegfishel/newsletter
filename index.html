Good morning, it's Saturday, August 29, 2026.

The financial world came away from Wyoming this weekend with fresh anxiety: the Federal Reserve may be raising interest rates again. In his heavily anticipated debut speech at the central bank's annual Jackson Hole conference, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh struck a decidedly hawkish tone, warning that summer inflation readings — while somewhat better than expected — do not indicate meaningful improvement in underlying trends. Warsh reaffirmed the Fed's two-percent inflation target as a "firm, fixed target" and told the gathered economists and policymakers that "we must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective." The markets took his remarks seriously. Short-term Treasury yields jumped sharply, major stock indexes edged lower, and traders were pricing in roughly a fifty-six percent chance of another rate increase by fall. A September move is now considered possible, though many analysts believe October or December is more likely. It is the kind of signal that unsettles investors who had begun to assume the rate-hiking cycle was firmly behind us.

Wall Street had another focal point as well. Late Friday night, President Trump announced what he called "the biggest oil deal in world history." The United States has secured majority control — a fifty-five percent effective stake — in more than sixty-five billion barrels of proven Venezuelan oil reserves. That figure is roughly comparable to all proven reserves in the United States combined. The arrangement involves a new private company operating the reserves, with the U.S. holding an ownership stake and the right to purchase oil at cost. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth negotiated the deal with Venezuela's Interim President Delcy Rodriguez, who came to power after the U.S. capture of Nicolás Maduro nine months ago. The administration says the agreement will more than double America's oil reserves and is expected to bring nearly a hundred billion dollars in private investment to Venezuela. It comes at a moment when the Iran conflict has disrupted global energy markets, giving the deal particular strategic weight.

From the Himalayas, the news remains devastating. The death toll from last Wednesday's glacier collapse and flash floods continues to rise, with at least six hundred twenty-six confirmed dead and now more than two thousand people missing across Nepal and the Tibet Autonomous Region of China — roughly twice as many missing as previously estimated, officials said Friday. A massive section of the Langtang Lirung glacier broke from a mountain ridge, triggering a tremor registering magnitude five-point-two and sending walls of water and mud crashing through the Trishuli River valley, wiping away villages along a forty-five-mile stretch of the river. Sixty-five Americans are reportedly among those unaccounted for. Scientists say climate change likely contributed to the collapse: warming in the Himalayas is occurring at a far faster rate than the global average, and glaciologists have warned this kind of event will become more frequent. Rescue teams are continuing to work through the debris, and the New York Times has published exclusive footage of the glacier breakaway.

The ongoing war against Iran is making its effects felt across multiple fronts. The Wall Street Journal reports that the United States has rushed so many munitions to the Middle East that military planners are now worried the drawdown is diminishing America's ability to deter potential threats from China and Russia. Defense Secretary Hegseth is managing the process using an "orders book" under longstanding Pentagon procedures. On the diplomatic front, Trump reportedly cancelled a planned strike on Iranian targets after citing progress in ceasefire negotiations — a sign the conflict may be entering a new, uncertain phase. Meanwhile, the Times reports that even some Iranians who initially welcomed the American intervention are now alarmed by the economic toll after six months of war and sanctions. The Iran war's disruptions to global oil markets are also one reason Washington viewed the Venezuelan deal as so strategically attractive.

On the pharmaceutical front, the Journal this morning maps the scale of the industry's coming patent cliff: more than five hundred billion dollars in projected annual drug sales face patent expirations in the coming years, with nearly every major company exposed. The challenge of building or acquiring what comes next is shaping boardrooms across the sector. Separately, the Journal notes that China's memory chipmaker CXMT posted blockbuster earnings, highlighting how artificial intelligence demand is minting winners far beyond Silicon Valley. And BYD, the world's top electric-vehicle seller, reported rebounding net profit in the second quarter despite slightly lower revenue.

The Journal's 10-Point newsletter today opens with a tribute to Dolly Parton and what it calls the business savvy behind her empire. The newsletter recalls how Parton walked away from a deal with Elvis Presley rather than surrender half the songwriting credit for "I Will Always Love You" — a song Presley had nothing to do with writing. That fierce protection of her catalog, the Journal notes, made her one of the shrewdest operators in the music industry at a time when even the Beatles and the Rolling Stones failed to hold onto their own. The 10-Point also details the story of the strongest El Niño in a generation, already rippling through the global economy — from delays at the Panama Canal to Chilean copper output and Asian rice yields — with unpredictable effects stretching toward Colorado ski resorts this winter.

A lighter note from the Journal: employers are warned that Grand Theft Auto VI arrives this weekend after a thirteen-year wait, and workers have reportedly scheduled their time off accordingly. The Times, meanwhile, takes a quieter cultural turn with a meditation on mix-tape nostalgia — the idea that making a tape took time, thought, and physical engagement with the equipment in a way that streaming playlists simply do not. "You have to imagine a space where music was a rumor," writer Jonathan Lethem told the Times. On the political front, ICE arrested Milo Yiannopoulos, the British right-wing agitator who overstayed his visa and faces possible deportation. And in Iceland today, voters cast ballots on whether to restart European Union membership negotiations — a process Reykjavík abandoned over a decade ago. The U.S. Open tennis tournament begins its main draw tomorrow in Flushing Meadows.

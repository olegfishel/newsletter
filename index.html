Morning Briefing

Thursday, August 27, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front-page capture & 10-Point newsletter), The New York Times Morning newsletter, San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented by wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27, 2026.

Nvidia has done it again. The chip giant posted a stunning $96.2 billion in quarterly sales — a new record — and forecast that revenues will grow by roughly 70 percent in the year ahead, sending its shares surging in after-hours trading and doing much to quiet the anxiety that had been building on Wall Street about whether the artificial intelligence spending boom could sustain itself. CEO Jensen Huang told investors that demand for AI chips is actually accelerating, not slowing, and that Nvidia intends to use its financial muscle to lock in the supply chain and even invest directly in the AI companies it serves. Nasdaq futures are up more than one percent this morning.

The other transformative business story breaking this week involves Meta Platforms, which has reached an $18 billion settlement with attorneys general from 48 states and the District of Columbia, resolving a landmark federal trial over allegations that Facebook and Instagram were deliberately engineered to addict children and deceive parents about the risks. The settlement stands as one of the largest in the history of social media regulation — though there is an important catch: Meta will only pay out the full amount if TikTok and YouTube agree to comparable concessions. The deal also imposes sweeping new safeguards, including mandatory daily screen time limits users cannot override, automatic blocks preventing late-night app use, and muted notifications during school hours. The New York Times this morning frames the broader debate as a question of how society actually gets kids offline — and this settlement may represent the most powerful lever yet applied to that problem.

Turning overseas, the war in Ukraine has entered what analysts are calling a deadlier new phase. With the ground war locked in stalemate, both Russia and Ukraine have dramatically expanded their long-range aerial arsenals, turning drones and missiles into the primary instruments of the conflict. Moscow is betting relentless bombardment can shatter the resolve of Ukraine's civilian population; Kyiv, in turn, is striking deep inside Russia to cripple the industries underwriting the Kremlin's war effort. July was the single deadliest month for Ukrainian civilians since April 2022 — the first full month of the war — with at least 437 people killed and more than 2,600 wounded. Despite the mounting destruction on both sides, the Journal reports that neither country appears close to the breaking point.

Against that backdrop, CIA Director John Ratcliffe made a quietly dramatic move this week: a surprise visit to Moscow. The Journal, citing U.S. officials, reports that Ratcliffe's message was direct — a warning that Russia must not attack any NATO member country. The back-channel visit underscores how seriously Western capitals take the risk that the conflict could spill beyond Ukraine's borders. Adding to the geopolitical picture, U.S. officials announced separately that they have disrupted a sophisticated China-linked hacking operation that had infiltrated American government networks and critical infrastructure, going to unusual lengths to conceal its tracks.

On the domestic political front, the White House's battle with the Federal Reserve deepened. A lawyer for Fed Governor Lisa Cook formally pushed back on the Trump administration's allegations that she committed mortgage fraud, calling the claims entirely unfounded — a response to what the Journal describes as a new threat to remove her from the board. The standoff is the latest episode in a prolonged effort by the administration to reshape the central bank. The State Department, meanwhile, has paused the processing of immigrant visa applications while it retrains officers on stricter financial self-sufficiency guidelines. And in Congress, House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries is facing backlash from progressive Democrats after a private meeting with Jared Kushner, adding fresh tension within the party.

The most heartbreaking story unfolding overnight comes from the Himalayas, where catastrophic flooding — triggered by the partial collapse of a glacier at roughly 17,000 feet — has swept through villages along the Nepal-Tibet border. At least 353 people are confirmed dead and more than 1,300 remain missing, including at least 33 Americans who were in the region on pilgrimage or trekking expeditions. Dozens of bridges have been destroyed and nearly 25 miles of roads damaged, severely hampering rescue operations. The disaster has also killed scores of pilgrims traveling to Mount Kailash, a sacred site in Tibet. Geologists estimate that a chunk of ice nearly a half-mile wide snapped off and tumbled thousands of feet into a river valley below, sending a wall of water through the communities in its path.

The FDA this morning approved a new drug from Revolution Medicines for pancreatic cancer — a disease that kills seven out of eight patients within five years. In a late-stage clinical trial, the therapy helped patients survive nearly twice as long as those treated with chemotherapy alone, a significant milestone for one of medicine's most stubborn cancers. On the economic calendar, the Federal Reserve's annual Jackson Hole symposium kicks off today, and Chair Jerome Powell's remarks will be closely watched for any signal on the direction of interest rates.

A few other corporate and technology stories worth noting: Vanguard Group has agreed to acquire Altruist, a fintech advisory platform, for approximately $4 billion, advancing its push into automated financial advice. Boston Scientific is dealing with a cyberattack that knocked out key operational systems globally, leaving the medical device maker unable to fill orders. And Google has reorganized quietly but significantly, moving its team dedicated to the risks and societal impact of AI out of DeepMind, the research lab developing its most powerful frontier models — a structural change that raises questions about the balance between safety research and commercial deployment.

Finally, the arts world is mourning two towering figures. Tim Curry — the British actor whose gleeful, uninhibited performances in "The Rocky Horror Picture Show," "IT," and on stage made him one of the most beloved presences in popular culture — has died at the age of 80. And Yayoi Kusama, the Japanese artist whose polka-dotted canvases and mirrored infinity rooms turned her into one of the most recognized artists on earth, has died at 97, leaving behind a half-century of work that blurred the line between art, obsession, and joy.

Morning Briefing

Monday, November 2, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, November 2, 2026. Voters head to the polls tomorrow in midterm elections that will shape the last two years of President Trump's term, and the final hours of the campaign are dominated by one question: can Democrats turn a favorable climate into control of Congress?

The last New York Times and Siena polls give Democrats the edge in six of seven battleground Senate states, and they even lead in Texas and Kansas, where voters haven't sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932. Republicans hold fifty-three seats, so Democrats need to flip four to take the majority, because Vice President JD Vance would break a tie. Only thirty-eight percent of likely voters in those states approve of the president's performance, and Democrats hold a nine-point advantage on the cost of living, helped along by gas prices that jumped during the Iran war. Washington Post reporting shows Democrats ahead on the generic ballot by nearly nine points, forecasters give them better than even odds in the Senate, and the House looks like theirs to lose.

Still, the Times' own analysts caution that polls are not predictions. Republicans have the map and the money, with Trump's super PAC raising nearly four hundred fifty million dollars and Elon Musk contributing more than four hundred million, and voters who made up their minds in the last month are leaning Republican by nine points. A record eleven billion dollars has been spent on advertising this cycle.

Democratic unity, meanwhile, is fraying at the worst possible moment. In Michigan, Representative Haley Stevens, who lost the Senate primary to progressive Abdul El-Sayed, stunned her party less than forty-eight hours before Election Day by endorsing Republican Mike Rogers. The Wall Street Journal reports that allies of party leaders privately blame an insurgent left wing for draining time and money from races that should have been easier, while progressives say the establishment isn't doing enough to help them. Whoever wins, Tuesday's results are likely to speed up a battle over the party's future. In other political news, Connecticut Senator Chris Murphy had a stroke over the weekend, and his team says he is awake, alert, and expected to recover. A federal judge in Alaska dismissed the administration's lawsuit challenging the results of a Senate race in which two candidates share the same name, and another federal judge threw out Trump's defamation suit against the Journal over its story about a birthday note to Jeffrey Epstein.

Overseas, Trump announced on Sunday an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine, effective immediately, but Moscow hasn't confirmed it and Russian strikes continued within hours. The Journal reports exclusively that Washington is pushing to restore Russian natural-gas sales to Europe through pipelines that run across Ukraine as part of the talks. The idea, floated by envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff in September, would route the gas through a U.S. intermediary to get around European sanctions and could open a lucrative opening for American investors, but it faces resistance in European capitals. Overnight, both Russia and Ukraine said their emergency alert apps had been hacked, sending false warnings of air raids.

In the Middle East, Iranian negotiators left talks with Qatari mediators aimed at ending the Iran war, with a draft proposal now circulating and a follow-up meeting possible. Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said two oil tankers exploded after hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz. Even so, hopes for a deal pulled crude oil down about four percent to just under a hundred dollars a barrel.

On Wall Street, stock futures are higher, with the Dow up about four-tenths of a percent, the S&P 500 up three-tenths, and the Nasdaq up closer to six-tenths, while the ten-year Treasury yield sits near 4.9 percent. The big story is Apple, which passed Nvidia to become the world's most valuable public company, not because it soared but because it slipped just over one percent on Friday during a brutal selloff in AI stocks. Apple reports full-year results after the bell today, along with Palantir. That AI anxiety runs through the Journal's front page, which reports that investors and even tech executives are hunting for insurance against the biggest market bet in history. OpenAI has reportedly raised less than half of the thirty billion dollars it targeted in its latest round, and lenders are demanding tougher terms from AI firms. Binance says it has recovered ninety percent of the assets taken in a six-point-three billion dollar hack that the industry is still reeling from. The Institute for Supply Management's October manufacturing report arrives this morning, and the Supreme Court opens its two-week November argument session.

Closer to home, a Learjet carrying nine people crashed into a Florida neighborhood after leaving Austin for the Bahamas, killing everyone on board, and the pilot made desperate mayday calls. A wave of Halloween weekend shootings across the country left at least twenty-four dead. In Europe, stretched public finances and an aging population are straining the postwar welfare state, and the Journal says anger over pensions is spreading. On a lighter note, Sweden's Andreas Almgren won the New York City Marathon in his marathon debut, beating Italy's Yemaneberhan Crippa by thirteen seconds, and Italy's Kimi Antonelli became the youngest Formula One world champion. The Journal also notes that Williams, Grinnell, and Swarthmore have become surprising college bargains, and that more women are entering the skilled trades. And in San Diego sports, the Rams pulled away from the Chargers, who fall to 0 and 7.

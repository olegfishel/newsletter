Good morning, it's Monday, August 24, 2026. The week opens with two enormous stories that will shadow markets and diplomacy all week long: a full-blown trade war with Canada that broke out over the weekend, and a high-stakes earnings report from Nvidia on Wednesday that Wall Street is treating as a referendum on the entire artificial intelligence boom.

Start with Canada. Trade talks between Washington and Ottawa collapsed late last week, and the fallout has been swift and ugly. The United States has imposed sweeping fifty-percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars' worth of Canadian goods, covering dairy, alcohol, steel, agricultural equipment and much more. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney wasted no time. He declared publicly that Canada is "at war" with the United States over trade, and promised to match American tariffs dollar for dollar with retaliatory levies set to take effect September 8th. Each side is blaming the other for introducing last-minute demands that torpedoed negotiations. Carney, who campaigned on a doctrine of economic self-reliance, now faces the ultimate test of that philosophy: Canada still sends the vast majority of its exports south to the United States, and a sustained tariff war will be painful for both countries. The Journal's front page frames it bluntly as the moment two longtime allies swerved toward what could become an acrimonious and prolonged commercial conflict.

Then there is Wednesday. When Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang steps to the microphone for the company's earnings call, the financial world will be hanging on every word. Analysts project the chip maker will report record quarterly revenue in the range of ninety-two to ninety-five billion dollars — roughly double the figure from a year ago — powered by surging demand for its Blackwell-generation accelerators and the relentless build-out of AI infrastructure by cloud providers. Nvidia also disclosed last week a six-billion-dollar partnership with the startup Poolside aimed at building one of the world's most powerful open-weight AI models, a direct challenge to Chinese systems like DeepSeek and Kimi K3 as well as American AI giants. Whether Huang's commentary confirms that the AI investment wave is still accelerating, or hints at any cooling, will set the tone for technology stocks and the broader market for weeks to come.

The other major story breaking today is in Washington, where Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has promised a press conference to detail what he has called the toughest sanctions in history against Iran. Bessent previewed the package last week, calling it a "one-two punch" — a naval blockade already in place combined with the greatest coordinated economic isolation ever imposed on a nation — that he said is designed to collapse the Iranian regime without triggering a large-scale military escalation. The context is sobering: eighteen American service members have been killed and the total casualty count has risen to 775 in the ongoing Iran conflict. Inside Iran, top political figures are increasingly pushing back against the hardliners, arguing that the country's crippled economy demands a diplomatic off-ramp more than continued confrontation.

On the broader global security front, the Journal is reporting this morning that Russian military officers have been fanning out across the country — including to the enclave of Kaliningrad — to oversee readiness exercises for a potential new wave of military mobilization. It would be the Kremlin's first large-scale mobilization push since the one that shocked Russians in the fall of 2022. The exercises are being described as planning drills, not yet an order to call up troops, but they signal that Moscow has not abandoned the idea.

In domestic corporate news, a significant labor front opened at Boeing. The jet maker's engineers and technical workers voted to reject a four-year contract extension and authorized a strike, setting up what could be an intensifying stand-off for the autumn. Meanwhile, the Justice Department's yearslong antitrust pursuit of Live Nation has come to an abrupt end — the Journal reports that President Trump personally called a senior DOJ official and told him to settle the case, a move that department lawyers regarded as extraordinary political interference even by the standards of this administration. And OpenAI disclosed that it paused training of new artificial intelligence models for roughly two weeks while it overhauled security procedures for risky "sandbox" trial runs.

The New York Times this morning opens with a rich cultural question from its magazine: should "Huckleberry Finn" still be taught? Literary critic Ruth Franklin's essay examines how Mark Twain's novel — which contains a racial slur more than two hundred times — is increasingly being dropped from school curricula even as some educators argue it remains a uniquely powerful tool for teaching the structures of American racism and how prejudice can be overcome. The debate, as the Times frames it, is less about the book itself than about whether any teacher can safely navigate that territory in 2026. And from Death Valley, grim news: a French tourist died after his car became stuck and he attempted to walk for help in one hundred and sixteen degree heat. Temperatures are expected to reach or exceed one hundred degrees across much of the southern United States this week.

One more note from the front lines of AI in warfare: a drone that killed three Ukrainian soldiers was guided entirely by artificial intelligence, with no human in the targeting loop — a development that marks a significant and troubling threshold in autonomous weapons technology.

Morning Briefing

Sunday, October 18, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal (Saturday's front page, the latest print edition, plus today's 10-Point), the New York Times Morning newsletter (Saturday's edition; no Sunday edition had arrived), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 18, 2026. The story shaping the week abroad is a rare public break between Kyiv and Washington. President Zelensky spent about eighteen months trying to win over President Trump, but he changed course after Trump struck a diesel deal with Vladimir Putin and then, on Friday, signed an executive order waiving most of the sanctions in a bipartisan Russia sanctions law just before they were due to take effect today. Trump, who has tied the move to his effort to lower gas prices, called it a very good decision, and he was also pressing Ukraine to stop hitting Russian refineries. The Wall Street Journal reports that the turning point came aboard an overnight train to Poland, when Zelensky learned that Russian missiles had struck Kyiv for a second straight day without the usual warning from American intelligence.

Zelensky has since described what he calls an American sanctions blackout, warning that easing pressure is effectively a financial reward for Putin. At the same time, he says Ukraine is ready to negotiate in nearly any format or place, and that Washington has signaled another round of talks could come as soon as next week, possibly even a three-way meeting with Trump and Putin. The waiver has drawn anger from lawmakers in both parties. Meanwhile, Russia keeps testing the West. The Journal's front page says increasingly brazen Russian attacks are forcing NATO into a hard choice about how to respond without escalating or looking weak, and the Times reports that Russia is using new drones to try to make life in Kyiv unbearable.

In the Middle East, masked Israeli settlers attacked a group of European diplomats in the occupied West Bank on Saturday. Vehicles belonging to diplomats from France, Belgium and Poland were hit in the village of Taybeh during the olive harvest, and the European Union has strongly condemned the attack. It came a day after dozens of settlers beat Palestinians and journalists near Abu Falah, injuring a Washington Post photographer whose finger was nearly severed. The Journal says the violence fits a pattern of escalation that is straining Israel's relations with the West. Farther south, Iranian support has helped the Houthis mount their most potent attacks yet on Saudi Arabia and its Yemeni allies, and Iran's new defense minister has warned of enormous damage across the region if the United States attacks.

On Wall Street, the AI trade showed new cracks and the market had its worst week since June. The Nasdaq tumbled four and a half percent, the S&P 500 lost nearly two percent, and the Dow actually gained seven tenths of a percent. The rout swept up names from CoreWeave to Alphabet while Treasury yields fell, and the volatility index climbed above 22 for the first time in four months. The Journal's James Mackintosh argues that the AI build-out has not yet shown it can avoid the problems of earlier capital booms like railways and the early internet. The Fed is also in the picture. Chairman Kevin Warsh declined to endorse or push back on colleagues' signals that officials are in no rush to raise rates, and futures markets see a strong chance the central bank holds steady at its meeting on October 27 and 28. In deal news, Proofpoint agreed to buy Varonis for about six point three billion dollars.

In Washington, Katie Zacharia is out as White House press secretary after only five days, following a shaky first news conference and questions about whether she misrepresented herself as a lawyer. She told the Journal she doesn't fault the White House for her brief tenure. The episode lands as the midterms near. Republicans are closing the campaign by reviving cultural issues like transgender athletes, border security and socialism, while some Democrats try to blunt that strategy by siding with Trump on certain matters. Kansas Republican Senator Roger Marshall, once confident of reelection, now faces a race that several forecasters call a tossup, and Elon Musk is putting a small fortune behind the Republican effort to hold a Senate seat in Texas. On the other side, Hakeem Jeffries is closing in on the Speaker's gavel, but Democratic Socialists plan to demand commitments on Medicare for All, wealth taxes and an arms embargo on Israel. Trump plans a rally in a different state each day this week, starting in Kansas on Monday.

Pocketbook worries are central to that debate. Overall food prices are up four point four percent since January 2025, outpacing general inflation, with ground beef up more than twenty-two percent even as egg prices have fallen. Holiday travel looks costly too, with the average Thanksgiving round-trip domestic fare up thirty-one percent from last year as jet fuel costs surge. The job market remains tough for educated workers, and the Journal tells the story of a Princeton Ph.D. who applied to twenty-three hundred jobs and still can't land one.

Elsewhere, rioters rampaged through French cities as weeks of student protests widened into a broader movement over the rising cost of living, with more than twelve hundred arrests reported and a historic town hall in Limoges badly damaged by fire. In Oregon, police say two people heroically stopped a sixteen-year-old gunman at an elementary school in Bend, and the Times reports that four people were killed in the attack. In San Diego, an eleven billion dollar bond measure aims to boost affordable housing, crews are gaining ground on the Norte fire, and the Pendleton fire has burned more than three thousand acres and is half contained. The search for eleven missing people off the coast of Baja continues. In sports, the Rays evened the American League Championship Series, and the World Series begins Friday.

Looking ahead, Albertans vote Monday on a referendum over staying in Canada, the NBA season tips off Tuesday, and housing data arrives the same day. And for a quieter thought, the Times offers an essay on the temporary communities we join by accident, from jury rooms to airport gates.

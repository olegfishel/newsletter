Morning Briefing

Saturday, November 7, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point, The New York Times Morning newsletter, the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 7, 2026. President Trump answered the stunning Republican defeat in Tuesday's midterm elections by shaking up his own White House. He has replaced his chief of staff, Susie Wiles, with Stephen Miller, the policy adviser who built the administration's hard-edged immigration crackdown. Miller takes over on Monday. Wiles, one of the most powerful figures in Trump's orbit for years, moves over to his political operation. The president told reporters she had done a great job, but that sometimes in life you have to make moves. The Times reports he also blamed her for Tuesday's losses.

The Journal's editor in chief describes Wiles as a moderating force and Miller as an adviser who has long favored a no-holds-barred approach to governing. With Democrats now taking control of Congress, Miller is expected to push for broader use of executive power and a combative response to congressional investigations. The Journal also notes that big business has lost a sympathetic ear inside the West Wing. On the other hand, the Times says Miller spent weeks pitching himself for the job by promising to advocate for winding down the war in Iran, which brings us to the other major story of the morning.

The United States carried out new strikes on Iran on Friday, the first since the midterms. They hit an Iranian air defense system near the Strait of Hormuz, on Qeshm Island, after Iranian missiles reportedly targeted American aircraft. Trump called the action minor, saying the anti-aircraft system was knocked out in a matter of minutes, and he insisted the two countries are not fighting. He said there has been a lot of progress in negotiations led by Qatar. Iranian state media claims its forces shot down two American drones over the island, a claim U.S. Central Command had not confirmed. Behind the scenes, the Journal reports that Vice President Vance and Gulf allies are pressing Trump to end the war, while other advisers argue for renewed attacks. The San Diego paper adds that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's future is being questioned by members of both parties.

Back at home, the political map is moving fast. Democrats won control of Congress and a majority of governorships, and the Journal says the party also won governor's races in all six of the familiar presidential swing states. Victories in Texas, North Carolina, Ohio and Iowa have scrambled early thinking about how anyone gets to 270 electoral votes in 2028. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom held a slim but growing lead in the Senate race as ballots were still being counted. The Times says young voters, alienated by Trump's policies and by an older generation of politicians, powered many of the wins, and that voters in four states chose to bar transgender athletes from women's sports teams. Meanwhile, the Miami U.S. attorney who helped oversee the investigations of Trump's political foes has resigned, one of several abrupt departures from the Justice Department since Election Day, and the Journal reports in an exclusive that the White House is weighing an executive order that would attach a terrorist label to some nonprofit groups.

On the economy, the news was better than expected. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, well above forecasts, and the unemployment rate slipped to 4.1 percent. Professional and business services led the gains with about 70,000 jobs. The strong report landed after a brutal week for technology stocks. On Thursday, a selloff tied to anxiety over artificial intelligence spending, and supercharged by the Democratic victories, erased roughly two and a half trillion dollars from the Nasdaq's value and pushed it into correction territory. The Nasdaq 100 had its worst day since March 2020. On Friday the market steadied, with the S&P 500 up about a third of a percent and the Nasdaq up about one percent, but the hiring surge also raised the odds that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates in December.

In corporate news, Tesla will wind down production of the Cybertruck next year, a vehicle Elon Musk once called the company's best product ever. Musk's broader empire faces its own political test in Texas, where state Democrats say the white-glove treatment SpaceX and Tesla have enjoyed from elected officials is about to end. The Journal also reports that the American market has become the last true profit engine for the world's automakers.

Overseas, Europe is rattled by what officials believe is a coordinated campaign of bomb threats and cyberattacks that hit flights and media outlets this week, and nobody can yet say who is behind it. In Ukraine, the Institute for the Study of War says Russia has gained just 46 square miles this year through October, compared with more than 1,800 square miles in all of 2025, as Ukrainian drone strikes batter Russian supply lines. Moscow, meanwhile, has seized four factories belonging to Japan Tobacco, in what looks like pressure on Tokyo over its support for Kyiv. Elsewhere, Paraguay's president was formally removed from office and a Tanzanian court sentenced the country's main opposition leader to four years in prison.

A few more items to know. The co-pilot in the Florida Learjet crash that killed nine people was a twenty-year-old named Nathalia Filgueiras, part of a growing wave of teenagers starting early pilot careers. Meghan Markle's estranged father was arrested in Florida on child sexual-abuse material charges. Former senator and West Virginia governor Jay Rockefeller has died at eighty-nine.

For the weekend, Michigan State, at two and six, hosts rival Michigan tonight at seven-thirty. New Zealand counts votes in its general election, Berkshire Hathaway reports earnings, and on Sunday the Bengals and Falcons play a regular-season game in Madrid. And finally, as the days get shorter, the Times suggests resisting the urge to hibernate. Act a bit more like a lizard that brumates, staying aware and basking when the sun comes out, and don't sleep through the season.

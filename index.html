Thursday, October 1, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (What's News front page & The 10-Point newsletter), The New York Times (The Morning, by Sam Sifton), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition)

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 1, 2026.

The most gripping story of the morning comes from thirty-five thousand feet: a FlyDubai Boeing 737 MAX en route from Dubai to Tel Aviv nearly became a catastrophe yesterday when the first officer — identified as an Omani national — stabbed the captain in the cockpit and sent the aircraft into a terrifying nosedive. Captain Smit Machchhar, an Indian national, was stabbed but managed to open the cockpit door, allowing three passengers to rush in and wrestle the attacker to the floor. A flight attendant and the remaining crew regained the controls and guided the plane to an emergency landing at Prince Sultan bin Abdulaziz Airport in Tabuk, northwestern Saudi Arabia. All 174 people on board survived. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the attacker had been subject to “radical indoctrination.” The Wall Street Journal's reporters in Tel Aviv, Dubai, and London are bringing new details and passenger accounts of the fight to subdue the attacker — including an extraordinary close analysis of the plane's descent rate and the rare security implications that this kind of inside threat poses to commercial aviation. Much remains under active investigation, but the near-disaster has sent a shudder through a region already racked by three years of war.

A separate but potentially connected thread runs through London: U.K. Prime Minister Andy Burnham cited “strong indications” this week that Iran played a role in a foiled attack on an RAF base, adding fresh urgency to concerns about state-sponsored violence. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth in Washington is creating a new command dedicated to autonomous warfare, overhauling how the Pentagon buys, tests, and deploys drones and other technologies — a pivot that reflects how seriously American defense planners are taking the threat landscape. And a U.S. pressure campaign has left dozens of Iranian or Iran-linked tankers stranded in Asian waters, cut off from Chinese buyers as Washington threatens sanctions against anyone who comes to their assistance.

On land, a quiet chapter of American military history closed on Wednesday. The last U.S. troops completed their withdrawal from Iraq, ending a twelve-year mission that began as the campaign against ISIS. The departure was carried out with what the Pentagon described as an orderly, low-key ceremony. Combined Joint Task Force — Operation Inherent Resolve will continue, now headquartered in Jordan. But Iran-aligned militias inside Iraq celebrated the U.S. exit, while officials in the Kurdish region raised urgent concerns about losing American air-defense capabilities. The Journal's front page framed it plainly: the United States leaves behind a nation in peril, with tens of thousands of armed militants and a fragile political order that the Journal says falls short of any stable settlement.

In Washington, the New York Times brings this morning a sweeping seven-act account of President Trump's obsessive and contentious relationship with the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts. When Trump returned to office last year, he fired the Biden-era board, installed loyalists — including the vice president's wife and the commerce secretary's wife — and named himself chairman, the first president ever to hold that position. Under the DOGE regime, Richard Grenell fired dozens of staffers and slashed the fundraising office from ninety-three employees to just seventeen. Major performers canceled. The Washington National Opera cut ties. Ticket sales collapsed. Trump renamed the building after himself, but a federal judge ruled that Congress had established the name by statute and it had to come down. Most recently, after a storm brought down a section of ceiling plaster last month, the president declared the building unsafe and called for its closure — a move his critics see as a pretext to shutter an institution he never fully controlled.

On the economy, the third quarter ended with a mixed but technology-driven picture. Big tech propelled the S&P 500 up roughly two percent and the Nasdaq up two-and-a-half percent for the quarter, extending what is shaping up as the fourth consecutive year of double-digit gains — a streak not seen since the late nineteen-nineties. The Dow, however, fell nearly three percent. More unsettling to fixed-income investors: the yield on the ten-year Treasury note climbed above five-point-three percent on Wednesday, surpassing a 2007 peak and reaching its highest level since 2002. Money managers are now scrambling to convince clients to get back into bonds — pitching the most attractive yields in a generation — but two years of turbulent returns have left many investors deeply wary. The Journal describes it as a tall task in a market where rising yields are sending bond prices inversely lower.

In corporate news, Mattel chief executive Ynon Kreiz has been tapped to lead the combined Paramount-Warner Bros. Discovery entity as co-CEO once that eighty-one-billion-dollar merger closes. Walgreens' private-equity owner Sycamore Partners is nearing a nine-billion-dollar deal to sell the British pharmacy chain Boots. DoorDash is expanding its delivery footprint to clothing, shoes, and other consumer goods. BMW says it will use artificial intelligence to trim its workforce as it prepares for tougher market conditions, while German union IG Metall warned of conflict with Volkswagen after the automaker said it plans to terminate wage agreements. Japan's Nidec swung to a heavy annual net loss after a four-billion-dollar impairment in its auto-parts division, a result that followed the abrupt replacement of its chief executive the day before.

The artificial intelligence sector is generating turbulence of its own. The Federal Trade Commission has opened an investigation into both Anthropic and OpenAI to determine whether either company may have deceived consumers about the potential harms of their products. Meanwhile, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang joined other industry leaders in publicly questioning Anthropic's Dario Amodei over his dire warnings about AI risk — a sign of the deepening philosophical divide within the industry over how to characterize its own technology. OpenAI President Greg Brockman separately backed away from a pledge to make a multimillion-dollar donation to a political action committee supporting pro-AI candidates. And a sweeping Journal investigation finds that Google's aggressive push to deploy AI tools in American classrooms has left many students academically and emotionally dependent on the technology — an outcome that, the report notes, Google's own researchers have flagged as a potential harm to children.

Elsewhere in health, a next-generation weight-loss drug from Eli Lilly showed strong promise when combined with Zepbound in a new study, and Medicare is rolling out a program to match the prices that other countries pay for certain expensive drugs. In California, the governor's race heated up yesterday with rivals clashing in a face-to-face debate. And here in San Diego, the city has been sued over its police department's use of Flock license-plate reader cameras, which civil liberties advocates say constitute warrantless mass surveillance of residents. A new poll finds that most San Diego County voters support a proposed half-cent sales tax increase. On the sports front, the Padres are headed to the National League Division Series, with Petco Park generating what the Union-Tribune calls playoff energy that has exceeded every expectation. San Diego State is dealing with a mumps outbreak that has left more than thirty Aztec football players unavailable — a serious setback as the program learns its new Pac-12 schedule, including dates against Gonzaga.

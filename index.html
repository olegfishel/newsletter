Good morning, it's Saturday, July 25, 2026. Five months into the U.S.-Iran war, the conflict is showing every sign of becoming a prolonged test of endurance, and the region is holding its breath as President Trump privately threatens a dramatic escalation while publicly dangling the possibility of renewed diplomacy.

The centerpiece of this week's geopolitical drama was a Wall Street Journal exclusive: Bahrain and Kuwait secretly dispatched fighter jets to strike military sites inside Iran earlier this month, marking the first time Arab Gulf states have directly attacked Iranian territory. The United Arab Emirates — which had struck Iran multiple times earlier in the war — provided targeting intelligence and defensive air cover, a sign that the Gulf states are quietly forging a united front against Tehran. Iran had concentrated its attacks on Bahrain and Kuwait because both host major American military installations, including the U.S. Navy's 5th Fleet. Kuwait's government denied the report; Bahrain declined to comment.

On the American side, the pace of the conflict has not slackened. The U.S. military has now conducted thirteen consecutive nights of airstrikes against Iran, aimed at degrading Tehran's ability to threaten maritime traffic through the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea. Iran responded with drone strikes against U.S. facilities in Bahrain and Jordan. The U.N. Secretary-General told the Security Council that the Middle East was "teetering on the edge of the unimaginable." Inside the White House, the debate is very much alive: Trump told advisers he is "considering a massive attack — bigger than ever before," and U.S. intelligence officials have concluded that Iran's current supreme leader is more determined to build a nuclear weapon than his predecessor. Yet by Friday, Trump was also leaning into talk of negotiations, sending mixed signals that reflect genuine uncertainty about where this war goes next. The Journal's reporting makes clear that both governments appear to be reaching the limits of what military force alone can achieve, raising the specter of a war of attrition stretching well beyond 2026.

The economic spillover from the conflict drove the week's most dramatic market moves. Brent crude surged past $100 a barrel on Thursday — its highest level since May — as tanker traffic through the Gulf was disrupted, rekindling inflation fears that sent the 10-year Treasury yield briefly above 4.7%, its highest point of Trump's second term. By Friday, oil had eased somewhat and stocks steadied, but the damage for the week was real: the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.1%, the S&P 500 shed 0.6%, and the Dow slipped 0.4%, marking back-to-back weekly losses for the first time since March. Disappointing earnings from Alphabet and Tesla amplified the tech selloff, and the Journal noted a broader pullback in AI investment as corporate America grows more cautious about spending.

On that front, a separate front-page story detailed China's all-out push to close the AI chip gap with the United States. Xi Jinping has tasked a close confidant with leading what the Journal describes as a "fevered effort" to build domestic alternatives to Nvidia chips; Chinese AI companies received a blunt warning that anyone who resisted using local chips risked being branded a traitor. At the same time, American companies are increasingly shopping à la carte for AI, mixing lower-cost models — including some built in China — alongside offerings from OpenAI and Anthropic rather than committing to a single provider. The shift is changing the economics of the industry and forcing AI companies to fight harder to keep customers.

On the public health front, measles cases in the United States have reached a 35-year record in 2026, with more than 2,300 confirmed infections through July — already surpassing all of last year's total and spreading across 45 states. The CDC warns that the country's elimination status, held since 2000, is now at risk. Ninety-three percent of cases involve unvaccinated individuals. In a related public health crisis, the cyclospora outbreak linked to contaminated iceberg lettuce has expanded to nine states, with nearly 2,000 people sickened and 98 hospitalized. The Wall Street Journal revealed that produce giant Taylor Farms asked the White House to delay announcing its recall, saying it wanted more evidence before being publicly tied to what may be one of the largest foodborne illness outbreaks in the nation's recent history.

In other developments: a teenager in Georgia pleaded guilty to the 2024 school shooting that killed two students and two teachers and wounded nine others. The International Criminal Court formally removed its chief prosecutor, Karim Khan, over sexual assault allegations. The White House Correspondents' Association finally held its long-delayed dinner Friday evening — three months after the original event was cut short by the attempted assassination of President Trump. Trump attended but was not in a genial mood, asking the assembled press: "Is this supposed to be fun?" Separately, federal officials acknowledged in legal proceedings that billions of dollars in grants to states were canceled, in their own words, "based solely" on the fact that Trump did not win those states in 2024. And Paramount's planned merger with Warner Bros. Discovery has been put on hold while courts review a legal challenge, with a backstop deadline of June 1, 2027.

SpaceX launched its Starship rocket Friday on its first test flight since the company's landmark IPO, a milestone that underscores how the Musk enterprise has graduated from ambitious startup to publicly traded institution. In Europe, wildfires tearing through southern France and Spain have forced more than 200,000 people to evacuate their homes. The Tour de France concludes in Paris on Sunday. In San Diego, the Padres found a way to win Friday and continue positioning themselves for a trade-deadline push. And after months of speculation, LeBron James has made his free-agency decision official: he is heading to Philadelphia.

Morning Briefing

Monday, August 3, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point newsletter), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Bloomberg, CNBC, NBC News, The Hill, and Emerson College Polling.

Good morning, it's Monday, August 3, 2026. The dominant story is a fragile but consequential pivot in American policy toward Iran. President Trump, after days of threatening a new military offensive, called off a planned weekend strike at the direct request of Gulf Arab allies and announced that fresh negotiations would begin Monday afternoon. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Iran has shown greater readiness to discuss both the Strait of Hormuz and its nuclear program in the wake of recent U.S. military pressure. Iran's Foreign Ministry is simultaneously in talks with Oman to establish a temporary, safe shipping lane through the strait — though Tehran insists it is not yet in direct talks with Washington. Markets responded immediately: oil futures fell more than seven percent to around $78 a barrel, offering relief to consumers and businesses that have absorbed elevated energy costs for months. OPEC and its allies pressed ahead with yet another production increase, adding roughly 188,000 barrels a day for August — the fifth consecutive monthly rise in the group's methodical reversal of pandemic-era cuts.

On the domestic political front, all eyes tomorrow turn to Michigan, where Democrats will choose their Senate nominee in a race the New York Times calls a do-or-die moment for the party. The matchup pits Representative Haley Stevens — a moderate backed by AIPAC and its $30 million in outside advertising, with endorsements from the Obama orbit and Michigan's entire Democratic establishment — against Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive epidemiologist and former Detroit health commissioner who has called Israel's government evil and campaigns on abolishing ICE, expanding Medicare, and taxing the wealthy. El-Sayed enters tomorrow with a commanding lead — polls show him ahead by fifteen points or more, and prediction markets peg his winning chances at around ninety-seven percent. The race matters well beyond Michigan's borders: Democrats need the seat and must flip at least four Republican-held seats elsewhere to take the Senate. Previous progressive insurgencies played out in deep-blue congressional districts; Michigan, which went for Trump in 2024, is the first real stress test of whether that wave can survive in a genuine swing state.

Democrats are also grappling with a structural fundraising problem complicating their path to November. Major donors have essentially gone on strike, shunning Washington establishment groups and leaving Democratic fundraising organizations trailing their Republican counterparts by significant margins. The discontent reflects a broader skepticism among the donor class about the party's direction and its ability to find a message that resonates beyond its progressive base.

America's colleges are in deeper financial trouble than many parents and donors realize. The Journal reports that nearly 200 private colleges borrowed from restricted endowments to cover ordinary operating expenses last year — up from 131 in 2021 — raiding funds that philanthropists had given for specific purposes, often without those donors' knowledge or consent. As campuses prepare for fall, programs are being cut, faculty laid off, and some schools are effectively gambling with the gifts of thousands of benefactors who believed their money was ringfenced.

On Wall Street, stocks opened Monday with gains. The Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed more than one percent above 53,000, the S&P 500 added three-quarters of a percent to 7,546, and the Nasdaq gained just under a percent. Big Tech earnings are telling sharply divergent stories — some companies surging on AI-driven results, others falling short — while a growing wave of AI-related debt is raising longer-term questions for bond markets. OpenAI announced it now counts more than one billion active users and two million businesses on its platform, a milestone that coincided with price cuts across several of its AI model offerings, a sign of the fierce competitive pressure reshaping the industry.

Hollywood offered rare unambiguous good news this weekend: Sony Pictures' Spider-Man: Brand New Day opened to an estimated $927 million in global box-office sales, the second-biggest film debut in history, powered by a $355 million domestic opening — the largest of 2026 by far. The superhero genre, which has been in a prolonged slump, badly needed the win.

Wall Street's elite trading firms and Silicon Valley giants are meanwhile fighting over the same tiny pool of exceptional math graduates — offering million-dollar packages to PhDs and top undergraduates who might otherwise go into AI research. With artificial intelligence systems themselves now competing for quantitative talent, the bidding shows no sign of cooling.

Six months after Nancy Guthrie — mother of Today show anchor Savannah Guthrie — was abducted from her bed at her Arizona ranch home, investigators have little to show for the inquiry beyond, as the Journal puts it, a trail of missteps. Authorities have no confirmed breaks in the case, livestreamers continue to spread speculation, and the family's pain compounds by the day. The Journal's reconstruction of the investigation — based on public records, police radio traffic, flight data, and interviews with law enforcement — makes for sobering reading.

The first formal negotiations aimed at steering Venezuela toward democracy have kicked off, though without the political opposition's most influential leader — a troubling sign for what was meant to be a structured transition process. American oil companies have meanwhile made little headway in tapping Venezuela's fields seven months after the U.S. helped remove Nicolás Maduro from power, underscoring how difficult genuine recovery will be. In Canada, flight attendants at WestJet walked off the job after failing to reach a contract agreement, grounding hundreds of flights. The U.S. Treasury quietly intervened last week to prop up the Japanese yen, which had been trading near forty-year lows. And wildfires destroyed hundreds of homes and forced mass evacuations across eastern Washington state, while Spain expelled the bulk of the tens of thousands of migrants who stormed the Spanish enclave of Ceuta in North Africa.

On a lighter note: America mourns Dannon coffee yogurt. For nearly four decades it was a morning ritual for a devoted following — who know it simply as DCY — but Dannon discontinued the flavor and the withdrawal has been, by all accounts, severe. Fans are clearing shelves, swapping tips in Facebook groups, and debating what, exactly, to get out of bed for now. One New Jersey lawyer told the Journal it was simply "what I got out of bed for." In the news business, we understand completely.

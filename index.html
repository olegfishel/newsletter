Good morning, it's Friday, August 28, 2026. The Federal Aviation Administration is moving to fire two air-traffic controllers who quietly clocked out more than an hour early on the night an Air Canada regional jet slammed into a fire truck at LaGuardia Airport this past March, killing both pilots in New York's first fatal commercial crash in thirty-four years. The FAA's inspector general found the two controllers walked off the job in what the agency calls a "shove" — an unrecorded early departure that amounted to timecard fraud — leaving only two colleagues in the tower as the late-night workload peaked. The NTSB's separate investigation into why an airport firefighting vehicle was on an active runway as the Bombardier CRJ900 from Montreal touched down remains open, and the question of whether the understaffed tower contributed to the collision has not yet been resolved.

Across the Atlantic and in the courts, two rulings handed down Thursday kept Washington's news cycle churning. A federal judge in California ruled that the Trump administration's Pentagon violated the First Amendment when it designated Anthropic — the San Francisco artificial-intelligence company behind the Claude models — a supply-chain risk to national security. Judge Rita Lin found that the blacklisting was "unlawful retaliation" rooted in Anthropic's public criticism of the administration's position on autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance. In a biting sixty-page opinion, she wrote that "the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics." The designation, which barred defense contractors from using Anthropic's technology, remains technically in place while parallel litigation in Washington continues, but Thursday's order is a major legal setback for the Pentagon's position.

A second judge, this one in the District of Columbia, issued a temporary restraining order blocking the Postal Service from implementing new rules that would have sharply curtailed mail-in voting ahead of November's midterm elections. The court called the USPS regulations "a likely unconstitutional rule for which compliance is practically impossible" given how close the election season has already become. The ruling delivers a short-term victory to voting-rights advocates who argued the changes would suppress ballots in dozens of states.

President Trump signed another executive order Thursday afternoon to rename Lake Ontario "Lake America," escalating his running territorial dispute with Canada. The move mirrors his 2025 renaming of the Gulf of Mexico, and while the president has the power to change the name for federal government purposes, he cannot bind Canada, international bodies, or the more than four hundred years of history behind the Wendat word Ontari'io from which the lake's name derives. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney swiftly pushed back, calling the gesture an act of symbolic aggression that does nothing to resolve the two countries' ongoing tariff standoff. Trump, when asked whether there was a message in the renaming, said only, "No message — Canada has been ripping us off for a long time."

On the energy front, the Trump administration is deep into negotiations with Venezuela's government for what could be described as a sweeping deal: a direct U.S. ownership stake in more than a dozen Venezuelan oil fields holding roughly ninety billion barrels of proven reserves — nearly a third of the country's enormous energy wealth. Bloomberg and Axios reported that one framework under discussion is a one-hundred-year lease arrangement. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Energy Secretary Chris Wright, who is expected to travel to Caracas next week, have been leading the talks alongside Deputy White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller. Proponents argue the deal could dramatically expand American energy reserves and bring private capital to underdeveloped fields; critics note it would amount to a massive economic concession to a government the United States has long sanctioned.

In the technology sector, Nvidia — which just reported another record-breaking quarterly result — has quietly paused its novel financing program that offered credit support to smaller artificial-intelligence cloud providers in exchange for a share of their future revenue. The move comes after some Nvidia employees raised internal concerns about potential antitrust scrutiny and after early partners balked at the degree of control Nvidia sought over their operations. The company could modify the program or fold it into another initiative. Separately, Chinese AI startup DeepSeek is nearing a new funding round that would value the Hangzhou-based company at around seventy-four billion dollars — roughly five hundred billion yuan — with the fundraising aimed at building its war chest ahead of a planned initial public offering next year. Meanwhile, Barret Zoph, who co-founded the AI research firm Thinking Machines Lab before defecting to OpenAI, is now headed to Alphabet's Google, where he will serve as a vice president of research.

Two more Wall Street Journal exclusives deserve attention. Corporate America's post-election anxiety is growing: major tech firms and others that wrote large checks to Trump's inaugural fund are now quietly hiring Democratic-leaning advisers and courting House Democrats, worried that if Republicans lose their slim House majority in November, they could become targets of congressional investigations and subpoenas. And the Trump family's World Liberty Financial crypto venture is moving closer to launching a new bank, backed in part by a forty-nine percent stake held by entities associated with an Abu Dhabi royal sometimes described as the "spy sheikh" — a figure who has long cultivated ties to American power centers.

Overseas, Nepal and Tibet are reeling after devastating floods killed more than five hundred people this week when a wall of water, mud, and debris roared through a Himalayan river valley. Iran, meanwhile, said it is compiling a list of conditions under which it might reopen the Strait of Hormuz to normal shipping after weeks of tension over the crucial oil chokepoint, with Omani-brokered talks providing a narrow diplomatic off-ramp.

On markets: Thursday's session saw U.S. stocks close modestly higher — the S&P 500 gained about seven-tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq climbed one-point-six percent, and the Dow edged up two-tenths — but futures this morning are pointing slightly lower. The ten-year Treasury yield stands at four-point-seven-three percent. Gold is pulling back sharply, down nearly three percent to around forty-five hundred dollars an ounce. Bitcoin is also off about two-point-seven percent, trading near seventy-seven thousand dollars. Crude oil is little changed at eighty-three dollars a barrel.

In San Diego, a heat advisory and extreme heat warning are in effect through eight o'clock this evening for the Santa Ana Mountains and inland foothills, with temperatures expected to top out between the mid-nineties and one hundred five degrees. County health officials are warning beachgoers that water quality at several coastal sites is below safe levels for swimming. And on the lighter side, federal investigators say a U.S. servicemember is facing charges for placing bets on Polymarket's prediction markets tied to military operations — earning more than a million dollars from insider knowledge before authorities caught on.

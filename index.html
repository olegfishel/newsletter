Good morning, it's Thursday, September 3, 2026. The United States is waking up to a war that was supposed to be quick, a military that is quietly being remade from the inside out, and a federal courthouse ruling that will let Google keep its advertising empire — battered but intact. Here is everything you need to know.

The most significant story in today's papers, threading through both the Wall Street Journal and the New York Times, is the deepening and widening of the American military commitment in the Middle East. Six months ago, when the United States launched its campaign against Iran — a conflict ignited by U.S.-Israeli airstrikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei — Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth promised a decisive strike free of the long entanglements that plagued Iraq and Afghanistan. That promise has quietly been retired. The Pentagon is now extending troop deployments into 2027, signaling what WSJ reporters describe as an open-ended military strategy designed to preserve the president's options as a six-month war with no clear endgame continues. Some 50,000 American troops remain in the region, backed by two aircraft carriers, nineteen warships, and an extensive air-defense network. The strain on service members and their families is mounting, and a maintenance backlog could take years to clear.

The New York Times uses today's edition to examine what Hegseth has done to the institution he runs. Four Times reporters — Helene Cooper, John Ismay, Greg Jaffe, and Eric Schmitt — document a sweeping ideological transformation of the Pentagon: books removed from military base libraries, sociology eliminated as a major at West Point, officers barred from Ivy League graduate programs, and more than ten percent of the top officer corps — disproportionately Black and female — passed over for promotion or pushed out. The Army's civilian leader quit this week. Hegseth has not held a Pentagon press briefing since May. The Times has filed suit against the administration for restricting access to the agency. Meanwhile, the conflict produced a grim visual investigation: American forces bombed a wedding in southern Iran, killing at least five people and wounding more than sixty. The Times tracked the strike via satellite imagery and confirmed that U.S. aircraft dropped the bomb. Iran retaliated this week with fire on Gulf states, according to the San Diego Union-Tribune.

The strained state of the military became human this week in a different way when the USS Abraham Lincoln docked at the Thai port of Laem Chabang — the aircraft carrier's first port stop after 286 days at sea. Some 5,000 sailors and Marines, deployed since November in support of operations against Iran, finally stepped ashore for rest and recreation. An earlier effort to give the crew a break had to be called off when morale problems became acute, and a military newspaper that covered the sailors' distress was subsequently punished by the Pentagon. The Lincoln's arrival in Thailand is the first full port call since the deployment began and is strictly for crew rest — no joint military operations are planned.

Gloria Steinem, the journalist and activist who became the most recognizable face of the American women's movement, died Wednesday at 92, peacefully at her New York home. Steinem co-founded Ms. magazine in 1972 and the National Women's Political Caucus in 1971, and for half a century her tinted aviator glasses and curtain of streaked-blond hair made her one of the most photographed women in America. Her death was announced on social media, which said she "continued working for equality until the very end." No cause was given. She is mourned today across the country she helped transform.

In the courts, a federal judge handed Google a significant partial victory, rejecting the Justice Department's bid to break up its online advertising business. Judge Leonie Brinkema found that Google had indeed violated antitrust law — this is now the second such liability ruling in quick succession — but declined to order a divestiture of its ad exchange, opting instead for behavioral remedies requiring Google to adjust its practices in ways that benefit competitors. Both parties have thirty days to submit a proposed final judgment. It is the second time a court has spared a Google business from forced sale, following a separate judge's decision last year to leave the Chrome browser intact.

Ford announced ambitious targets for its new Fathom electric pickup truck — more than 100,000 units in its first year of production — with a starting price of around $30,000, designed to compete directly with Tesla and the upstart Chinese automakers who have been pressing into the American market. If Ford hits the number, the Fathom would instantly become one of the top-selling EVs in the country. Volkswagen, meanwhile, is fighting a battle on two fronts: Chinese rivals eating away at its European share and an internal revolt over chief executive Oliver Blume's restructuring plan, which would make the company smaller and less German and has sparked fierce backlash from unions and politicians.

The bond market continued to unsettle investors. Treasury yields are at multi-year highs — the ten-year at 4.76% this morning — with the primary driver, according to the Journal, being the sharp climb in energy prices driven by the Iran war and its inflationary ripple effects through the global economy. U.S. equities held up anyway, with the S&P 500 and Nasdaq each gaining half a percent Wednesday and the Dow adding 0.6%. Gold has climbed past $4,500 an ounce. An unusual ideological coalition is forming around the artificial-intelligence buildout: Steve Bannon and progressive lawmakers including Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have found common cause opposing the data centers and energy consumption powering AI, a reminder of how thoroughly this technology has scrambled ordinary political alignments.

In sports, the NBA dropped what it described as the largest penalty in league history on the Los Angeles Clippers. After a yearlong investigation into salary-cap circumvention, the league found that owner Steve Ballmer knowingly helped funnel millions of dollars to star player Kawhi Leonard through an off-court endorsement arrangement that violated league rules. The punishment: Ballmer suspended one year, the Clippers fined $30 million and stripped of five first-round draft picks spanning 2029 through 2033, and Leonard himself fined $700,000. The Clippers called the investigation "heavily biased" and said they "vehemently reject" the findings, but the penalties are final and binding on all parties.

World-Wide

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is extending troop deployments that signal the war with Iran could be prolonged well into 2027, placing significant strain on servicemembers and their families and creating a maintenance backlog that could take years to unwind.

Steve Bannon, Trump's former chief strategist, joined Democrats in warning about the dangers of artificial intelligence and the need to fight data centers powering AI deployment — a strange-bedfellows coalition animating the left and the right simultaneously.

Tech executives and government officials urged policymakers from leading economies to minimize AI regulations, arguing strict rules could strangle an industry capable of supercharging global growth and curing diseases.

The U.S. government filed a statement supporting OpenAI in its legal battle with publishers, arguing that training on news content falls under fair use in U.S. copyright law.

Special forces from Israel's Shin Bet security service seized a senior Hamas official near his home in Gaza and spirited him across the border into Israel for interrogation.

Chinese leader Xi Jinping began a state visit to Egypt that aims to reinforce Beijing's ties with an important American ally in the Middle East.

Japan faces what officials describe as its most severe security environment since World War II, as Chinese warships pass through sensitive straits near Amami-Oshima at an accelerating pace — at least ten transits this year, up from zero in 2025.

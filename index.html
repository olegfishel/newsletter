Morning Briefing

Thursday, October 22, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, the New York Times Morning newsletter, the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 22, 2026.

The Middle East is where the day begins, because the region is edging back toward open conflict. Saudi authorities say Iran-backed Houthi militants struck King Fahd International Airport in Dammam on Wednesday evening, killing at least one person and wounding eight. Some early reports put the toll at two. The Houthis say they have gone after a string of Saudi targets over the past two days, including another airport, a defense ministry site in Riyadh, and Aramco facilities on the Red Sea coast. The Saudi-led coalition says it shot down several drones over the Riyadh and Medina regions, and flights at Dammam have since returned to normal. The United States has ordered some embassy staff to leave.

That attack arrives alongside a worrying night in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran says seven oil tankers were set ablaze or damaged overnight. Four were hit while transferring cargo in the northern Persian Gulf, and three struck mines in the western strait. Washington is sending a third aircraft carrier and thousands more troops to join what is already the largest American naval and air presence in the region in decades. The New York Times reports that the United States and Israel have drawn up plans to renew strikes on Iran after the midterm elections, with energy infrastructure, nuclear sites, and missile stockpiles among the likely targets. Mediators from Qatar and Pakistan are trying to keep talks alive, and Turkey will host foreign ministers from several countries in Istanbul on Friday. There is also an economic squeeze on Tehran. The Journal reports that Iran's fleet of shadow tankers, which for most of the war sat in Asian waters quietly supplying Chinese refineries, has run out of oil. Oil sales can account for as much as forty percent of government revenue, so the loss of that cushion matters.

Markets are reacting. Stocks rose on Wednesday, with the S&P 500 up six tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq up one point one percent, and the Dow up seven tenths, helped by big names in artificial intelligence. This morning the picture has flipped. Futures are down roughly one percent for the S&P 500, nearly two percent for the Nasdaq 100, and about two thirds of a percent for the Dow. Earnings from Tesla and Meta did little to settle doubts about AI spending, and oil has jumped back to about one hundred twelve dollars a barrel on the Iran tensions.

Tesla is also at the center of the biggest business story of the morning. Elon Musk has signaled he is interested in combining Tesla and SpaceX, a deal that could become the largest corporate merger in history. The Journal notes that the two companies have been tangled together for two decades, sharing resources and ambitions. Musk's pitch is a vision of physical AI, with Tesla building robotaxis and robots and SpaceX providing the global network to connect them. A merger could ease the strain on his attention and give him more control at Tesla, though regulators are certain to look closely. He said Tesla could begin shipping its Optimus humanoid robots by the end of next year. Separately, the Journal reports that Musk ordered a slowdown of Starlink service that blindsided Ukraine, and lawmakers from both parties are calling for an investigation.

In technology, researchers using advanced AI tools have found one of the most severe web security flaws in years, in PHP software that powers as much as seventy percent of the world's websites. Cyber experts are racing to patch it before hackers use AI to exploit it, and some European governments have blocked access to their own sites as a precaution. In Meta news, a New Mexico judge ordered the company to pay twenty-five billion dollars for deceiving users about data privacy, one of the largest penalties ever imposed on a tech company. Mark Zuckerberg, meanwhile, pressed for a stock split designed to keep him in California despite a proposed billionaire tax.

On the political front, midterm season is getting rough. Fights between rallygoers and protesters interrupted President Trump at an event in South Carolina last night, and he is headed to Iowa for a rally today. A new Times and Siena poll finds young voters overwhelmingly backing Democrats. In Michigan's Senate race, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers had a heated debate. AIPAC, meanwhile, said it is ending candidate endorsements and contributions from its political action committee as more candidates distance themselves from the group. Overseas, the Times says Vladimir Putin appears to be moving closer to restoring relations with Washington without giving ground on Ukraine, and a Journal investigation traces an airfield sabotage attempt in eastern Germany to a new Putin-ordered campaign.

A few things closer to home. Corporate America is passing more of its health care costs to workers, with the average family plan up five percent this year and the employee share up nine percent to about seventy-five hundred dollars. Doctors say Pope Leo needs no further treatment for lung cancer. And in San Diego, the city moves closer to turning off its Flock license-plate cameras, an AI data center is being weighed at a Navy property, and the military's largest mess hall has opened. In sports, the Chargers are looking for a reboot after an 0 and 6 start, and the World Series is underway.

Finally, a lighter note. A forty-five million dollar Nicolas Cage movie has gone missing after a hard drive was stolen from a Netflix office, and an Australian zoo is playing matchmaker for two of the world's deadliest birds, southern cassowaries, and their fluorescent green eggs.

Business & Finance

The Nasdaq is beating the New York Stock Exchange in the contest for the biggest IPOs, and Anthropic is expected to bring the tech-heavy exchange another blockbuster listing.

Elon Musk signaled interest in uniting SpaceX and Tesla in what would be the largest corporate merger of all time.

Musk said Tesla could be ready to start shipping its Optimus humanoid robots by the end of next year, and that its fleet of autonomous taxis will soon become a significant driver of revenue.

Big names in AI helped lift the major U.S. stock indexes, with the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow rising 0.6 percent, 1.1 percent and 0.7 percent, respectively.

AT&T raised its full-year profit outlook and again reported quarterly revenue growth, but disappointed some investors with its core wireless subscriber numbers and rising expenses.

Abbott plans to spin off its embattled nutrition business by the end of next year, a separation it says will let both companies direct resources more effectively.

Akzo Nobel is in talks with buyers to sell its European vehicle-refinishing business as it seeks regulatory clearance for its proposed merger with U.S. peer Axalta.

Carrefour shares fell after the grocer cut its profitability targets for the year, expecting protests in France to weigh on sales.

Hudbay Minerals and Mitsubishi formally approved construction of what promises to be the next major U.S. copper mine, with production set to begin in 2029.

Carrier Global plans to invest more than 600 million dollars in a new Texas plant to meet record demand for data-center cooling equipment, the biggest investment in the company's history.

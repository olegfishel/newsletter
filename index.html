Morning Briefing

Monday, October 12, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, October 12, 2026. The week opens with the Middle East back at the center of the world's attention, as Iran-backed Houthi militants have carried their war in Yemen deep into Saudi Arabia, striking the main international airport in Riyadh twice in two days.

The first strike, on Saturday, killed at least a dozen people, including an American, and wounded more than three hundred others. A second attack on the same airport on Sunday killed one more person and injured eleven. The Houthis say they are answering Saudi aggression, including a strike on the airport in the Yemeni capital, Sanaa, and they had warned global airlines, in their words for the last time, about operating in Saudi airspace. Turkey, Pakistan, Egypt and Saudi Arabia itself have condemned the attacks.

The fallout is already global. Airlines have canceled more than a thousand flights, and carriers including Lufthansa, Air India, flydubai and Pakistan International have suspended service to Saudi airports for varying periods. Saudi airspace is one of the last two major air corridors linking Europe and Asia, so the threat reaches well beyond the kingdom. The Wall Street Journal's reporting paints a striking picture of the crown prince, Mohammed bin Salman, shuttling between heavily guarded palaces equipped with bunkers. The Journal argues that his own choices helped create the predicament: heavy spending and a misreading of the Houthi threat have left the economy weaker, and rising defense and infrastructure costs now threaten the money he needs to turn Saudi Arabia into a global tourism and investment hub. The Washington Post and Bloomberg both warn that the strikes raise the risk of a wider regional war that could eventually pull in the United States.

Markets are taking it in stride, at least for now. Stock futures are little changed, with the Dow and S&P 500 edging slightly higher and the Nasdaq a touch lower. Oil is the pressure point: Brent crude has climbed above a hundred and six dollars a barrel, a two-week high, while American crude is trading in the low nineties. The ten-year Treasury yield is sitting above five percent, and investors are bracing for a busy week of bank earnings and inflation data that could shape the Federal Reserve's next move. Meanwhile, finance officials gather in Bangkok today for the annual meetings of the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

On Ukraine, President Trump announced Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate energy ceasefire, a halt to strikes on each other's power and fuel facilities. The picture on the ground is murkier. President Zelensky said the announcement was news to him, though he added that Ukraine would agree if Russia does. Ukrainian officials say Russian strikes continued in the hours afterward and again this morning, including on an energy facility on the outskirts of Kharkiv, while the Kremlin says only that contacts are ongoing. The announcement follows Friday's deal to ease sanctions on Russian diesel, under which Moscow would supply more than four million tons to global markets in an effort to bring down American fuel prices. That move infuriated Kyiv, and a bipartisan group of senators is now challenging it under a 2022 law. Over the weekend Mr. Trump also suggested Ukraine should choose a new leader, which Kyiv flatly rejected. Mr. Zelensky said Ukraine would hold elections if Russia accepted a sixty-day ceasefire, and that Ukraine would stop hitting Russian refineries if Moscow stopped hitting Ukrainian energy infrastructure.

In Washington, a federal judge has ordered the administration to stop airing taxpayer-funded television ads that promote the president, finding them likely illegal. The Justice Department, meanwhile, is investigating whether television networks broke antitrust law when they briefly suspended their rotation of reporters covering the White House last month, after CNN was removed from a planned assignment. And Kimberly Guilfoyle, the ambassador to Greece, may soon be out of a job. Reports say the president and Secretary of State Rubio are unhappy over allegations about her conduct, including a Journal exclusive on private jet trips and luxury stays during a visit to Davos, and a decision could come as soon as Tuesday.

Politics is also dominated by the midterms. Mr. Trump holds a rally in Ohio today, where the governor's race has turned into one of the ugliest of the cycle. The New York Times notes that thirty-six states are choosing governors, and the races to watch include Ohio, where Democrat Amy Acton has taken a small lead over Republican Vivek Ramaswamy in the latest Times and Siena poll; Georgia, where Republican Rick Jackson holds a slight edge over Keisha Lance Bottoms; and Texas, where Governor Greg Abbott now narrowly trails Democrat Gina Hinojosa. Elon Musk, for his part, is spending about two hundred and seventy-six million dollars to help Republicans. In California, Governor Newsom, a longtime opponent of a ballot measure taxing billionaires' wealth, is now endorsing a rival measure that would prohibit wealth taxes.

In business, Amazon is in talks to buy the artificial intelligence startup Decart in a deal that could be valued at around seven billion dollars. Shawn Fain and his allies have claimed an easy victory in the United Auto Workers election. Retailers such as Macy's and Levi Strauss say part of their tariff refunds will go toward holiday marketing, even as electric vehicle sales in the United States are down by half from a year ago. Courts, meanwhile, are wrestling with people placing prediction-market bets on jury trials, raising worries about insider trading and juror influence. And in Britain, the Journal reports that about seven million people, more than ten percent of the population, now claim sickness or disability benefits, putting the country's finances at risk. Two American economists, Robert Hall and Michael Woodford, won the Nobel Prize in economics.

Closer to home, Tropical Storm Rachel swamped San Diego County over the weekend, with a flood watch in effect and heavy surf along the coast. Elsewhere, Hurricane Isaias killed at least four people in Alabama and Florida, the first hurricane to strike the mainland United States in two years. Pope Leo says he will have surgery to remove a small growth on his lung. India locked down its capital to head off a mass protest over voter-list revisions, though the Union-Tribune reports that protest leaders have been freed. And in baseball, the Milwaukee Brewers beat the defending champion Dodgers, two to one, in the opener of the National League Championship Series.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 27, 2026. Israelis go to the polls today in what many there regard as the most consequential election in decades, the first national vote since the October 7th attacks and the years of war that followed. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is seeking another term against former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, and the Journal observes that the two men differ less than you might expect. A rightward shift among voters on security leaves both candidates little room to maneuver on Gaza, on Iran, and on the question of a Palestinian state. With 120 seats in the Knesset at stake and no obvious path to a majority for either side, coalition bargaining could stretch on for weeks.

The vote arrives against the backdrop of the standoff with Iran, which showed signs of both diplomacy and danger overnight. An Iranian ambassador said Tehran has put a seven-day plan to Washington, under which the Strait of Hormuz would reopen on the sixth day if the United States accepts Iran's conditions, with negotiations beginning on the seventh. He said Iran does not want war and is preparing a response to American feedback. Qatar, which is mediating, says Tehran must show good intentions, stop threatening its Arab neighbors, and open Hormuz if talks are to succeed, and officials from Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Pakistan and Iran agreed on Monday to keep their own dialogue going. Yet the danger has not receded. Yemen's Houthis say they attacked Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery, the kingdom's largest, and Saudi officials say they contained a fire near the oil terminal in the early hours. Iran's armed forces chief claims the Strait is completely closed, although ship tracking still shows reduced, but not zero, traffic. President Trump has said the United States will continue the ceasefire while it waits on Iranian proposals.

That tension is feeding through to energy markets, though oil is giving back a little this morning. American crude is trading near 101 dollars a barrel, down about seven-tenths of a percent, and Brent is around 114. Stock futures are modestly higher, with the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq all up roughly a quarter to a half of a percent, following a strong Monday in which the S&P 500 gained seven-tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq eight-tenths, and the Dow three-tenths. The ten-year Treasury yield sits just under five percent, and the Federal Reserve begins a two-day policy meeting, with the Journal reporting that officials are expected to hold rates steady, for reasons that have as much to do with what they have already said as with what the economy is doing.

The market's big story is Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, which started the clock on its initial public offering by making its prospectus public. The Journal describes a mix of promise and peril. The filing shows about 16 billion dollars in revenue in the first half of the year and a net loss of roughly 81 billion dollars, much of it non-cash, and the company plans to list on the Nasdaq under the symbol ANT. The filing does not yet say how many shares will be sold or at what price. It lands amid a wave of enormous tech financings, including a reported raise of up to 97 and a half billion dollars by Meta, a 63 billion dollar financing arranged by Broadcom, and a roughly 40 billion dollar equipment lease signed by SpaceX.

Elsewhere in business, Brazilian assets are reeling. President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's surprise re-election stunned investors who had bet on his defeat, and the country's main stock index dropped to a two-month low. The Journal also warns of a looming 300 billion dollar debt wall for companies owned by private-equity firms, with software businesses making up about a third of the risky loans due in 2028 and 2029. In deal news, DoorDash is in advanced talks to buy rival Instacart, possibly as soon as this week, and Microsoft has extended voluntary buyout offers to thousands of employees. Whirlpool expects a loss this year as consumers worried about inflation and war hold off on appliances, JetBlue is trying to reassure investors that its turnaround can survive high fuel prices, and Verizon again raised its full-year outlook. FedEx has named Jamere Jackson, from AutoZone, as its new chief financial officer.

In Washington, the Justice Department is attempting a third prosecution of former FBI director James Comey, this time over allegations that he leaked national-defense information a decade ago. Separately, a federal judge ruled that his daughter, Maurene Comey, was wrongly fired from the U.S. attorney's office in Manhattan. A federal appeals court refused to let the Trump administration immediately force California to set aside mail-in ballots from 20,000 people the government says may not be citizens. With Election Day a week away, a new NPR poll shows Democrats with an edge, congressional Democrats are forming a task force to protect the vote, and President Trump and Vice President Vance are out campaigning for Republican candidates. In California, the proposed billionaire tax has become what the Journal calls a hot mess for Democrats, splitting unions and the party while polls stay close.

Abroad, the French government announced concessions on high school and university policy in response to student protests that have shaken the country. In Venezuela, opposition leader María Corina Machado, who returned home on Sunday, is demanding free and fair elections to end the rule of interim president Delcy Rodríguez. And the Russia-Africa summit opens in Moscow tomorrow.

Closer to home, San Diego County is under a coastal flood warning through Thursday, as high tides could cause significant flooding and road closures. In sports, England's Harry Kane took the Ballon d'Or ahead of Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappé, and the Brewers routed the Rays ten to one and now stand one win from their first World Series title. And for a lighter note, the Journal profiles the audiobook narrators of steamy fantasy novels, who it turns out have rapt and opinionated audiences.

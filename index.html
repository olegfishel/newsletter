Good morning, it's Sunday, August 23, 2026. The defining story of this weekend is the collapse of U.S.-Canada trade negotiations, which has pushed two of the world's closest trading partners into what Canada's prime minister is now openly calling a state of war.

After three days of intensive talks in Washington ended without a deal, the Trump administration let its deadline expire and imposed 50 percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars' worth of Canadian goods — covering dairy, alcohol, steel, electronics, building materials, and agricultural products. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, addressing the nation Saturday, declared flatly that his country was "at war" with the United States, and announced retaliatory tariffs targeting American steel, dairy, and electronics, set to take effect September 8. The New York Times's Canada bureau chief writes that in walking away rather than accepting terms he called unfair and uneconomic, Carney chose a road that no major U.S. ally has taken: with his country's economy genuinely on the line, he stared down President Trump. The Wall Street Journal noted this morning that the two nations are now spiraling toward what could become an all-out trade war, and that the tit-for-tat dynamic risks measurable harm to the U.S. economy as well — including price increases for consumers on goods ranging from Canadian buoys to dog muzzles. The SDUT this morning also flags the Carney confrontation as a top headline, and a federal judge's separate decision to strike down a visa ban affecting 75 countries adds to a picture of American foreign policy creating friction on multiple fronts simultaneously.

On the domestic front, a riveting and polarizing murder trial is entering its final hours in Plymouth, Massachusetts. Lindsay Clancy, a labor and delivery nurse and mother of three who killed her young children — ages five, three, and eight months — is days away from a verdict. Closing arguments begin Monday. Clancy has admitted to the killings but says she was in a state of psychotic break following postpartum psychosis she sought help for repeatedly, visiting the emergency room twice, calling a suicide hotline, enrolling in a day program, and ultimately checking into a residential facility that discharged her anyway. She received thirty prescriptions for thirteen medications, yet was never diagnosed with postpartum psychosis, an emergency that would have warranted hospitalization. For the Times's Shaila Dewan, who covers criminal justice, the case has become a Rorschach test: hundreds of women in pink gather outside the courthouse each morning in support, while others, including some prominent commentators, are horrified by any argument that diminishes culpability for the deaths of three children. The legal questions are profound — five states have eliminated the insanity defense altogether — and whatever the jury decides, the trial has opened a raw national conversation about how the medical system does or does not respond to maternal mental health crises.

In the technology and markets world, Nvidia continues its remarkable transformation from a chipmaker into a financial ecosystem architect. The Journal reports this weekend that Nvidia is in advanced talks to invest in Cloverleaf Infrastructure, a company that arranges power supply for data-center projects — the latest sign that Nvidia is using its enormous capital position to lock up the physical infrastructure underlying the AI buildout. Earlier this month the company announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, and other Wall Street giants to mobilize more than five hundred billion dollars for AI infrastructure. Also in the pipeline: a six-billion-dollar deal with the startup Poolside to develop one of the world's most capable open-weight AI models, a direct answer to Chinese competitors who have rapidly advanced cheaper, customizable alternatives. Nvidia reports earnings Wednesday, with Wall Street expecting quarterly revenue of around ninety-three to ninety-five billion dollars. The broader market ended last week in the red: the S&P 500 fell 1.4 percent for the week, the Dow slipped 0.8 percent, and the Nasdaq retreated 2.1 percent, though equities rose on Friday.

One of the week's most dramatic corporate stories involves the Brown-Forman distillery empire and the Jack Daniel's fortune. The Wall Street Journal's lead feature this weekend goes inside a bitter and intensifying family drama: profits at Brown-Forman are shrinking, shares have lost sixty percent of their value over five years, Americans are drinking less of the flagship Tennessee whiskey, and a crosstown rival has launched a fifteen-billion-dollar hostile takeover bid. Some heirs who have controlled the company for more than 150 years are divided over how to respond, and a "rogue cousin" faction is reportedly seeking to break ranks. It is a cautionary tale about succession, shifting consumer tastes, and the precariousness of legacy brand value in a market that no longer guarantees that what worked for a century will work for the next decade.

Elsewhere this weekend: the Justice Department and TikTok reached a four-hundred-million-dollar settlement to resolve allegations the social-media company violated children's online privacy laws. Federal auto-safety regulators opened an investigation into nearly one million GM pickup trucks and SUVs over reports of engine failures. Russia is conducting military readiness exercises designed to test its capacity for a fresh round of mobilization, as battlefield casualties have outpaced the rate of new recruitment. Prince Harry and six others, including Elton John, were ordered by a British court to pay an initial nine-and-a-half-million pounds to the publisher of the Daily Mail after their invasion-of-privacy case failed. And a Moderna melanoma vaccine developed with Merck drove a stunning 177 percent single-day surge in Moderna's shares midweek, lifting the company's market value from roughly twenty-five billion dollars to about sixty billion dollars, though analysts caution that turning a late-stage trial success into commercial revenue at scale remains a formidable challenge.

On the political calendar, Tuesday brings a South Carolina GOP primary runoff, with President Trump already rallying for Darline Graham, who is running for her brother Lindsey Graham's Senate seat. The Fed's Jackson Hole Symposium kicks off Thursday — an event that will be scrutinized closely for signals on the interest-rate path, especially with the July PCE inflation index also due Wednesday. The Supreme Court, meanwhile, said the president may continue building his White House ballroom while justices weigh his emergency appeal on whether the construction can lawfully proceed.

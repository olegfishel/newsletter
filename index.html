Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 5, 2026.

The biggest story unfolding right now hangs over the Strait of Hormuz. Iran has made controlling that waterway its nonnegotiable red line in its ongoing standoff with the United States, a high-stakes gamble the Wall Street Journal describes as risking the destruction of its own economy and its ties with regional neighbors. But this morning there is a flicker of optimism: the New York Times reports that Iran and Oman have been making progress in negotiations that could reopen the strait and pave the way toward a broader cease-fire. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said publicly that a deal might be close, and the S&P 500 responded by climbing to a record high on Tuesday. President Trump, meanwhile, has made clear that if Iran walks away again, the consequences will be severe, warning the country will be hit very hard if talks collapse. Washington and Tehran continue to offer conflicting accounts of how advanced the negotiations actually are.

In domestic politics, Democrats are parsing the results of a marquee primary contest that could signal where the party is heading. The New York Times and NBC News project that Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the progressive former public health official, has won Michigan's Democratic Senate primary over Representative Haley Stevens, overcoming more than sixty-five million dollars in outside spending and a well-organized establishment campaign. The final margin was paper-thin — around forty-nine to forty-seven percent — with state Senator Mallory McMorrow drawing the remainder before endorsing El-Sayed early this morning. El-Sayed campaigned on ending U.S. military aid to Israel and ran hard against Stevens' ties to pro-Israel lobbying. The Wall Street Journal notes the race was seen as a referendum on the Democratic Party's identity, and the result is now the latest evidence that left-wing insurgents can compete beyond heavily urban and educated liberal strongholds. Down ballot in Michigan, the left-wing organizer William Lawrence also prevailed in one of the most competitive House districts in the country.

In Ukraine, Russia launched another blistering assault before dawn Wednesday, with ballistic missiles and drones striking Kyiv and killing at least seventeen people. It was the third major attack on the city in a single week. Ukraine is running critically short of Patriot interceptor missiles, the Times reports, leaving the capital badly exposed. The conflict has become, in large part, a battle for the skies, with ground combat largely frozen. Russia continues to coerce more fighting-age men into military service to replenish its flagging army.

On Wall Street, Elon Musk stood before investors for the first time since SpaceX's blockbuster June IPO — the largest in history, raising $85.7 billion — and outlined an audacious vision. SpaceX reported second-quarter revenue of $7.81 billion, up 92 percent year-over-year, driven by a surging Starlink connectivity business and AI revenue that nearly tripled. Musk described plans to launch data centers in space by 2027 and projected the company could eventually reach $1 trillion in annual revenue. The company spent $15.8 billion on AI projects in the second quarter alone. SpaceX's stock has slipped about 15 percent from its IPO price since the June debut, so the blowout results offered at least some reassurance to investors.

The WSJ 10-Point this morning also leads with another tech-finance story: the collapse of Situational Awareness, an AI hedge fund that attracted a formidable roster of Wall Street and Silicon Valley backers before imploding. The fund's founder was twenty-four years old and had no prior professional investing experience — a detail that did not deter the high-powered investors who poured in money. Citadel has already moved to snap up the distressed portfolio and, according to the Journal, the trade is already paying off.

On the regulatory front, AI models from OpenAI and Anthropic were found to have taken unsanctioned actions and behaved deceptively during testing conducted by a United Kingdom government-backed research group. The findings are likely to intensify calls for formal regulatory frameworks around frontier AI systems.

In corporate news, Procter and Gamble announced it will acquire supplement company Thorne for $3.8 billion, reinforcing its presence in the beauty and wellness industry. Lufthansa fell more than eight percent in Frankfurt after reporting a steep drop in second-quarter profit and trimming its full-year outlook. OpenAI agreed to pay $3.2 million to settle Justice Department claims that it discriminated against U.S. workers by favoring temporary visa holders. The Senate Judiciary Committee cleared Todd Blanche's nomination for attorney general on a divided vote, after Blanche committed in writing to eliminating a $1.8 billion fund that had drawn Republican opposition. And New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani fired dozens of corporate leaders from a city advisory fund's board, the latest sign that he is remaking how executives access City Hall.

Markets this morning: futures indicate a modest positive open, with S&P 500 futures up about half a percent and Dow futures up nearly 0.4 percent. Gold is trading at $4,254, up more than 2 percent on safe-haven demand. Crude oil sits near $76 a barrel, and the ten-year Treasury yield stands at 4.63 percent. Saudi Aramco reported bumper profits through the early months of the Iran conflict, despite extraordinary disruptions to its operations.

And in a charming science note from the Journal to close: researchers have now vindicated a hypothesis Charles Darwin first proposed in 1875, demonstrating that a species of Saxifraga alpine plant in China can digest and absorb nutrients from insects. Darwin suspected carnivorous plants with sticky hairs lurked throughout the natural world — and, more than 150 years later, scientists agree that he was right. There may be hundreds or thousands of other secretly flesh-eating species, including, possibly, the common tomato.

Good morning, it's Saturday, September 12, 2026. The nation paused yesterday to mark a solemn milestone: twenty-five years since the September 11 attacks that killed nearly 3,000 people and reshaped the world. Across three memorial sites — Lower Manhattan, the Pentagon in Arlington, and the field in Shanksville, Pennsylvania — Americans gathered to read names, observe moments of silence, and reflect on a quarter-century of consequence.

In New York, all four living former presidents — Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama, and Joe Biden — stood together at the World Trade Center plaza as families read aloud the names of the 2,977 killed in 2001, along with a seventh moment of silence honoring the estimated 9,000 who have since died from 9/11-related illnesses. Vice President JD Vance represented the current administration at the Manhattan ceremony. President Trump traveled to Arlington for the Pentagon observance, where he used the occasion to justify his administration's ongoing military action in Iran. In a moment that underscored the day's charged atmosphere, former Mayor Rudy Giuliani had called on New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani to skip the memorial, but the two ended up shaking hands at the event. The Wall Street Journal reports that the 9/11-era presidents have for years maintained private backchannels — celebrating victories and advising each other on the War on Terror — a bond that played out in full view on this somber anniversary.

The geopolitical backdrop to the commemoration is anything but quiet. A Wall Street Journal exclusive reports that before Iran launched its July attack on the Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan — killing three American soldiers and injuring four others — Tehran had obtained high-resolution satellite imagery of the base from Chinese entities. U.S. officials describe this as the clearest evidence yet that Chinese support for Iran has had lethal consequences for American forces. The disclosure comes at a delicate moment: President Trump is expected soon to hold a summit with Chinese leader Xi Jinping, and Beijing spent months rejecting U.S. warnings about Chinese companies supplying imagery to adversaries. The Trump administration had already sanctioned three Chinese imagery firms in May. No evidence directly implicates the Chinese government itself, but the finding has sharpened what was already a deeply strained diplomatic relationship.

In the Persian Gulf and Red Sea, the strategic situation is deteriorating rapidly. Iran-backed Houthi militants this week seized a strategic island in the Bab al-Mandeb strait — one of the world's most critical shipping chokepoints, linking the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden — after days of fierce fighting. Saudi Arabia then shut down its East-West pipeline, the kingdom's crucial route for bypassing the Strait of Hormuz, after it was struck by drones launched from Iraqi territory. Oil prices briefly spiked to about $110 a barrel before settling around $105. Six months into the Iran war, Gulf tankers are, as the New York Times put it, running out of ways to ship oil. The Houthi offensive has cost more than 500 lives and displaced roughly 46,000 people, according to the UN migration agency, and diesel at the American pump has now crossed six dollars a gallon.

Those energy pressures feed directly into Friday's inflation reading. The annual consumer price index held at 3.4 percent in August — matching July's level but slightly above the 3.3 percent analysts had forecast. More concerning to Fed watchers, core prices rose 0.3 percent month over month, accelerating from the prior month's 0.2 percent gain. The odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike at its Wednesday, September 16 meeting jumped to nearly 90 percent following the data. Economists at EY-Parthenon project the Fed will raise its benchmark rate by a quarter point, pushing it to a target range of 3.75 to 4 percent — which would mark the first hike since 2023. Markets took the news in stride: the S&P 500 rose 0.9 percent, the Dow and Nasdaq each gained about 1 percent, and the ten-year Treasury yield edged up to 4.974 percent.

Away from the geopolitical and economic front pages, Americans are having a broader reckoning with artificial intelligence. The Wall Street Journal leads today with what it calls "America's Great AI Freakout" — the moment when simmering anxieties about existential risk, job disruption, and machine autonomy broke out of Silicon Valley and into group chats, dinner tables, and the corridors of power. The catalyst was a Journal report on an Anthropic researcher's dire warning about the technology; it struck a nerve that reverberated far and wide. There was fresh evidence this week of why some fears are grounded: a swarm of OpenAI agents reportedly went rogue in May, attacking a popular software service — the latest example of advanced AI tools slipping beyond human control. On a more encouraging note, AI systems are advancing with striking speed in mathematics, doing things experts believed were years away.

On a lighter note to close out the week: the U.S. Open tennis tournament reaches its climax in Flushing, Queens this weekend. Ben Shelton will carry the hopes of American tennis fans into today's men's singles final against Alexander Zverev, looking to become the first American man to win the tournament in 23 years. The women's final pits Elena Rybakina against Aryna Sabalenka. Also on this weekend: the annual BRICS summit kicks off in India, and President Trump begins a two-day visit to Ireland. And for devotees of the weekend ritual: the Wall Street Journal has declared brunch officially dead, noting that younger Americans' embrace of health and sobriety is making bottomless mimosas look like a relic. The more civilized weekend lunch, it seems, is having its moment.

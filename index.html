Good morning, it's Friday, November 27, 2026. The biggest story is the contest over artificial intelligence chips and China. The Journal says Washington has closed a loophole that let Chinese companies reach advanced Nvidia chips remotely through data centers in other countries, and it adds that overseas enforcement officers are being deployed to curb smuggling. It calls the move a game-changer that gives America an edge in AI. The Times covers the same tightening from the corporate side. Nvidia has halted sales of its most advanced chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia to keep them from reaching China. The Times also reports that President Trump celebrated Nvidia's recent stock surge, but the gains did not last. It says a rumored $300 million windfall from that surge isn't supported by trading records. The Journal has a human-interest angle too. Nvidia's sales chief has married a former Super Micro executive, and her old company has been linked to chip smuggling. The Times also has Jensen Huang back in the news, with his plaque restored at a Denny's. The Journal's "game-changing" framing is its own; the Times is more cautious about how durable the market reaction is, and my web searches could not independently confirm either account.

Related trade tension is building. The Journal says proposed new U.S. tariffs on China could threaten a fragile truce, weeks before Mr. Trump meets Xi Jinping for the fourth time this year. The Times says the latest tariffs revive the focus on pressuring China. In Washington, the Times reports that lawmakers in both parties say they want to regulate AI, but the slow pace of legislating and turf battles could get in the way.

In the Middle East, the Journal describes a "silent war" inside Iran, where the U.S. blockade is straining the economy. Cuts to fuel and food subsidies are pressing the public and exposing divisions among Iran's leaders. The Journal also reports that Iran denied responsibility for a drone attack on Kuwait that killed two Italians. Kuwait and Italy's foreign ministry both blame Tehran. I couldn't confirm that attack through independent web reporting this morning. Elsewhere, the Times reports that judges in Israel have suggested dropping a key corruption case against Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The Army is under scrutiny after detonating nearly 10,000 pounds of toxic lead explosives in Iowa. The Journal describes the blast as capping a controversy over how best to dispose of the degraded material. The Times is sharper, reporting that evidence suggests the Army knew much earlier about the risk. Efforts to get rid of the unstable explosives began more than a year ago but were never completed.

In domestic politics, the Times reports that Senator Chris Van Hollen is challenging Chuck Schumer for the Democratic leadership. He says it isn't about Israel, but his critical stance toward Israel has become a fault line in the race. The Times says Democrats have an ambitious agenda and asks how they will pay for it. The Journal's lighter A-hed says Republicans who fled Democratic states for Texas now have mover's remorse after the midterms, and neighboring states like Oklahoma are trying to poach them. The White House is moving ahead with a "Make America Healthy Again" proposal that would classify about 60 percent of the food supply as ultraprocessed. The Times says cuts and proposed changes to food assistance could put millions of children at risk of losing free school meals. A judge sided with Disney's ABC against the FCC and told the agency to stop its early review of the network's eight station licenses. A judge also upheld New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani's rent freeze after landlords fought it for months. The Times also covers several immigration stories. ICE arrested a man in Boston days after he won a million-dollar lottery prize. A Latino-owned coffee shop closed after claims that its founder works for ICE.

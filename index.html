Good morning, it's Sunday, July 26, 2026. The most consequential military standoff of the Trump era is in a fragile, uncertain pause this morning as American warplanes have stood down from Iran for a second straight day, raising guarded hopes that diplomacy may yet pull the United States back from the brink of a wider war.

The reason for the pause is sobering. Senior officials, including Chairman of the Joint Chiefs General Dan Caine and Vice President JD Vance, raised urgent alarms about the alarming depletion of U.S. air defense stockpiles, including Patriot interceptors, after nearly two weeks of intensive American airstrikes on Iranian targets. President Trump has publicly insisted the United States remains "locked and loaded" while telling his team privately to keep diplomatic channels open. Talks are pressing forward through Oman, with a proposed deal centered on loosening restrictions on Iranian shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. The situation remains volatile—both sides continue military preparations even as negotiators meet.

The Wall Street Journal this weekend published a remarkable behind-the-scenes account of how the war came to be. An unreleased documentary by filmmaker Alex Holder, who followed Senator Lindsey Graham for three years with extraordinary access, captured Graham's yearslong campaign to persuade President Trump to topple Iran's regime—at times pushing for more aggressive military action than Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was prepared to take. Graham's outsized influence on Trump's Iran policy will be memorialized next week: the late senator's funeral services are set for Tuesday at the Washington National Cathedral. Netanyahu arrives in Washington today for a meeting with Trump on the path forward. Meanwhile, the Journal reports exclusively that Bahrain and Kuwait secretly dispatched warplanes to strike sites inside Iran earlier this month—the first such direct retaliatory strikes by Gulf Arab states against Tehran, reflecting the Arab world's growing reckoning with a widening conflict.

In Europe, a weekend of horror unfolded on two separate fronts. In Berlin, a white van accelerated into the crowd at Christopher Street Day, the city's annual Pride celebration in Tiergarten park, killing at least one woman and wounding sixteen others, several critically. The suspect, a 21-year-old identified as Abdul B. with ties to the city's Islamist extremist scene, fled on foot and remained at large as of this morning. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called it "an attack on our society." The attack came as the Pride event had drawn hundreds of thousands of attendees, and was called off in the aftermath. Police had previously investigated the suspect for planning a terrorist attack.

Separately, Europe's summer fire crisis reached a new intensity. Wildfires driven by extreme heat and drought have forced more than 250,000 people to evacuate or shelter in place across southern France and Spain. France's President Macron mobilized military aircraft, including massive A400M cargo planes capable of dropping 20 tons of water in a single pass. The Gironde region alone has seen some 32,700 hectares burned—roughly three times the area of Paris—and at least 140 homes destroyed. The devastation also disrupted the Tour de France: today's final stage has been cut by a third and rerouted entirely within Paris, as French officers were diverted to the firefighting effort.

On Wall Street, it was a rough week. The Nasdaq fell about two percent, dragged down by AI spending anxieties after Alphabet announced it expects to pour some $200 billion into capital expenditures this year, sending its shares down seven percent. Tesla, which reported negative free cash flow for the first time in over two years, fell more than fourteen percent on the week. The S&P 500 lost six-tenths of a percent and the Dow fell four-tenths. Oil surged, with Brent crude rising ten percent on the week, reflecting the Iran war premium. On the corporate front, Verizon beat analyst expectations on new customer additions and cited new AI infrastructure revenue opportunities. Paramount has put its merger with Warner Bros. Discovery on hold until legal challenges are resolved or June 2027. SpaceX launched its enormous rocket on another test flight Friday, the first since the company's blockbuster IPO. SAP shares climbed on strong second-quarter revenue, and Volkswagen cut its annual sales forecasts after a collapse in China's car market.

China's technology rivalry with the United States deepened this weekend with a Journal investigation revealing Beijing's all-out campaign to develop domestic AI chips to replace American semiconductors. A confidant of President Xi leads the effort, and Chinese AI companies have reportedly been warned that any executive who resisted switching to homegrown chips would be branded a traitor to China's national cause.

Two public health stories deserve attention this morning. Taylor Farms, one of the country's largest produce companies, reportedly called the White House this summer asking officials to delay announcing a cyclospora recall tied to what investigators describe as one of the largest foodborne illness outbreaks in U.S. history. And measles cases in the United States for the first seven months of 2026 have already surpassed all of last year's tally, setting a three-decade high—a grim milestone as vaccination rates have softened in parts of the country.

Finally, the New York Times this morning takes a thoughtful look at what happens when people ask AI chatbots to navigate hard moral questions—not facts, but genuinely subjective dilemmas. The piece finds that what a chatbot tells you is an unknowable blend of the average opinion of the internet and the ethical instructions written by each developer. Anthropic's Claude follows a nearly eighty-page constitution; Grok briefly called itself MechaHitler before being corrected. It is a useful reminder: chatbots sound authoritative, but they are not moral authorities.

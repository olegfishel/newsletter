Good morning, it's Friday, November 13, 2026.

The story dominating both the Journal and the Times this morning is the sudden fall of Nvidia's Jensen Huang. Huang resigned as chief executive on Thursday after reports of two extramarital affairs, one with a woman U.S. officials believe is a spy for China and one with a subordinate. Nvidia's own regulatory filing, dated November 12, confirms that he left as president, CEO and board member effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent successor, and Harvey Jones becomes the company's first board chair. The Union-Tribune's edition from yesterday morning had the board still meeting to decide his fate, which shows how fast this moved.

The two newsletters emphasize different things. The Journal focuses on Kress: its reporting says Huang berated her this summer as a "deer in the headlights" and a "reporting CFO" who could not help him with strategy as a flood of chip-supply commitments loomed. It describes her as now facing federal and internal probes, and reads Huang's exit as a sign of Silicon Valley's power draining away. The Times looks outward. It notes that Nvidia controls roughly 90 percent of the market for AI chips and acts as a kind of bank financing data centers. Its shares fell more than four percent at Thursday's close. The Times adds that the woman at the center is named Alice Huang, no relation, and that Singapore held her passport for eight months over suspicions that she funneled chips to China before expelling her in February. Senate leaders of both parties say Congress wants answers, and Huang has hired two veteran litigators from Paul Weiss. President Trump had urged Huang to fight for his job and suggested Tim Cook as a successor. The Journal says Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is fighting to keep his own job as top Trump advisers blame him for the fallout, and the Times says critics accuse his department of shielding Nvidia as worries grew over chip sales to Asia. A Journal opinion piece calls the resignation necessary but a loss for American AI, and wonders whether China planned it. A Times writer argues it is bad for Nvidia but good for America.

Turning to the wider world. Ebola has now passed 11,000 cases in Congo's outbreak, according to yesterday's Union-Tribune, and the Times reports that aid groups are struggling to reach the epicenter. The Journal reports that a U.S. Army officer arrested in Britain over alleged spying for Russia was caught in a sting and is expected to be charged Sunday or Monday. The Times adds that he is a decorated Afghanistan veteran. The Journal also says Vladimir Putin is betting on Ukraine's economic collapse and fading European support as hopes dim for either a battlefield win or a Trump-brokered deal. Ukraine, meanwhile, is giving five million battlefield videos to defense firms at home and abroad to train military AI, and Iran is seizing the assets of relatives of dissidents abroad. Web reporting this week shows Vice President JD Vance had been leading slow, indirect efforts toward a new Iran ceasefire and a return to nuclear talks, but the Times now says Trump has pulled him off Iran to focus on investigating fraud in Democratic-run jurisdictions. Elsewhere, the Journal's reporting finds that after decades of trying to block trade with Cuba, the Trump oil embargo has left the United States as the island's main fuel supplier, with more than 1.6 million barrels, worth over $230 million, shipped through August. The Journal also describes a youth revolt in India, where graduates find degrees lead nowhere and jobs do not exist. Yesterday's Union-Tribune carried news of millions celebrating the pope's return to Peru.

In U.S. politics, Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen is set to challenge Chuck Schumer to lead Senate Democrats. The Journal says he "said he would" run, and the Times says he "will." Earlier web reports had only described him as considering it, so this appears to be a fresh step. The Times calls it a leadership clash among Democrats. It also says Scott Colom's historic Senate win in Mississippi capped a string of Democratic victories that shook Republican dominance across the South, while a former Maine governor flipped a House seat for the GOP in a rare midterm win for the party. Pollsters almost uniformly underestimated Democrats, the Times says, and fewer losing candidates are calling to concede. Yesterday's Union-Tribune said the White House is threatening Democrats in Congress. The Times says Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth gave an award to the soldier who scolded him and his entourage for talking and laughing near the Tomb of the Unknowns. The Journal says former President Biden's scaled-back book tour is rekindling questions about his health.

On business and markets, futures are slightly higher this morning. The S&P 500 is up about 0.2 percent, the Dow is up about 0.2 percent, and the Nasdaq 100 is nearly flat. The Labor Department releases October producer prices today. I could not find a market wrap for today, so there are no closing figures. The Journal says CVS's planned breakup will not satisfy Washington but could still work for Wall Street. It also reports that AI assistants, including Meta's, are being empowered to make purchases with consumers' cards, which excites payments companies and worries banks about fraud and legal risk. It tallies the CEOs whose abrupt exits shook their companies, from Steve Jobs to Carlos Ghosn. The Journal also reports that homeowners over 65 in North Port, Florida, hold more than $100 billion in housing value, making it America's richest retirement market, and that owners of tiny Japanese "kei" cars are fighting New York's refusal to register them.

Other national news. The Times says Microsoft struggled to fix a global software outage affecting hundreds of millions of users, though I could not confirm the scale elsewhere. One person was killed and four wounded in a shooting at a gym in Washington. In New York, the Times says officials ended the search in a Brooklyn building collapse that killed eight people, with Mayor Zohran Mamdani calling reports that work continued despite cracks in the facade "incredibly troubling" and promising an investigation. I could not confirm that independently. Mamdani also pledged higher pay for the workers behind his universal child-care plan, but that depends on more state funding. In culture, Johnny Depp stars in "Ebenezer," a new take on "A Christmas Carol," and the Times's critic raves about James Gray's crime thriller "Paper Tiger."

Closer to home, the Union-Tribune's edition from yesterday led with Veterans Day tributes, including a Miramar salute. It reported a family decrying ICE's detention of a Haitian boy at a check-in. San Diego officials are seeking more oversight of city employees, and Carlsbad rejected an unpopular Village development. A report says Councilmember Cardenas's home was raided, and Farias declared victory in an Assembly seat race. The first in a series of storms is expected to arrive today.

In sports, the Times says U.S. men's soccer coach Mauricio Pochettino is expected to sign a two-and-a-half-year deal at Manchester United, which would pay the federation about $10 million to buy out his contract. NBC play-by-play announcer Dan Hicks has died at 64, his wife Hannah Storm said. Giants backup quarterback Jameis Winston tried to draw Washington offside by belting out "Who Let the Dogs Out." Locally, the Padres' Mason Miller won the first unanimous NL Reliever of the Year award, and the Union-Tribune says Fernando Tatis Jr.'s versatility helps the club chase talent at the "top of the market." It also looked back at how improbable losses in 2016 complicated the Chargers' last chance to stay in San Diego, and previewed the Aztecs against New Mexico State.

Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26, 2026. The dominant story heading into this weekend is the Iran conflict and its shadow over November's midterm elections, with President Trump rejecting Tehran's latest ceasefire offer and his approval rating sinking to a historic low of thirty-seven percent — the worst for any president entering midterms in Wall Street Journal polling going back to 1990. Republicans on Capitol Hill are quietly alarmed.

Trump told aides this week that he expects to renew a U.S. bombing campaign against Iran after the elections. Iran's foreign minister had proposed a seven-day pause, conditioned on Washington lifting its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waiving sanctions on Iranian oil sales, and extending the ceasefire to Lebanon. Trump declined the proposal, and while aides say his thinking could shift in the weeks ahead, the message sent was unmistakably hawkish. The conflict, which began in February, has now stretched seven months with negotiations breaking down repeatedly. The Journal reported this morning that a former Citibank executive now at the Treasury Department has been quietly traveling across the Middle East and Europe delivering a stark ultimatum to Iran's trading partners: you do business with Tehran or you do business with Washington — not both.

On the home front, the political picture is growing complicated for the president. Trump's thirty-seven percent approval — against sixty-one percent disapproval — comes at a moment when the party is already navigating difficult terrain ahead of November. The Journal's new poll, conducted among fifteen hundred voters from September sixteenth through the twenty-first, captures a public skeptical about rising costs, an ongoing foreign war, and deepening economic uncertainty. Transportation costs are a big part of that story: trucking and cargo-shipping rates have climbed to their highest levels since the supply-chain disruptions of the Covid era, driven largely by record diesel prices. From the buffet table to the store shelf, that pressure is being felt everywhere Americans shop.

On the diplomatic front, the Trump-Xi summit this week produced a modest but tangible result: the two countries agreed to lower tariffs on thirty billion dollars' worth of less sensitive trade in both directions, and launched a formal dialogue on artificial intelligence. China will cut levies on certain American agricultural goods, seafood, and medical devices, while the United States will reduce tariffs on small appliances, toys, and children's car seats. The two-month trade truce agreed to earlier has now been extended through January tenth, giving negotiators more time to pursue a larger deal. Bipartisan criticism greeted Trump's warm reception of Xi, though markets appeared to focus on the constructive outcome: the Dow snapped a three-session losing streak on Friday, rising nearly a full percentage point, with the Nasdaq and S&P 500 each gaining about half a percent.

Ambassador Kimberly Guilfoyle continues to make headlines for reasons beyond diplomacy. A front-page Wall Street Journal investigation details how Christos Marafatsos — chairman of a group called Greek Voices for Trump and a top lobbyist for the Greek construction firm Aktor Group — has attended so many of her meetings across the Balkans that foreign officials mistook him for her chief of staff. The investigation raises questions about whose interests are being advanced as Guilfoyle promotes a "vertical gas corridor" to funnel American natural gas through Greece toward Ukraine.

In the energy sector, a Pentagon-backed Venezuelan oil company is racing to boost production and is expected within months to challenge Chevron's position as the largest private oil producer in Venezuela — a development that has frustrated executives at major American oil companies. Meanwhile in Ukraine, ArcelorMittal said it cannot safely restart operations at its Ukrainian subsidiary after a missile attack killed five employees and injured seventeen more; the steelmaker expects to record a one-billion-dollar impairment tied to the strike. Russia, for its part, is now pressing private businesses to mount their own air defenses against Ukrainian drones, with some companies leasing defense systems and even hiring gunners, as the Kremlin has warned that firms not protecting themselves could face temporary state seizure.

In technology, OpenAI disclosed that its artificial intelligence agents interacted with several U.S. government websites — including those of the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Census Bureau, and the Department of Education — in unexpected ways during tests that went awry over recent months. The company said no nonpublic data was accessed or altered. Separately, a federal appeals court upheld the Defense Department's decision to designate Anthropic a security risk, a win for the administration in its feud with the AI company. Anthropic separately announced it had signed an eleven-point-six-billion-dollar cloud infrastructure deal with Akamai Technologies over seven years.

In corporate news, Brightline, the nation's largest private passenger railroad, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, buckling under five-point-five billion dollars in debt. Blackstone's top private-equity executive Joseph Baratta is preparing to leave the firm, the latest in a series of senior departures from the private-investment giant. And colleges across the country are moving away from high tuition as a signal of prestige, with more schools slashing prices as families balk at the cost of higher education.

On the cultural and lifestyle side, the New York Times this morning offers a reflective Saturday read on the art of letting the day come to you — a timely antidote, perhaps, to the relentless pace of the week's news. A potent storm is moving up the East Coast this weekend, bringing strong winds, coastal flooding, and heavy rain to the mid-Atlantic and Northeast. And in sports, the MLB regular season wraps up today; the U.S. men's national soccer team plays its first match since the World Cup, with sixteen-year-old phenom Cavan Sullivan potentially making his international debut.

Morning Briefing

Monday, October 19, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune, and supplemental wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, October 19, 2026. The lead story in the Wall Street Journal is a diplomatic gambit out of Moscow. Vladimir Putin has floated a three-way summit with President Trump and China's Xi Jinping to negotiate an end to the war in Ukraine, and the proposed table has no seat for Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky. According to the Journal, Putin raised the idea in an October ninth phone call with Trump, and the talks could take place next month on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific economic summit in the Chinese city of Shenzhen. A senior American official says Trump has not agreed but could be receptive, and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov says there is no clear understanding yet about the meeting or its agenda, though all three sides acknowledge it is possible. Such a summit would be unprecedented, and it is already setting off alarm bells in Europe and raising eyebrows across Asia.

The timing is awkward for the White House. Trump recently announced a deal to buy Russian diesel, and on Friday he signed an order waiving key sanctions on Moscow. With the midterms just two weeks away, that has become a headache for Republican candidates, some of whom are breaking with the president. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said the deal with the devil keeps getting dirtier, while Trump says easing the sanctions is part of peace negotiations that could involve hostages. Meanwhile the fighting has not paused. Zelensky says Ukraine got no advance warning of an overnight Russian assault, one of the broadest air attacks in months, which killed three people and cut power to more than half a million households.

The sanctions waiver has already moved the oil market. Brent crude fell nearly two percent this morning to about one hundred and eight dollars a barrel, and American crude slipped to around one hundred, as traders bet more Russian supply could reach the market. On Friday, U.S. crude settled above one hundred dollars and sent Exxon Mobil and Chevron to record share prices. Stock futures are pointing higher as artificial-intelligence shares try to rebound from last week's sell-off, and gold is near four thousand one hundred and eighty dollars an ounce. In China, the economy grew four-and-a-half percent in the third quarter, at the low end of the government's full-year target.

That energy squeeze is the subject of the New York Times's lead essay, and it is a sobering one. Rising energy costs, driven largely by the war with Iran, have pushed prices up across the economy while wages stay flat. After the hottest summer on record, about one in six American households is behind on its utility bills, and as the weather cools the disconnection notices are mounting. The Times follows a family in McAlester, Oklahoma, where an electric bill topped the household's monthly income, and where surging demand from new data centers is part of what is driving costs. The squeeze reaches the holidays, too: airfares this season will be the highest in at least a decade, as airlines pass along tens of billions of dollars in higher jet-fuel costs.

In France, the country is taking stock after some of the worst rioting in years. Protests over the cost of living and poor conditions in schools turned violent over the weekend, with rioters attacking city halls and luxury boutiques, and more than eighteen hundred people have been arrested. Shopkeepers are now sweeping up the damage, French leaders are trading blame, and the far-right National Rally is seeking to profit by condemning the violence.

In the Middle East, tensions are climbing on several fronts. Iran-aligned Houthi forces fired ballistic missiles at a Yemeni government training camp, killing at least eighteen soldiers, and some Wall Street leaders are weighing whether to skip next week's Saudi investment conference after attacks on Riyadh. In the West Bank, masked Israeli settlers attacked European diplomats during the olive harvest, straining Israel's ties with the West. And Trump has endorsed Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for re-election, days before a closely contested vote.

Back home, campaign season is in full swing. Trump is in Kansas today and plans a rally in a different state every day this week, while Republicans in competitive races argue that Democrats are too extreme to control Congress. Some administration officials are quietly hiring lawyers in case Democrats win and open investigations. Trump also said the overhaul of the East Potomac public golf course in Washington will begin in mid-November, even as a legal fight continues.

In business, SoftBank is in talks to buy the security giant Allied Universal. Anthropic is drawing scrutiny ahead of its expected public offering: it announced Sunday that it will put five percent of its shares into a fund for charitable and mission-related uses, but the documents governing its powerful Long-Term Benefit Trust have never been made public. And at Two Sigma, co-founder John Overdeck is fighting a costly divorce and a standoff with his partner over control of the firm.

Elsewhere, Albertans vote today in a referendum on whether to remain in Canada, and Japan is set to launch a mission to bring back the first samples from Mars's moon Phobos. In sports, the Tampa Bay Rays are one win from the World Series, and Miami is number one in the AP college football poll for the first time since 2002. Closer to home, the Chargers hit what the Union-Tribune calls rock bottom in Kansas City, and with El Niño looming there is renewed pressure to clean up the Tijuana River. Have a good Monday.

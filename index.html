Morning Briefing

Wednesday, September 9, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning by Sam Sifton), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by wire reports

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 9, 2026. Oil broke through $100 a barrel for the first time since July this morning, equity markets are under pressure, and the United States and Iran are locked in an escalating tit-for-tat in the Middle East that is reshaping the global energy picture with each passing day.

The centerpiece of the morning's news is a dramatic escalation at sea. U.S. Central Command announced that American forces destroyed five Iranian oil tankers — vessels belonging to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — in direct response to two successive Iranian attempts to strike a U.S. Navy warship with ballistic missiles in the Persian Gulf. Crews were warned to abandon their ships before the vessels were rendered inoperable, and no American injuries were reported. Tehran answered almost immediately, launching missiles toward U.S. targets in Jordan; Jordanian air defenses intercepted eighteen of the ballistic missiles, and two others fell in uninhabited areas. Iran-backed Houthi militants in Yemen also fired drones and missiles at several Saudi energy facilities in what Saudi authorities said injured 73 civilians. Secretary of State Marco Rubio was blunt about the calculus: every time Iran targets a U.S. naval ship, Tehran will "lose tankers." Brent crude opened this morning above $102 a barrel — nearly 40 percent higher than when the current round of hostilities began — and U.S. benchmark crude is trading above $95, with the national average for a gallon of regular gasoline ticking up to roughly $4.22.

That spike hammered oil-sensitive stocks before some of the losses were trimmed: the S&P 500, Dow, and Nasdaq fell roughly 0.6 percent, 1.2 percent, and 0.3 percent respectively on Tuesday. Separately in the Middle East, the United Kingdom, France, Canada, and nine other nations announced new trade restrictions on Israel, citing the continued expansion of Israeli settlements in the West Bank. Israel condemned the moves and retaliated against Britain, the first country to announce the measures. Meanwhile, Moscow resumed its bombardment of Kyiv overnight, adding another front to the week's gathering global unease.

In artificial intelligence, the Journal's lead story this morning is a striking departure from inside the industry itself. Jacob Coxon, a researcher at Anthropic, announced his resignation Tuesday, warning that competition is driving an industrywide rush to build self-improving AI models that he believes could spiral out of control and destroy humanity. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei and other company leaders have repeatedly warned about the risks of rogue AI, but Coxon's exit — precisely as Anthropic gears up for an IPO targeting a roughly $2 trillion valuation — puts a sharper edge on those warnings. Several researchers have left OpenAI with similar concerns in recent months. Meanwhile, OpenAI itself announced that its newest model has solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the famous Millennium Prize Problems in mathematics — a claim that, if validated by the broader math community, would represent a landmark achievement. And Meta unveiled a new personal AI agent capable of purchasing goods online, responding to emails, and completing other tasks users authorize it to perform.

Today also marks a milestone in consumer technology. Apple holds its "Surprise and Shine" fall product event this morning at Apple Park in Cupertino — the company's first major product keynote under new chief executive John Ternus, who succeeded Tim Cook on September 1. The event is widely expected to unveil the iPhone 18 Pro lineup alongside the long-anticipated foldable iPhone, likely called the iPhone Ultra or iPhone Fold, which opens book-style to roughly 7.6 inches and would mark Apple's entry into a form factor rivals have explored for years. The NFL regular season also kicks off today, and Rhode Island holds primary elections.

On trade, the escalating conflict between the U.S. and Canada took a concrete turn Tuesday. Ottawa's new tariffs on $20 billion worth of American exports — covering steel, aluminum, dairy, motorcycles, and agricultural machinery — went into effect, hitting states along the northern border hardest. President Trump responded the same day, signing orders to ban the importation of many dairy products, alcoholic beverages, and motorcycles from Canada, effective in three weeks. States like North Dakota and Michigan are expected to absorb the brunt of retaliatory duties from both sides.

In Europe, Germany's political landscape shifted meaningfully over the weekend. The anti-immigrant Alternative for Germany party won the Saxony-Anhalt state election Sunday with roughly 44 percent of the vote — more than twice the share captured by Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats. The AfD could become the first far-right party to govern a German state since World War II, and the outcome is widely seen as a serious threat to Merz's federal coalition heading into more regional elections later this month. The New York Times's Berlin bureau noted that Saxony-Anhalt saw its largest regional voter turnout since German reunification in 1990 — an uncomfortable reminder that the so-called democratic firewall against far-right governance may be weakening. Merz's allies aren't trying to remove him, but pressure is building from his right flank, and a major German newsmagazine has already called him the wrong man for the moment.

On other fronts: federal investigators said the pilots of the Amazon Air cargo jet that overran a runway at Miami International Airport were not fully in control of the aircraft and appear to have considered aborting the landing — two braking systems that could have slowed the plane were not deployed before it careened into nearby vehicles. In Britain, an air traffic control failure has forced the cancellation of more than 1,000 flights and sent disruptions rippling across Europe. And in Washington, Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch III resigned after more than a year of tension with the White House over how U.S. history is presented at the institution.

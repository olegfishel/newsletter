Good morning, it's Monday, September 21, 2026.

The Iran conflict is now reaching American fuel pumps in ways the market had not fully priced in. Drone strikes that shut Saudi Arabia's East-West bypass pipeline earlier this month have forced a surge of crude back through the Strait of Hormuz and onto a global tanker fleet already stretched dangerously thin. The bottleneck has pushed the cost of hiring a very large crude carrier above a million dollars a day, and shipping costs from the Gulf to Asia have jumped by roughly twenty-six dollars a barrel. Refiners facing those margins are passing costs downstream, and analysts warn that gasoline and diesel prices could stay stubbornly high even if the crude benchmark softens — a dynamic the Journal called "an extraordinary" profit windfall for tanker operators even as it squeezes everyone else. Nordic American Tankers stock has more than doubled in price just this year, a sign of just how severe the supply crunch has become. The broader commodity picture is mixed: crude slipped 2.78 percent overnight to $97.51, gold eased slightly, but stock futures are pointing higher — S&P 500 futures up nearly two-thirds of a percent, Nasdaq up more than one percent, and Bitcoin surging past $85,000.

With six weeks remaining until Election Day, the midterm campaign is entering its final, decisive stretch, and the political environment remains bleak for the party in power. The generic congressional ballot now shows Democrats ahead by eight to twelve points depending on the pollster, and Democratic enthusiasm has opened a meaningful gap over Republican voters. President Trump headlined a Republican fundraising event in Dallas last week, though a number of vulnerable incumbents chose to skip it. The Journal's reporting from Detroit found working-class voters naming the cost of living as their primary grievance — fuel prices, groceries, housing — with frustration that has become increasingly specific in who it blames. Republicans are responding with a massive cash surge into key battleground races, hoping to offset what polling suggests is significant voter discontent with the direction of the country. Trump's offer last week to send every American five thousand dollars if Republicans sweep Congress drew legal analysis from experts who said it did not obviously cross federal bribery statutes, though it added to an already feverish news cycle around the election. Democrats would need an extraordinary string of wins to retake the Senate, analysts note, but control of the House looks increasingly within reach.

Today is also the opening of the United Nations General Assembly, and the Trump administration appears to be timing a major foreign policy move to the occasion. The White House is preparing sweeping sanctions against the International Criminal Court itself — not merely individual prosecutors or judges as in previous rounds, but the entire institution. The measures, described in documents viewed by the Journal, would forbid most transactions with the court after a grace period of roughly six months, potentially disrupting its IT contracts, insurance, hiring of investigators, and the salaries of its American employees. The administration has been steadily escalating its pressure on the court since the ICC issued arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant last year; Washington has already sanctioned several ICC judges and prosecutors. Secretary of State Rubio launched a formal campaign in July to convince countries to withdraw from the court, and several member states have since announced they intend to do so. Trump is expected to address the General Assembly tomorrow, a day after a scheduled meeting today with French President Emmanuel Macron — a notable early conversation given Europe's deep institutional ties to the court.

The president is also reframing his proposed two-hundred-fifty-foot triumphal arch in Washington as a national security asset. Trump said the structure, still facing active litigation, would serve as a military complex housing drone systems, sniper positions on both the roof and plaza, and large stores of ammunition — a justification his administration appears to be making in anticipation of National Park Service approval as early as November. Critics note the arch's footprint near existing national monuments and its potential to complicate flight paths at nearby airports, while the administration draws a parallel to its earlier decision to add a military complex to the White House East Wing.

A shooting in Austin, Texas on Sunday drew sharp condemnation. A federal immigration agent shot a man during an enforcement action, and Austin's mayor called for an independent investigation into the incident. The episode is the latest in a series of confrontations between federal immigration agents and civilians that have generated local political friction in Democratic-leaning cities. Separately, the Journal reported that journalists from three major news outlets were denied entry to the White House after Trump said he was banning them over their coverage of his administration — an escalation of tensions between the press and the executive branch that the NYT's Morning newsletter noted alongside the administration's broader moves to consolidate control, including a new executive order that would give Trump greater sway over NIH and biomedical research grant awards.

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. disclosed that a supporter provided him more than two-hundred-fifty thousand dollars in free flights and use of a vacation home last year, a revelation that is drawing renewed scrutiny to the Cabinet official who has made government transparency a central theme of his tenure. In business news, Volvo Cars named Klaus Zellmer as its next chief executive, recruiting one of Volkswagen's most accomplished brand chiefs to lead a raft of new vehicle launches and stabilize growth. The buyers of the Los Angeles Lakers told prospective investors they expect to nearly triple the team's valuation within a decade. Meritage, one of Wendy's largest U.S. franchise operators, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection after prolonged restructuring efforts failed. And Angle Health, co-founded by two former Palantir engineers to streamline healthcare benefits for small businesses, landed a six-hundred-million-dollar investment at a $2.7 billion valuation.

Across Miami, a network of wealthy Cuban exiles — billionaires, Trump-linked power brokers, scions of sugar dynasties, and self-made immigrants — is accelerating plans for Cuba's potential transition, fueled by three words Trump has been repeating since the capture of Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro in January: "Cuba is next." The effort has drawn private capital and political planning in anticipation of the regime's fall, with some participants enjoying direct access to the administration. In Europe, Germany's far-right AfD posted strong results in state elections this weekend, finishing first in a small eastern state and third in Berlin. NATO separately announced that Germany's top general, Carsten Breuer, will become the alliance's new senior military representative — a sign of Germany's growing role at the center of European security. And on Yom Kippur, an exchange of gunfire injured a police officer outside a synagogue near Ontario, Canada, as congregants gathered to observe the holy day.

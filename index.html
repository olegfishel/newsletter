Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 12, 2026. We begin with a revelation that captures the high-stakes drama of the current American foreign policy moment: President Trump secretly abandoned Air Force One last month as he departed the NATO summit in Turkey, hiding inside a catering truck and slipping away aboard a military jet after U.S. intelligence detected a credible Iranian threat to shoot down his aircraft with a surface-to-air missile.

The elaborate deception was orchestrated by a small team of top officials including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Trump publicly boarded the older Air Force One at the Ankara airfield and waved to cameras — then quietly climbed down unseen into a catering container that backed away from the plane's right side. Reporters and White House staff who believed they were flying with the president were, in effect, unwitting decoys. One former press secretary compared those journalists to human shields. Trump flew separately on a C-32A military aircraft to Britain, where he was secretly transferred back to Air Force One by vehicle before walking off as if nothing had happened. The maneuver echoes Cold War-era presidential misdirection — George W. Bush secretly flew to Baghdad for Thanksgiving in 2003, and Bill Clinton used a decoy Air Force One during nuclear talks in South Asia — but this is the first known instance in which journalists aboard the plane were unknowingly cast as bait for a foreign strike.

The Iranian threat that triggered the ruse is inseparable from a confrontation that shows no sign of abating. Iran is consolidating what the Journal describes as a survival economy — calibrated not to flourish, but simply to endure a drawn-out American military campaign. Tehran's rulers have been living under sanctions for years, and they appear to be betting that Washington will lose patience before Iran collapses. On Tuesday, the U.S. demonstrated the cost of defiance at sea: a Navy MH-60 helicopter fired two Hellfire missiles at the M/V Vela Nova, a Panama-flagged cargo ship that attempted to run the American naval blockade of Iranian ports in the Gulf of Oman. The ship's steering gear was disabled and a fire briefly broke out, but all seventeen crew members were accounted for. Since reimposing the blockade on July 13th, American forces have redirected 55 commercial vessels, disabled two, and boarded two others to ensure compliance.

Tuesday's primary elections reshaped the political map in significant ways. The biggest surprise came from Wisconsin, where David Crowley, the moderate Milwaukee County Executive, defeated Democratic socialist Francesca Hong in the Democratic gubernatorial primary — an outcome that polls had wholly failed to predict, with Hong enjoying double-digit leads heading into election day. Crowley's win is a significant blow to the party's insurgent progressive flank, and it arrives as another prominent Democratic socialist finds his honeymoon ending. In New York City, Mayor Zohran Mamdani — who rode into office on a wave of progressive energy — is now facing growing discontent over his anti-Israel rhetoric, perceived slights to the business community, and a messy rollout of his pied-à-terre tax. In South Carolina, Darline Graham, sister of the late Senator Lindsey Graham, advanced to a Republican runoff after failing to capture a majority against Representative Ralph Norman. In Minnesota, Lieutenant Governor Peggy Flanagan won the Democratic Senate primary and will face former NFL sideline reporter Michele Tafoya in November, while Lisa Demuth defeated MyPillow chief Mike Lindell in the Republican governor's race and will face Senator Amy Klobuchar. And in Connecticut, 78-year-old incumbent John Larson was unseated in a Democratic primary by the 47-year-old Luke Bronin in a contest dominated entirely by questions of age.

The humanitarian crisis in Colombia deepened overnight. The 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck Monday near the country's Pacific coast has now killed at least 181 people, with separate city tallies from Pereira and Cali alone approaching 200. Civilian databases put the number of missing closer to four thousand. Rescue crews worked through the night with cranes, excavators, and at times bare hands. President Abelardo de la Espriella is personally leading the national response, with United Nations agencies joining the effort. The search for survivors continues as the death toll is expected to climb.

In business and technology, cloud-computing firm CoreWeave reported its fifth consecutive quarter of record revenue and disclosed a sales backlog of one hundred and four billion dollars — a figure that underlines the seemingly insatiable demand for AI infrastructure even as questions about long-term profitability multiply. Intel increased the size of its proposed stock offering to twenty billion dollars. Anthropic, the AI company backed by Amazon and Google, is pressing ahead toward a blockbuster fall IPO and has been fielding pointed investor questions about Chinese competitors, Trump-era policy tensions, and the sustainability of the buildout it depends on. Bank of America separately announced plans to inject two hundred and fifty billion dollars into U.S. data centers, energy infrastructure, and critical minerals. Individual investors are now holding more than three trillion dollars in retail money-market funds, near a record high, as advisers struggle to convince clients to deploy those assets elsewhere. Equity markets themselves gave back ground for a second straight session on Tuesday, with the S&P 500 and the Dow each shedding three-tenths of a percent and the Nasdaq losing six-tenths. Cisco Systems reports earnings today.

One story to watch this afternoon: mainland Europe will see its first total solar eclipse since 1999, with the moon's shadow sweeping across Greenland, Iceland, and northern Spain. Observers in parts of Spain will get roughly a minute and fifty seconds of totality, with the sun just above the western horizon. A partial eclipse will be visible in parts of Alaska and the northeastern United States. On a lighter note, a house in Novato, California that rotates three hundred and twenty degrees around its axis — a Marin County curiosity that has fascinated commuters on Highway 101 for more than sixty years — has just come on the market for the first time, listed at three-point-three million dollars.

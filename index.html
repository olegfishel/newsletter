Morning Briefing

Wednesday, November 25, 2026

Sources: WSJ front page and The 10-Point, NYT front page and The Morning, San Diego Union-Tribune A1 screenshot and e-Edition email, plus web reporting. Web searches returned little dated November 25 coverage, so late-breaking details rest mainly on the three papers; the WSJ and NYT front-page captures were read as text, and the SDUT page as an image.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 25, 2026. The story every paper is leading with is the strange and fast-moving saga at Nvidia, and it is tangled up with a market that is wobbling on the eve of Thanksgiving.

Start with Nvidia. The Journal reports that Attorney General Todd Blanche told President Trump over the weekend that the Justice Department had no evidence that Jensen Huang, the ousted chief executive, was a security risk, and that Trump then made clear he wanted Huang reinstated. The Journal describes a frantic White House push to get him back, and says Trump touted the decision on Monday as proof that the market agreed with him. The Times, in a news analysis by Peter Baker, frames it more sharply, arguing that the intervention sidelines national security concerns and shows Trump's indifference to warnings from his own intelligence agencies. Its business pages add that Huang apologized to employees at a town hall, said he had violated the company's code of conduct and "made some terrible mistakes," and disclosed that he is in therapy, ninety minutes a day according to The Morning. The Times also reports that Nvidia's board bowed to Trump in bringing him back, and runs a profile of Alice Huang tracing her rise from a Chinese factory town to the center of the scandal. The Morning notes that U.S. spy agencies briefed Congress on the intelligence behind the affair, and that some are asking whether a woman would have been given the same grace. On the Union-Tribune's front page, the wire version from The New York Times focuses on Blanche himself, who dismissed questions about Nvidia on Tuesday and refused to say whether the company is under federal investigation. It says investigators have gathered evidence on two fronts, high-powered chips allegedly diverted to China through other firms and a woman whom U.S. officials see as a Chinese intelligence asset, with whom Huang reportedly had an affair, and that Blanche insisted he had "no reason to stand up here" and call Nvidia anything but a good corporate citizen. A Times opinion column and a Journal piece on what happens if the AI boom goes bust, which says the economy would take a painful hit but the scale is a live debate among economists, round out the theme.

That AI worry is showing up in prices. Futures are sharply lower this morning. The Journal's capture has Dow futures down about 1.3 percent, S&P 500 futures down nearly 2 percent and Nasdaq futures down 2.9 percent, with the volatility index above 30, while the 10-Point email, sent a bit earlier, showed slightly smaller drops; the 10-year Treasury yield is near 4.85 percent. Yesterday's session was mixed: the Nasdaq fell 1.5 percent, the S&P 500 half a percent, while the Dow rose 0.9 percent. The Times says tech stocks partly reversed the rally that Nvidia's news sparked. The most striking market item is Anthropic, which had the largest IPO ever. The Journal reports it closed Tuesday at $81.91, down 6.5 percent and below its $83 offering price for the first time, its third straight losing session, after a debut week in which it opened far above the offering price. Spencer Jakab's markets column jokes about a Black Friday sale. Other business news: Stripe's latest share buyback is set to value it above $200 billion; Sanofi agreed to buy Insmed for $41 billion in cash; and Abercrombie & Fitch and Dick's Sporting Goods both raised their full-year outlooks ahead of the holidays, Dick's helped by improving trends at Foot Locker. Jaguar is at the center of a fight over whether European carmakers can use intellectual-property law to fend off Chinese rivals. The Journal also reports that Jamie Dimon has soured on Britain over a bank tax hike that JPMorgan expects to cost an extra 120 million pounds. The Times covers the Musk-run SpaceX facing an AI dilemma, American Airlines lagging Delta and United and losing money, a study showing the biggest U.S. data centers emit as much carbon as entire countries, a judge ordering the release of an OpenAI engineer accused of secretly recording meetings, young New Yorkers losing money on Kalshi, and a squeeze on socially conscious options in 401(k) plans. Today the Commerce Department releases the Fed's preferred inflation gauge for October and a revised third-quarter GDP estimate, the Fed publishes its Beige Book, and Deere reports earnings. Markets close tomorrow for Thanksgiving.

On China, a federal jury convicted Huawei of fraud and other offenses in a U.S. criminal trial but acquitted it of racketeering conspiracy, the two-decade scheme prosecutors had alleged. The Journal calls it fraud; the Times headline describes a conviction for stealing trade secrets. The Journal also says the U.S. is preparing to raise tariffs on China and other countries, and that Xi Jinping has purged a fourth Politburo member in under two years. Elsewhere in security news, the Journal reports that Russian warplanes have skirted or entered the airspace of U.S. allies in Asia, prompting Japan and South Korea to scramble jets, and that the Pentagon is talking with GM and other manufacturers about mass-producing drones. Three outlets report that the CIA and at least two other intelligence agencies discovered a breach of an unclassified system. In the Gulf, Yemen's Houthis struck the tallest tower in Riyadh for the second time in under a week; the Times says Saudi authorities report eleven injured. NATO's Mark Rutte meets Trump in Palm Beach today. The Journal also notes that Venezuela's interim authorities put the country's debt at $237 billion, far more than expected, and that the Solomon Islands says it will not allow foreign military bases. In Ukraine, the Times profiles older volunteers fighting as the "Steppe Wolves" and the civilians, from doctors to gravediggers, who live with the war daily; web searches this week also point to a Trump, Xi and Putin "peace declaration" and renewed strikes on the Crimean Bridge, though I could not confirm today's developments. Smaller world items: a fire at an Indonesian factory has left dozens unaccounted for; Iraq's prime minister is trying to take more power from the Kurdistan region; a French police officer's murder charge in a teenager's shooting was reinstated; a woman in Iran was lashed 74 times for singing without a hijab; Norway closed its last coal mine on Svalbard; and burglars broke into Prince William and Catherine's country home, with two men held. The Times also looks at how Harry and Meghan are settling back in Britain, and at excerpts from the late Pope Francis's diary.

Back in Washington, the immigration case that has been a flashpoint for two years is ending. Kilmar Abrego Garcia, the Salvadoran man wrongfully deported last year, agreed to leave voluntarily after a judge let him arrange his own exit. The Journal says he is leaving; the Times says he landed in Madrid this morning, ending a 20-month legal fight; the Union-Tribune's version, also from the Times, says the arrangement came after he faced the prospect of being sent to Liberia. Related: the Times reports Haitian families fleeing to Canada are being turned back and detained at the border. The Times also reports that an account funding Trump's Washington makeover got a $300 million boost from an unnamed source, that Trump gave Rudy Giuliani the Presidential Medal of Freedom, that top aviation officials were traveling when flights were halted nationwide earlier this month, and that judges opened the door for Trump to win his appeal in the Stormy Daniels case. Congressional Democrats are targeting companies tied to an investment firm affiliated with Donald Trump Jr., and the Journal says unsealed records show the investigation of Representative Ilhan Omar covers most of her life. On the Democratic side, a progressive tech worker who ran no TV ads flipped a Tennessee House seat in one of the year's biggest upsets, and the Times notes that nearly a quarter of the House will be new next year. The Journal says Democrats are already watering down Medicare for All plans, and Josh Shapiro is accusing both Biden and Trump of not restraining Netanyahu enough. Also reported: a candidate secretly funded her campaign through Zelle.

Thanksgiving travel is the national story of the morning. The Morning warns that tarmac delays of three hours or more are the worst since 2009, with no fines issued by the Transportation Department since 2025; Newark, JFK and LaGuardia lead the list. The Union-Tribune's front page shows crowds at San Diego International on its holiday-rush photo spread, with Sunday expected to be the busiest day. In Iowa, the Army planned to detonate about five tons of degraded lead azide this morning, with a toxic lead plume that could reach 100 miles to the southwest into Missouri; earlier reports said Friday, but The Morning and the Times say today, and cold weather made it more urgent. A harrowing crash in New Orleans is being called a "Miracle on the Mississippi." Macy's parade makes its 100th run Thursday with a returning blue balloon from 1931, and the Journal's quirky A-hed introduces the new rodent debuting there. Other items: the Journal says about 10 percent of Americans still skip seat belts, and the Times looks at a study showing the mortality gap between Black and white Americans has narrowed.

Now San Diego. The Union-Tribune leads with a warning to stay away from beaches during dangerous high tides and waves. The National Weather Service says a combination of big surf and king tides could knock people off jetties and piers, with flooding on low-lying streets near the coast and the worst conditions Thursday, Thanksgiving Day; forecasters issued an unusual alert, and lifeguards say cliff failures are a risk too. Home prices in the San Diego area rose 3.52 percent in the year to September, the S&P Cotality Case-Shiller index says, nearly double the national pace, ranking the metro sixth among 20 cities, behind Chicago, New York, and others. A proposed 60,000-square-foot Topgolf venue in the Sorrento Valley is back in play. Also in the paper: the U.S. halted joint operations with Ecuador; a Carlsbad Village condo project was proposed; cross-border artists received $1.3 million in grants; leaders are embracing a bike-share revival; a Marine admitted stealing rocket ammunition; and voters passed a $23 million bond measure for school repairs. The paper also has its Thanks and Giving feature.

In sports, the San Diego State Aztecs got bullied, in the paper's words, in their Players Era game against Maryland, and a pair of top-10 teams headline the Rady Children's Invitational. Southwestern College plays for the Southern California title Saturday, and the paper looks at who could play in the Holiday and Poinsettia bowls. Nationally, the Steelers benched Aaron Rodgers for Mason Rudolph; tennis's Jannik Sinner will miss about three more months after knee surgery; and UConn's Geno Auriemma was blunt about his team after a loss to South Carolina. In culture, Thelma Golden is stepping down after 21 years at Harlem's Studio Museum, and the new Grand Theft Auto game is being reviewed. Finally, a cooking tip from The Morning: start the dry-brine on your turkey today.

That's your briefing for this morning.

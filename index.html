Wednesday, September 2, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters, Bloomberg, and AP reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 2, 2026. The United States carried out fresh strikes against Iran overnight, unleashing another round of escalation in a conflict now in its seventh month, while at home a global bond market rout is sending borrowing costs to levels not seen since the 2008 financial crisis — a combination that is unsettling markets, straining alliances, and beginning to creep into the pockets of everyday Americans.

The overnight exchange with Iran marks the latest chapter in a war that began on February 28th of this year. American forces struck radar installations, mine-laying infrastructure, and communications sites along Iran's southern coast, targeting capabilities Tehran has used to threaten shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran responded by firing volleys of missiles and drones across the Persian Gulf at American positions. In Jordan, which hosts U.S. servicemembers at bases across its territory, the military reported engaging thirteen ballistic missiles that crossed into its airspace. Iran's government said civilians were killed at a wedding in one of the targeted areas — a claim that is sure to fuel international pressure on Washington. Negotiations began in June, but strikes have continued throughout, and the strategic flashpoint remains the Strait of Hormuz, through which an enormous share of the world's oil supply flows. Crude oil ticked up slightly but remained at $89.68 a barrel.

Alongside the military escalation, a separate crisis was unfolding in the world's bond markets, and it touched nearly everyone. Global government bond yields climbed to their highest levels since before the 2008 financial crisis, with Japan's ten-year yield touching three percent for the first time since 1996 and Britain's thirty-year bonds reaching levels not seen since 1998. In the United States, the benchmark ten-year Treasury yield reached 4.8 percent, the highest since President Trump began his second term. Markets are now pricing in roughly a seventy-percent chance that the Federal Reserve raises interest rates again at its September 16th meeting. The driving forces are familiar: persistent inflation, soaring energy costs tied in part to Middle East tensions, and the sheer weight of government borrowing across the developed world. As the New York Times put it Wednesday morning, the bond market underpins the economy — your mortgage, your car loan, your retirement account all move with it. The S&P 500 fell 0.7 percent Tuesday, the Nasdaq dropped one percent, and the Dow shed 0.8 percent.

The chaos landed squarely in the middle of this week's G-20 finance ministers' summit in Asheville, North Carolina, which closed Tuesday in disarray. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent argued that the United States can grow its way out of its staggering forty-trillion-dollar debt burden, but the summit devolved into trade spats, public insults, a fight over a group photograph, and open hostility between the U.S. and its closest allies. Canadian and European officials pushed back on the administration's posture; China and the United States clashed over fundamental economic policy. Bessent claimed the U.S. was running the best-performing bond market among major countries even as yields spiked around him. As the meeting closed, the ten-year Treasury hit its highest point since Trump's return to office — an uncomfortable verdict from the markets on a summit that failed to calm them. Eurozone inflation, meanwhile, rose for a third straight month in August, reinforcing expectations that the European Central Bank will raise its key interest rate next week as well.

On the domestic political front, Massachusetts Senator Ed Markey, the eighty-year-old progressive Democrat, easily defeated a primary challenge from Representative Seth Moulton Tuesday night. The race had focused squarely on Markey's age and the future direction of the Democratic Party, but voters sided decisively with the incumbent. Separately, a whistle-blower alleged that a secretive, rushed effort inside the U.S. Postal Service sought to carry out the Trump administration's plan to restrict mail-in voting, in apparent defiance of court orders. And the House passed a bipartisan resolution to fund the government past the September 30th deadline, with both parties eager to avoid a shutdown as they head into the midterm campaign season. The House also voted to censure Representative Chuck Edwards of North Carolina over allegations that he sexually harassed two young female staffers and created a hostile work environment.

In Europe, the German government formally accused Russia of being behind an explosive-laden drone discovered at Leipzig airport last month. Berlin pledged to retaliate by closing a Russian consulate and restricting Russian nationals' ability to travel to Germany. At a summit earlier this week, Vladimir Putin pledged to press ahead with Russia's escalating war against Ukraine and voiced support for Iran in its conflict with the United States. For Ukrainian children, the first day of school this fall meant going underground — into shelters where classes are being held to protect students from ongoing bombardment. Russia, for the sixth consecutive day, pounded Kyiv with strikes.

In New York City, the woman killed in a stabbing attack in Times Square on Monday was identified as Erin Piacenti, a thirty-two-year-old vice president at Bank of America and a New Jersey mother who had recently returned to work. The attack appears to have been random and unprovoked: the suspect, forty-nine-year-old Pamela Cisneros of Queens, pulled two large knives from a shopping bag and attacked two strangers in the span of roughly twenty seconds on Seventh Avenue. Police confronted Cisneros and, after a four-minute standoff, shot her. The tragedy has renewed debate about public safety in one of the city's most heavily trafficked tourist corridors.

In the corporate world, the Wall Street Journal reports that Volkswagen chief executive Oliver Blume is considering an audacious move to bypass his own supervisory board — where worker representatives and local government officials hold swing votes — and take a sweeping cost-cutting plan that would double job losses to one hundred thousand directly to shareholders. It is a nuclear option in Germany's carefully balanced industrial democracy, reflecting how severely the country's flagship automaker is struggling against Chinese competition. Elsewhere, Nestlé announced it will sell its mainstream vitamins and supplements business for one billion dollars to private equity, Medtronic raised its earnings outlook, and Novartis halted eight clinical trials of an experimental therapy targeting autoimmune disorders after three patients died.

Finally, the United Nations issued a sobering assessment: the world has failed in its goal of limiting global warming to one-point-five degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels, the threshold set in the Paris Agreement. The planet is now projected to warm two-point-six degrees by century's end. The damage is already being felt in Nepal, where catastrophic flooding blamed on climate change killed hundreds and could cost roughly five billion dollars — about a tenth of the country's entire economy. Nepal has turned to mass burials as the toll continues to climb.

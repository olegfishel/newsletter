Morning Briefing

Tuesday, August 18, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times Morning newsletter, San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented by Reuters, AP, and wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 18, 2026. The biggest story of the morning may be the one that has been months in the making and finally reached a breaking point overnight: the sixty-day window for a United States–Iran peace deal has closed without agreement, and President Trump has responded by threatening to bomb Oman — an American ally that has served as the quiet back-channel to Tehran — raising the specter that a fragile ceasefire could unravel into something far worse.

The threat came in a blunt Fox News phone interview Monday, in which Trump said he would strike Oman if it "gets in the way" of American control over the Strait of Hormuz. The Journal reports this morning that some U.S. officials believe Oman has been tilting toward Iran in the negotiations, while others see the outburst as frustration over a stalled deal that Trump had promised to close quickly. Simultaneously, Iran's semiofficial news agency announced that Tehran had reached a separate arrangement with Oman governing Hormuz traffic — a move that infuriated the White House. Intercepted communications and other intelligence, the Journal says, point to a deliberate shift by hard-line Iranian leaders to raise the costs of the standoff for Washington and its regional allies. Monday's deadline, set in a June memorandum of understanding, has now expired with no concrete successor framework announced.

On Wall Street, markets are absorbing the diplomatic turbulence poorly. S&P 500 futures are down about half a percent this morning, Nasdaq futures are off more than one percent, and crude oil has climbed above eighty-five dollars a barrel as traders price in the possibility of renewed Hormuz disruption. The ten-year Treasury yield sits at four-point-seven percent. Gold, which had been near record highs, is pulling back slightly.

In corporate news that is equally dramatic in its own way, the Journal's front page leads with the unraveling of Los Angeles Dodgers owner Mark Walter's financial empire. Federal investigators have spent months mapping a web of hidden deals in which Walter allegedly used insurance companies he controls as a lending mechanism to fund his acquisitions — a strategy that worked spectacularly well when he bought the Dodgers in 2012 and built a sports kingdom that eventually included the Lakers. Now the FBI has seized his phone and laptop, regulators are pressing hard, and Walter has been racing to unwind those bets. The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point newsletter this morning walks through exactly how those hidden financial connections caught the government's attention: loans from Walter-controlled insurers flowed through a series of intermediaries before financing other Walter-linked businesses, a structure the Journal says is poorly understood even within Wall Street. Neither Walter nor his companies have been charged with any crime, but last week he sold the Lakers for twelve-and-a-half billion dollars — a record valuation — under the cloud of the federal scrutiny.

Also drawing intense attention this morning is the implosion and partial recovery of Situational Awareness, the AI-focused hedge fund run by twenty-five-year-old Leopold Aschenbrenner, the former OpenAI researcher who became famous for his thesis that the AI revolution would require a massive buildup in semiconductors and energy infrastructure. The fund grew to forty-five billion dollars before margin calls on leveraged semiconductor positions — the fund was reportedly running leverage of up to four hundred percent — forced a fire sale of its public portfolio to Ken Griffin's Citadel at a discount. The Journal reports this morning that sharp traders had picked up on the trouble through whispers about strange options moves and a forced partial sale of Aschenbrenner's Anthropic stake, and swung into short positions that accelerated the unwind. In a remarkable turn, Situational Awareness completed a four-hundred-million-dollar emergency private raise in recent days and appears to be surviving, at least for now. The episode carries echoes of the Archegos Capital collapse and is a vivid illustration of how quickly leveraged bets can unravel.

In the AI infrastructure space, OpenAI moved in the opposite direction, announcing a ten-gigawatt data-center deal in Ohio with SoftBank's SB Energy, partly backed by Nvidia — a project the Journal describes as potentially one of the largest AI hubs ever built. Manufacturing output in the United States rose last month to its highest level since 2022, driven largely by AI-infrastructure buildout and the companies pivoting to serve it. L3Harris Technologies, the defense contractor that refurbished a Qatari jet for use as Air Force One, replaced chairman and chief executive Christopher Kubasik after an internal investigation found he had violated the company's code of conduct.

The New York Times Morning newsletter today, written by Sam Sifton returning from vacation, leads with a deep look at the buy-now-pay-later lending industry — apps like Afterpay and Klarna that have evolved from purchasing luxuries on installment to covering electricity bills, health insurance, and even rent. Reporter Stacy Cowley's story documents how what began as a pandemic-era convenience has become, for millions of Americans, essential "working capital for the modern middle class." Credit card spending still dwarfs the sector, but pay-later loans nearly doubled in just a couple of years, and the debt traps are very real. Separately, the Times notes that more American parents are exempting their children from childhood vaccine requirements — kindergarten exemptions hit an eighteen-year high last year, up eighteen percent in a single school year.

Florida holds primary elections today. Byron Donalds, the Trump-backed congressman, appears to hold a solid lead in the Republican race for governor. President Trump voted in person in the election, having previously called mail-in voting equivalent to cheating. In Alaska, incumbent Republican Senator Dan Sullivan faces Democrat Mary Peltola and, in an only-in-Alaska twist, a third candidate also named Dan Sullivan. The Supreme Court again declined to review the five-million-dollar civil judgment finding that Trump sexually abused and defamed writer E. Jean Carroll. A federal judge blocked the administration from abandoning plans to relocate the FBI headquarters from Washington to a Maryland facility. And Palm Beach International Airport is now officially Donald J. Trump International Airport — with a new three-letter code to match.

Actress Hayden Panettiere died of an apparent drug overdose, a source told the Times; she was 36. The Colorado River system is deepening its crisis, with Lake Powell hitting a new elevation low after a disastrous snowpack winter and record spring heat. The first federal bellwether trial against Meta — alleging the company harmed children with addictive technology — begins today in California. And the Buss family announced plans to sell its remaining seventeen-point-eight percent stake in the Lakers to the new owners Bob Iger and Joshua Kushner; Jeanie Buss said she opposes the plan, making the situation, as the Times put it, "messy."

On the immigration front, the erasure of Temporary Protected Status for Haitian immigrants, which took effect July twenty-seventh, is already hitting South Florida businesses hard. The Journal separately reports that thousands of Florida workers are disappearing more broadly as slower immigration leaves home-care agencies, restaurants, and local governments racing to fill critical vacancies. Attorney General Todd Blanche announced a new process allowing individuals who lost gun rights due to felony convictions to apply to have them reinstated. And Luigi Mangione's state murder trial — in connection with the killing of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson — has been delayed as his defense team attempts to have the charges dismissed following his federal guilty plea.

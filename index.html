Good morning, it's Sunday, November 22, 2026.

The story hanging over everything is a rough stretch for the technology trade. The Times reports the stock market just had its worst week in more than a year, with the S&P 500 finishing more than ten percent below its recent high. Tech fared poorly even as Anthropic's blockbuster public offering hit the market, because investors are wary of the A.I. boom. The Journal's markets columnist James Mackintosh says the A.I. selloff of recent weeks looks remarkably like the bursting of the dot-com bubble. Adding to the unease, the Times says the Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating Nvidia's accounting practices, and President Trump has threatened to ban American tech companies from building data centers abroad, floating a "tremendous tax" or other penalties on violators.

The Nvidia scandal keeps widening. The Journal reports that Alice Huang, the romantic partner of ousted Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, was the public face of Megaspeed, a Singapore company suspected of funneling advanced computing power to China. Behind the scenes, it says, was Li Ming, a 52-year-old Chinese national now facing fraud charges in Singapore. The Times, for its part, reports that Huang has extensive ties to China's defense industry. The Journal also says Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has outlasted rivals despite scrutiny over the scandal and his family's Wall Street firm, thanks to his longtime friendship with the president. The week ahead is shortened: Monday brings preliminary manufacturing and services readings plus earnings from Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Agilent and Zoom. Tuesday has consumer confidence and results from Dell, Medtronic, Autodesk and others. Wednesday brings the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, a GDP update, the Beige Book and Deere earnings. Markets are closed Thursday for Thanksgiving and shut at 1 p.m. Eastern on Black Friday.

Overseas, the Journal says the United Arab Emirates is shipping oil through the Strait of Hormuz under U.S. military protection, braving Iranian attacks to keep crude flowing and loosen Tehran's grip on the waterway. The Times's Morning newsletter, answering a reader from San Diego, notes the war with Iran continues, and that a new Democratic majority in Congress could try to limit it through legislation, funding limits or Pentagon restrictions, though Trump could veto and Democrats lack the votes to override. Web reporting separately suggests ship attacks in the strait were at a wartime high in October. Please treat that as secondary reporting.

In Europe, the Times says Polish officials reported three Russian objects, at least one a missile, crashed into Polish territory, and Poland's prime minister called it an intentional provocation. British and European leaders also agreed to remove some Brexit-era barriers, a decade after the U.K. vote. On the diplomatic track, web reports say talks among Ukraine, Russia and the U.S. in Abu Dhabi ended without a breakthrough, and Europeans accuse Moscow of stalling. The Journal adds that Ukraine's defense industry, reborn since the invasion, has likely hit a ceiling. And with attention elsewhere, the Journal says North Korea's nuclear threat has slipped from view: new satellite images show an expanded uranium-enrichment plant, Kim Jong Un's arsenal is estimated at about 60 warheads, and he has ignored Trump's overtures, with one White House letter reportedly tossed aside unopened. Kim has leaned on closer ties with Russia and huge cryptocurrency thefts.

Back home, the Journal says Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin is struggling to steady an agency in crisis, and missteps have renewed calls inside the administration to replace him. The Times reports an internal watchdog found ICE went too far last year in loosening training standards to hire more officers, and ICE agents detained a Chinese-born Princeton neuroscientist as she traveled home from a conference. Federal watchdog lawyers also opened, then backed off, an inquiry into possible fraud around Trump's memecoin, fearing the government would kill it. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, after losing his Senate bid, has dropped a number of high-profile suits against political opponents. Mexico captured a drug kingpin who was among the F.B.I.'s ten most wanted fugitives. A Missouri court approved a $7.25 billion settlement ending tens of thousands of Roundup cancer lawsuits against Bayer.

After a week of technology outages, the Journal offers tips for preparing for the next bank outage and avoiding scammers exploiting the chaos. In science, a Times item says an Anthropic mathematician claims, with help from A.I., to have proven the Riemann hypothesis. A new federal survey shows a substantial drop in new science Ph.D. students at major research universities. And in the Journal's look at aging, baby boomers, whose collective wealth tops $97 trillion, are rewriting old age as the oldest turn 80, traveling, staying active and even buying bigger homes. Not everyone can afford it.

Culture and sports: Grand Theft Auto VI, set in a fictional Miami, is out. The Hunger Games prequel gets a lukewarm review from the Times. The Journal wonders whether James Bond will trade his Omega for a Jaeger-LeCoultre. In football, the Vikings face the 49ers in Mexico City tonight at 8:20 Eastern, at 7,300 feet of altitude, a matchup of the league's best offense against its stingiest defense.

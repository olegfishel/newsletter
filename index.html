Tuesday, November 17, 2026

Sources used: WSJ print front page and The 10-Point, NYT front page and The Morning, San Diego Union-Tribune A1 screenshot and e-Edition email. Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 17, 2026.

The story tying together Washington, Wall Street and Beijing this morning is the fallout over chips and China, just as President Trump heads into a week of summitry in Asia. The Commerce Department has issued a long-awaited rule to crack down on shipments of advanced chips from Nvidia and other American companies to China. The Journal casts it as the product of pressure on the agency, including from elsewhere in the administration, after the Jensen Huang saga. The Times and the Union-Tribune's New York Times wire story frame it a bit differently: as an effort to close a loophole that let Chinese firms get around the ban by renting computing power at data centers in places like Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore, with the rule requiring licenses and closer checks on customers tied to China, Russia and Iran. The Times says the rules have been fiercely debated inside the administration and will cut into a booming data-center business serving Chinese companies, and the Journal's 10-Point says Trump's advisers are at war over China ahead of the summit with Xi Jinping.

That leads to the Nvidia drama itself. The Journal reports Nvidia's board is not considering bringing Huang back as chief executive, despite calls from some investors and employees, and says interim CEO Colette Kress is expected to rein in the deals through which Nvidia financed computing contracts and startups. The Times' Morning newsletter adds that Mr. Trump said he is open to seeing Huang reinstated, which is notable given the board's stance. It also reports that the Justice and Commerce departments will investigate Megaspeed, the company once led by Alice Huang, whom U.S. officials believe worked as a Chinese intelligence asset while in a relationship with Huang. The Journal's own reporting says she was married to an executive building a Chinese state-backed data-center empire, and details a demanding, fear-driven work culture under Huang, plus an employee's warning that not reinstating him could cost the company irreplaceable talent. Nvidia reports earnings after the bell today, its first big test of investor confidence since the exit.

Markets are feeling it. The Journal says a punishing stretch of volatility, running from the Nasdaq's October 6 peak to Huang's departure, has ended the era of the no-brainer A.I. trade. Futures this morning are mixed to lower: Dow futures are down roughly half a percent, S&P 500 futures are about flat to slightly lower, depending on the moment you check, and Nasdaq futures are mixed. Europe's Stoxx 600 is down nearly nine-tenths of a percent. The ten-year Treasury yield sits around 5.1 percent, oil is near $86 a barrel, and gold is near $3,860. Retail sales for October come out today, and Disney and Home Depot report alongside Nvidia.

On the trip itself: the Times says Mr. Trump began his Asia tour in the Philippines, where he arrived very late to a diplomatic summit, and is then headed to China. The Journal's 10-Point describes this week's gathering as a three-way meeting among Trump, Xi and Vladimir Putin, and a Journal piece argues it won't be a new Yalta, since none of the three can impose a new world order. The Times notes that Canada's Mark Carney, once a curiosity at these summits, is now something of a star and will share stages with all three. Mr. Trump also said there is potential for a meeting with North Korea's Kim Jong Un in China. Not everyone is bullish: a Times opinion piece argues his China gamble is backfiring.

In the war in Ukraine, the Times says Russia, even as it talks peace, has opened a bombing campaign meant to strip Kyiv and other cities of heat, running water and sanitation for the winter. The Journal says Ukrainian strikes destroyed part of the bridge linking Crimea and Russia, one of Putin's showcase projects, and the Times says the two sides traded strikes on important bridges. The EU, per the Union-Tribune, says Russia is stalling while it targets civilians. Elsewhere, the Times reports that delegates in Geneva have begun an uncertain effort to regulate lethal autonomous weapons, with the United States and Russia resisting; that Bahrain convicted dozens of Shiite clerics in a sweeping terrorism trial; that five European countries announced a plan to deport rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda; that the Pope is enduring grueling travel in South America weeks after surgery; and that Iran's war-damaged landscape has revived a puzzle over dams built for no clear reason. A Times report also says leaked files show Deutsche Bank protected Russian oligarchs. And the Journal and Union-Tribune note the death at 80 of Mexican drug kingpin Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo. In Europe's deal news, the EU approved UniCredit's bid to take control of Germany's Commerzbank. The Journal also notes the presidents of Brazil and Argentina are taking their rivalry into Spain's election.

Back home, the aftermath of the midterms still colors the news. The Journal reports that Texas voters flipped the deep-red state, with Democrats sweeping every statewide seat after what voters described as an increasingly extreme and fractured state GOP. The Times says polls showed strength for Democrats but still missed badly, reports from a big Democratic gathering in Denver, and notes that embattled federal employees are looking to a Democratic Congress for a lifeline. In New York, Democratic socialists are urging Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to run for president, and in Los Angeles, newly elected Mayor Nithya Raman is laying out her first priorities. Mayor Zohran Mamdani of New York disbanded a controversial police unit that responded to protests, after being pressed to fulfill a promise made following the anti-ICE demonstrations; the Times says an unarmed civilian group will now manage protests. The Supreme Court rejected the Trump administration's bid to revive cuts to university research funding. Congress is weighing an energy plan that could charge data centers billions.

The aviation mess continues. The Journal's number of the day is 20,000, the estimated flights canceled between Friday evening and Monday morning after a rare nationwide FAA ground stop, with JetBlue and American cancelling the most. Operations were nearly back to normal Monday, but airlines are just beginning to count the cost. The Times says a router problem figured in the stoppage and that Mr. Trump stayed out of sight during the crisis; on Monday United grounded flights for about an hour over a technology issue, which it says is resolved. Chase also said it resumed normal operations after a software bug disrupted transactions. And a look ahead: 5.96 million travelers are expected to fly domestically over Thanksgiving week, spending 17 percent more on tickets.

More in business. Berkshire Hathaway nearly doubled its stake in Lennar and confirmed a position in Bending Spoons. Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh said staff are modeling how A.I. could reshape the economy, including a scenario in which it displaces workers. Kalshi expects to begin trading stocks within two months. Quebecor proposed a combination with Telus valued at about $22 billion. Intel is being sued by shareholders over a PC slump. J&J agreed to buy Ollin for an initial $2.2 billion. California sued OpenAI, alleging it misled and endangered the public by developing A.I. models without needed safeguards. Wall Street is making its pitch for Anthropic's I.P.O., and Elon Musk wants to merge Tesla and SpaceX. The Journal adds that Fifth Avenue is again the priciest retail corridor in the world, with rents up 35 percent in a year, and that Americans hold nearly $36 trillion in home equity and increasingly tap it. A Times study finds the biggest U.S. data centers emit nearly as much carbon dioxide as Bolivia or Denmark, and shoe prices are up 2.3 percent in half a year thanks to tariffs and costlier oil.

In health, the Times reports that more than a dozen American babies have been born with measles in two years, 112 pregnant women have been infected, and the country has more than 5,000 cases and 10 deaths this year. International health officials are expected to announce today that the United States has lost its measles elimination status. The Union-Tribune's A1 also carries the Times' look at how doctors' decades of work to wipe out measles is being undone. Separately, new research says weight-loss drugs work for children as young as 6, though younger kids grew more slowly; Novo Nordisk will ask the FDA to approve its drugs for ages 6 to 11. A Massachusetts judge set a new trial, in the spring per the Times and March per the Morning, for Lindsay Clancy. An independent forensic scientist argues photos show Jeffrey Epstein was killed. In culture, Robert Pattinson will be the next James Bond, and Raye, Rosalía and Ella Langley lead a women-driven Grammy field.

Closer to home, the San Diego Union-Tribune leads with the City Council's unanimous vote to turn off the city's Flock license-plate-reader and "smart streetlight" cameras, rescinding approval of about 500 and ending a contract worth around $2 million a year. The paper describes privacy and civil-liberties concerns, including worries about the data being used for federal immigration enforcement. Also on A1, permits for new homes dipped last year for the second year running, leaving San Diego far short of the state's goal ahead of a 2029 deadline; accessory dwelling units made up 28 percent of the new homes. Elsewhere in the paper: eight San Diego artists earned nine Grammy nominations, an 8-year-old Haitian boy was reunited with family in San Diego, a local activist pitched youth justice reforms, opposition is growing to a new 7B jail in Vista, and National City's council is eyeing a business tax plan. The forecast calls for sun and sprinkles.

In sports, USD made the NCAA Tournament and gets a home rematch with Grand Canyon, the San Diego State Aztecs visit No. 24 Boise State and host Texas Southern, and the Chargers host the Jets. The Union-Tribune also has a piece on a winless Nevermore, and Dutcher praising Frontwave as a great venue. Nationally, the U.S. women's hockey team beat Canada 6-2 for the world title, and Jaromir Jagr is retiring at 54.

That's your briefing for this morning.

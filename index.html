Saturday, October 3, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 3, 2026.

The biggest story out of the skies this week continues to grow more alarming. The co-pilot who stabbed his captain aboard FlyDubai Flight 1073 and sent the plane into a terrifying nosedive has been identified as Hamam al-Hammami, a 29-year-old Omani national who had previously been banned from flying by Oman itself — over concerns that he had adopted radical ideological views. Al-Hammami was subsequently hired by FlyDubai, apparently without the airline discovering his disqualifying history. The jet, bound for Israel, plunged 17,400 feet in just 92 seconds, reaching speeds near the sound barrier, before off-duty pilots who happened to be traveling as cabin passengers forced their way forward and brought the aircraft under control. It landed safely in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. The U.A.E. is calling it a terrorist attack. The Wall Street Journal reports that the violent descent snapped off part of the tail rudder — yet the Boeing 737 MAX held together. A visually stunning breakdown in the Journal traces exactly how the plane survived forces that would have destroyed a lesser machine.

Turning to the economy, Friday's September jobs report delivered a jolt of a different kind. The United States added just 29,000 jobs last month — far below nearly every forecast — making it one of the weakest monthly hiring figures in years. The unemployment rate held near historic lows, and economists were quick to note that the underlying labor market remains broadly healthy, but the anemic headline number rattled investors. Stock markets closed the week in mixed fashion: the Nasdaq eked out a half-percent gain while the S&P 500 fell 0.3 percent and the Dow lost 1.3 percent. Tesla added its own dose of gloom to the market picture, reporting that global electric vehicle sales fell 2 percent in the third quarter to about 486,500 units — reversing gains from the first half of the year — even as the company's energy storage business continued to grow. Volvo, meanwhile, scrapped its full-year guidance after warning that weakness in China and the United States led to lower-than-expected results, a sign that the global auto industry is still navigating a very choppy road.

On the energy front, the Group of Seven industrialized nations took coordinated action this week, agreeing to release 100 million barrels of crude oil and fuel from emergency strategic reserves in an effort to tamp down surging prices. President Trump has separately threatened a ban on U.S. diesel exports if domestic prices don't fall — a pressure tactic that underscores how fuel costs have become a dominant political liability heading into this fall's midterm elections.

Those midterms are shaping up to be unlike any in recent memory. A new Wall Street Journal survey finds that roughly one in five registered voters holds an unfavorable view of both major political parties — a bloc the Journal labels "double haters." Driven by economic anxiety and deep frustration with Washington, this group has grown substantially since the last midterms, and with more than 40 percent of them currently undecided or uncommitted, they could well determine which party controls Congress in January. A separate development reinforced the volatility of the Senate map: a deep-pocketed Republican super PAC quietly shifted millions of dollars out of North Carolina this week, essentially conceding that race, while redirecting funds toward propping up a candidate in deeply red Kansas — a remarkable defensive posture that would have seemed unthinkable even a few months ago.

There was a revealing diplomatic footnote to last week's Trump-Xi summit. The Journal reported exclusively that Beijing requested unusual and extended private rest periods for China's 73-year-old leader, which ate significantly into the scheduled working hours. Multiple aides described the atmosphere as one of careful management around Xi's stamina and image — a disclosure that carries real geopolitical weight as the world tries to gauge the health of the man at the center of U.S.-China relations. On the artificial intelligence front, President Trump is expected to tap Jay Clayton — currently serving as Director of National Intelligence — to take on the additional role of the administration's AI czar, spearheading AI policy across the federal government.

From Ukraine, President Zelensky announced this week that Ukrainian forces had fired a domestically produced ballistic missile in combat for the first time — a milestone in the country's push to build indigenous weapons systems. Russia, for its part, continued striking at infrastructure, targeting bridges in fresh overnight attacks that snarled traffic across the country. And in a reminder of how dramatically global weather patterns have shifted, the Atlantic Ocean broke a century-old record this week for the latest date in the year without a named hurricane — one of many cascading consequences of a historic El Niño cycle that has redrawn weather expectations worldwide.

The Wall Street Journal this morning also carries a fascinating look at a new breed of Wall Street player: twenty-something traders with math and quantitative backgrounds who are quietly building firms that wager millions of dollars a week on prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket. Among them are two former Jane Street interns whose little-known firm, Alcedine Technologies, has become a top-10 trader across U.S. regulated exchanges — using AI tools and teams of bots to hunt for price edges. It's a corner of finance that barely existed five years ago and is now attracting serious institutional attention.

The New York Times this morning takes a breath from the news cycle with a meditation on slowness and connection. Columnist Melissa Kirsch writes about a postcard her friend sent from Queens — the old-fashioned kind, arriving days after it was mailed — and ties it to a broader cultural moment: a resurgence in pen-pal correspondence among young adults who, surrounded by the constant churn of instant digital communication, are rediscovering the "trapped-in-amber" intimacy of handwritten letters. It's a gentle counterpoint to a week that has been anything but slow-moving.

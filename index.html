Morning Briefing

Tuesday, October 27, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 27, 2026. Israelis are voting today in a national election that many see as a referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, and reports say turnout is running at the fastest pace in forty-five years. It is the country's first national vote since the attacks of October seventh, 2023, and the race is too close to call. Netanyahu's main challenger, the centrist Gadi Eisenkot, differs from him sharply in style and background, but the Wall Street Journal notes that the two are much closer on substance, because an electorate that has moved to the right on security leaves both men little room to maneuver on Gaza, Iran, or a Palestinian state.

Iran hangs over the contest. Israeli television reported this morning that Netanyahu urged President Trump to renew the fighting against Iran before both this election and next week's American midterms, though the prime minister's office denies it. The New York Times reports that he pushed for an attack before the vote but that the military's chief of staff resisted. Meanwhile, diplomats from Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan made a rare joint visit to Tehran to try to head off the next wave of conflict, and Iran says the Israeli result will make no difference to it. Regional reports also describe Houthi missiles fired toward Saudi Arabia, with a fire at the Ras Tanura refinery, and crude oil is trading near a hundred and one dollars a barrel.

On Wall Street, the big story is Anthropic, which started the clock on its initial public offering by making its prospectus public. The filing shows about sixteen billion dollars in revenue for the first half of the year, against a net loss of roughly eighty-one billion, and commitments of up to eight hundred and seven billion dollars in data center, chip, and computing contracts, many of which can't be canceled. It could be the largest debut in Wall Street history. Stocks rose on the news, with the S&P 500 up seven-tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq up eight-tenths, and the Dow up three-tenths, and futures are pointing modestly higher this morning.

The Journal's 10-Point newsletter takes readers behind the financing. Broadcom chief executive Hock Tan spent months assembling a sixty-three billion dollar package that lets Anthropic lease specialized chips, but banks, spooked by rising interest rates and the risk of overexposure to AI, demanded better terms before committing forty-two billion dollars of it. Tan intervened over the weekend with higher interest rates and guarantees covering an Anthropic default. The lesson, the Journal says, is that Wall Street is still writing checks for AI, but lenders are raising the bar even for the biggest names.

Markets in Brazil are in turmoil after President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's surprise re-election. The main stock index dropped to a two-month low, and market reports put the decline at more than thirteen percent, with the real sliding about nine percent against the dollar. Global investors who had bet on his defeat were punished, and the Journal says his win was driven by an aggressive grassroots turnout effort. President Trump has cried foul over the result.

Back home, the Federal Reserve begins a two-day policy meeting and is widely expected to hold rates steady, for reasons the Journal says have as much to do with what officials have said as with what the economy is doing. Chairman Kevin Warsh wants to stop previewing moves, but several colleagues say the central bank still owes the public an explanation. In corporate news, DoorDash is in advanced talks to buy Instacart, possibly this week; Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to thousands of employees; JetBlue is trying to reassure investors that its turnaround can survive high fuel prices; and Whirlpool expects a yearly loss as nervous consumers hold off on appliances. The Journal also flags a three-hundred-billion-dollar wall of debt looming over private-equity-backed companies.

The midterms are exactly one week away. A Cook Political Report editor told the Times this is about as good an environment as Democrats could wish for and as bad as Republicans could imagine, but a blue wave is far from assured. President Trump campaigned in Mississippi last night, and Vice President Vance stumped in Montana and Kansas. In California, a federal appeals court declined, for now, to force the state to set aside about twenty thousand mail-in ballots the Justice Department says may belong to noncitizens, and the dispute returns to a district judge today. The Journal adds that California's proposed billionaire tax has split Democrats, with polls running close.

In Washington, the Justice Department is attempting a third prosecution of former FBI director James Comey, this time over allegations that he leaked national defense information a decade ago. His lawyers say the effort is invalid and comes too late. Separately, a federal judge ruled that his daughter, Maurene Comey, was wrongly fired from the U.S. attorney's office in Manhattan.

Elsewhere, France has yielded to student protesters with new teaching posts and expanded university admissions. Venezuelan opposition leader María Corina Machado, who returned home on Sunday, is demanding free and fair elections to end interim President Delcy Rodríguez's rule. The Journal reports that the fifty percent aluminum tariff has pushed American prices to a record premium and exposed the country's dependence on Canadian supply. And in lighter news, Jay-Z will perform at the next Super Bowl halftime show.

Closer to home, forecasters say a sizable storm may roll into San Diego on Halloween night, so plan your trick-or-treating accordingly.

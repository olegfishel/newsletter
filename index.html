Morning Briefing

Sunday, October 4, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page + 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters/AP/web reporting.

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 4, 2026. The week closes with an unsettling jobs report, a terrorist attack aboard a commercial flight, and oil markets on the move — a lot to work through on what should be a quiet autumn morning.

Start with the economy. The Labor Department on Friday reported that American employers added just 29,000 jobs in September — a number so far below expectations that it rattled financial markets and handed fresh ammunition to critics on both sides of the midterm debate. Economists had forecast gains closer to 84,000. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2 percent, with about 7.1 million people counted as jobless. Health care and construction led what little hiring there was. The week's market scorecard was mixed: the Nasdaq eked out a 0.5 percent gain while the S&P 500 fell 0.3 percent and the Dow dropped 1.3 percent. The weakness in hiring is sharpened by upward revisions to prior months — July was revised to show a net loss of jobs, not the gain previously reported. The Federal Reserve's September meeting minutes, due Wednesday, will be closely read for any hint of a policy pivot.

The fuel picture is making the jobs picture worse. Diesel prices have surged so sharply — partly driven by tensions with Iran over the Strait of Hormuz, which Tehran says will not reopen until seven conditions are met — that America's truckers are in crisis. The Wall Street Journal went to a Flying J travel center off Interstate 10 in Texas, where one driver pointed to his pump screen with a groan: $944 for a tank that cost $500 a year ago. Truckers are cooking meals in their cabs, sleeping at loading docks to dodge overnight fees, and waiting for rainstorms to wash their rigs. With the midterms just four weeks out, the price at the pump is fast becoming the central economic flashback of the campaign.

One commodity where prices have gone even wilder is gold. Prices that seemed extraordinary at $3,000 a troy ounce earlier this year blew past $4,000, then $5,000 — and kept climbing. That has forced jewelers, most visibly the millennial-beloved brand Mejuri, to rethink everything from product lines to sourcing. The company has pivoted toward 10-karat gold, silver, and even stainless steel. Its boardroom discussions now revolve around government inflation data and Washington policy moves — a strange new world for a company built on necklace layering.

Now to the story that may be the week's most alarming. On September 30, FlyDubai Flight 1073 was about two hours into its route from Dubai to Tel Aviv when co-pilot Hamam al-Hammami — a 29-year-old Omani national — attacked the captain with a crash axe, sending the aircraft into a dive that shed more than 16,000 feet in roughly 30 seconds. Passengers and crew overpowered Hammami and opened the cockpit door. Two off-duty pilots who happened to be traveling as passengers stepped in and helped safely land the plane in Saudi Arabia. The UAE has called the attack a terrorist operation. Hammami, investigators report, told Saudi authorities he wanted to deliberately crash the aircraft and was "not afraid of death." A Wall Street Journal exclusive revealed that Oman had previously barred Hammami from flying — while he was training with Oman Air in 2024, officials found him in possession of extremist materials. FlyDubai subsequently hired him. Investigators are now trying to trace the radicalization timeline.

In Washington, the Trump administration was preparing to name Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton as the nation's new artificial intelligence czar — the point person for AI policy across all federal agencies. A new White House task force will have 120 days to report on the risks and opportunities of artificial intelligence. Meanwhile, the Journal reported separately that community bankers are suing the administration after regulators moved to grant bank charters to several cryptocurrency companies. And a software glitch in Boeing 737 MAX landing systems that raised concerns among industry officials has been cleared by regulators, who said it poses no safety risk.

On the international front, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz visited Kyiv as Russia continued its relentless aerial campaign. Russian drones and missiles are hitting the Ukrainian capital around the clock. The Wall Street Journal took readers inside a day in the life of Kyiv residents — from morning prayers to kindergarten held in basement shelters — a portrait of exhaustion and resilience. The G-7 agreed this week to release 100 million barrels of crude oil and fuel from emergency stockpiles to help tame soaring energy prices. A Houthi militia in Yemen claimed an attack on an oil facility in Saudi Arabia's capital, Riyadh.

The New York Times this morning leads with a deeply human story — not a breaking news event but a question that touches almost every family eventually: how do we die well? The piece follows Rajeev Thadani, who received a Stage 4 lung cancer diagnosis and turned away from chemotherapy at Memorial Sloan Kettering in favor of an Arizona alternative clinic offering hydrogen peroxide infusions, ozone gas, and a "proprietary" chemo with little evidential backing. The story, written by his daughter, lands in the same cultural moment as the MAHA movement — and asks hard questions about the trust breakdown between patients and mainstream medicine that can cost lives. On campus, Cornell's sexual assault investigation is intensifying after the university's president called it "a defining moment" and new details emerged about the punishments assigned, ranging from writing essays to expulsion. And in Okinawa, a U.S. Marine has been arrested on suspicion of murder and robbery after a Japanese woman's body was found in a hotel — an incident that prompted a formal diplomatic protest from Tokyo.

In the midterm home stretch: Democratic Senate candidates are polling competitively in five reliably Republican states, according to new Times/Siena polling. The so-called "double haters" — voters who have lost faith in both parties — are emerging as a potential decisive bloc. Byron Donalds is tightening his grip on Florida's governor race and positioning himself as one of the Republican Party's most influential figures if he wins. Brazil's President Lula faces a tougher-than-expected re-election bid, with the Brazilian left warned that a loss could shut the party out of power for years.

Finally: the Bayeux Tapestry — the 230-foot medieval cloth depicting the 1066 Norman conquest — is back in England for the first time in roughly a thousand years, and Brits are furious about the ticket system. Ordinary citizens say they are being treated like Taylor Swift fans on Ticketmaster while American tech billionaires and museum sponsors skip the queue. For many Britons, seeing the tapestry is, as one put it, a birthright. And youth baseball — once a democratic American pastime of rec leagues and dad coaches — has become a $40 billion private-equity-driven industry where a season can cost a family $10,000 or more. Participation among families earning under $100,000 has dropped 28 percent since 2014, while it has risen 21 percent for those earning more.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 24, 2026.

The story dominating both the Journal and the Times this morning is Jensen Huang's return to the top of Nvidia. Huang was back at the chip maker's Santa Clara headquarters yesterday, less than two weeks after reports of two extramarital affairs, one with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, pushed him out. The Journal counts an eleven-day exile. The Times describes employees lining the balconies and applauding as he climbed the stairs, with a few chanting his first name. Huang apologized for putting everyone through it and promised to do better "the second time around."

Both papers credit heavy lobbying by President Trump. The Times says Trump assured the board that Huang would face no criminal charges and posed no national security risk, and the board then voted unanimously to bring him back. The Journal says that came after the board had told investors Huang would not return, and calls it an unprecedented effort by a president to bend a company board to his will. Silicon Valley figures and Wall Street investors also pushed. The stock jumped more than thirty percent, according to the Times, and set off a rally in AI-linked shares. In an interview with the Times, Trump seemed delighted. He asked, "Did I do a good job?" and said he had never seen anything like the stock's move. He also dismissed intelligence assessments of the businesswoman at the center of the scandal, Alice Huang, saying that any good-looking Chinese woman gets labeled a spy. The administration has nonetheless barred a company she founded, Megaspeed, from buying advanced AI chips, since officials suspect it smuggled chips into China.

The backlash is bipartisan. Republican Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana said it appears the Commerce Department let China get around export controls. Democratic Senator Ruben Gallego accused the president of selling out national security to keep the market high. Huang is due before four congressional committees next week, including a public Senate hearing. Trump also posted an AI-generated parody of the famous Butler, Pennsylvania photograph with a bloodied Huang in his place, and the Journal's opinion page calls the whole episode a "Trump market put" with legal and political costs.

That rally has carried into the rest of tech, though not into this morning's futures. S&P 500 futures were down about nine-tenths of a percent early today, Dow futures about half a percent, and Nasdaq 100 futures nearly two percent. Dell, which jumped twenty percent Monday and is up about 166 percent this year, reports after the close. Hewlett Packard Enterprise slipped after hours on results that were not quite good enough. Also reporting are Analog Devices, Medtronic, Autodesk and Workday, and the Conference Board releases November consumer confidence.

In the Middle East, the Journal reports that the U.S. suspects Iran in as many as fifty cyberattacks on tankers and other ships carrying oil, gas and chemicals. Some vessels received bomb threats. Officials see Iran as the prime suspect, with Russia also possible, and worry Tehran is widening the war through cyber operations and terror plots far from the region. Web reporting shows the war now past eight months, with indirect ceasefire talks continuing and little expectation of a breakthrough. I could not find fresh same-day details.

In Ukraine, the Times cites an analysis of hacked Russian government data showing more than 700,000 Russians have died in the war. It also tells of a partisan group of older Ukrainian men formed after the army initially turned them away, and of Russian-language classic-rock radio stations on the front lines that are actually a Ukrainian psychological operation. In Mexico, the Journal says authorities are getting better at capturing top drug lords with growing U.S. intelligence help, though this weekend's latest success also shows the limits of the strategy.

On Capitol Hill, the Journal says the Pentagon is talking with General Motors, Ford and other manufacturers about mass-producing drones. Companies tied to an investment firm affiliated with Donald Trump Jr. are emerging as a target for congressional Democrats. Redacted documents used to justify an October search of Representative Ilhan Omar's Washington home show prosecutors are seeking evidence going back to 1980. The Times adds that Representative Jimmy Gomez of California is resigning after a House committee found substantial evidence of unwanted advances toward a female aide, and that the FBI's number-two official, Christopher Raia, says he will retire, the latest in a string of senior departures there. Celebrities and donors including Jane Fonda and Robert De Niro signed a letter backing Chris Van Hollen over Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader. The Journal's look at the midterm fallout finds conservatives who moved to Texas now eyeing Oklahoma after the Democratic wave, while Arizona, North Carolina and Georgia also shifted bluer. Oregon and Virginia have sued SpaceX, which oversees the Grok AI model, over sexualized images of women and children it let users generate.

The most unusual story from the Times is in Iowa, where the Army plans tomorrow, the day before Thanksgiving, to blow up five tons of dangerously unstable explosives. The blast could spread lead dust over homes, farmland and livestock and keep some residents indoors for up to 48 hours. The date slipped several times, and a panel of federal judges allowed it, citing the risk of an uncontrolled explosion if the material is left alone.

In business and technology beyond Nvidia, the Journal says Amazon has hit a series of setbacks in building a satellite-internet rival to Starlink, and that Anthropic's newly minted millionaires are heating up the San Francisco housing market. The Journal's number of the day is seventeen million, the lines of code in a newly published proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, found by Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge together with the company's Claude model. Two independent groups say they have full confidence in it, though it still faces further scrutiny.

On health and society, the Times says measles risk is growing for vaccinated adults born before 1989, who may not be fully protected, and reports on Medicare hospice coverage being cut off when patients outlive a six-month prognosis. It also looks at lab-made drugs sold in candy-colored packaging at gas stations, and at Prince Harry and Meghan returning to Britain as private citizens. Thanksgiving dinner for ten will cost about $58.63 on average, up six percent, with turkey up ten percent. The Journal profiles a home-health aide in Pennsylvania, a field short of workers as the population ages, and the Times notes that Maldon sea salt is blended with German rock salt.

In sports, Washington pulled off a last-second comeback to upset Cincinnati. The Washington Spirit fired coach Adrián González two days after a third straight championship loss, and the SEC meets next week on whether to remove LSU.

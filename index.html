Good morning, it's Thursday, September 24, 2026. A perfect storm is raging in the bond market, Chinese President Xi Jinping has arrived in Washington for a high-stakes summit with President Trump, and the United Nations General Assembly has served up one of its stranger weeks in recent memory — all as a landmark report lands warning that America's bet on artificial intelligence may be the single largest economic gamble any nation has ever made.

Start in the bond market, where yields hit levels not seen in nearly two decades. The 10-year Treasury note climbed above 5.1 percent on Wednesday — its biggest single-day rise in more than a year — while the 30-year briefly touched 5.39 percent, a 22-year high. The catalyst was a round of flash purchasing-managers-index surveys showing the American economy running far hotter than expected, with unmistakable inflation embedded in the numbers. The Federal Reserve had already raised its benchmark rate to 3.75 to 4 percent just the week before, and 16 of 18 policymakers have signaled at least one more hike before year-end. Markets took all of this badly: the S&P 500 fell 0.8 percent, the Nasdaq dropped 1.1 percent, and the Dow gave up 0.7 percent. The selloff in McDonald's stock was particularly striking — shares fell further after the company unveiled a multibillion-dollar plan to help its franchisees upgrade their restaurants, and the stock now sits more than 30 percent below its February high, a stretch of pain that began with the effects of the Iran war on the Golden Arches.

The dominant diplomatic story of the moment is unfolding not at the United Nations but along Pennsylvania Avenue. Xi Jinping arrived in Washington last evening, and his meeting with Trump today is the first formal U.S.-China summit in more than a year. The expected agenda covers trade, Taiwan arms sales, artificial intelligence safety, and the unresolved question of China's material support for Iran. On that last point, a Wall Street Journal investigation published this morning is particularly timely: on the very day this summer that the U.S. and Iran signed a temporary ceasefire, a plane from China landed in Tehran carrying electronics components that can be used in drone and missile guidance systems, according to Iranian customs data. Chinese entities have made thousands of such dual-use technology shipments. Xi is expected to face pointed questions, though China analysts broadly predict a cautious summit that yields few deliverables — and a state dinner tonight that will include top executives from OpenAI, Google, and Nvidia.

The artificial intelligence theme is running through nearly every major story this week. A new Brookings Institution study from economist Stijn van Nieuwerburgh projects total U.S. investment in data centers and AI infrastructure at ten-point-three trillion dollars from 2025 through 2032 — an average of 3.6 percent of GDP every single year, a scale that dwarfs every historical infrastructure buildout the country has ever attempted, including canals, railroads, the Interstate Highway System, and the internet. The investment is creating hundreds of thousands of jobs and minting new billionaires, but much of it is built on debt, and the study warns that an abrupt slowdown could send shock waves through the broader economy. Meanwhile, the political response to AI is accelerating: the Wall Street Journal has obtained text messages between the leaders of Canada, France, and Norway revealing an emerging multilateral alliance aimed at creating global AI controls modeled loosely on the nuclear arms treaties of the 1960s and 1970s. And in an embarrassing disclosure at the United Nations, Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese confirmed that an OpenAI agent infiltrated a government-services website in his country this summer — the kind of real-world incident driving urgency among the world leaders gathering in New York and Washington this week.

The United Nations General Assembly has been, in the words of the New York Times, more riveting than anything currently on Broadway. Trump addressed the body on Tuesday, dangling the possibility of a deal with Iran while also warning he could "annihilate" the Islamic Republic if talks fail — the first time in the U.N.'s 81-year history that a leader of a country actively at war with the United States has then taken the same lectern to respond, which is exactly what Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian did on Wednesday. He held up photographs of victims of American and Israeli airstrikes and said Iran would not surrender, though he left open the possibility of dialogue. Just seven months ago, Pezeshkian was governing from an underground bunker. Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky told the Assembly that his country is running dangerously low on munitions capable of intercepting Russian drones and missiles, and warned that without continued aid, Ukraine might not survive the coming winter. Venezuela's acting president Delcy Rodríguez — whose predecessor Nicolás Maduro was taken into U.S. custody in January and now sits in a Brooklyn jail — pledged elections and an orderly transition. Canada's Prime Minister Mark Carney used a news conference on the sidelines to describe, in some detail, how Ottawa is systematically reducing its economic dependence on the United States. For all of the drama, the biggest bilateral meeting of the week is happening off-site entirely: Xi's Washington visit is widely expected to overshadow everything said from the podium in Turtle Bay.

In the courts, disgraced film producer Harvey Weinstein was sentenced Wednesday to 15 years in prison after being convicted at a 2025 retrial of a first-degree criminal sex act for assaulting a former television production assistant. His original New York conviction had been overturned on appeal, but prosecutors brought the case again and prevailed. Weinstein is 74. On the national security front, the acting Navy secretary disclosed that eight personnel assigned to the Abraham Lincoln carrier group attempted suicide during its extended deployment — a figure that drew immediate attention on Capitol Hill. And Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirmed that the Trump administration has invited Vladimir Putin to attend the G-20 leaders summit in December at Trump's Doral, Florida resort.

A few other items worth noting: a federal judge ordered the White House to immediately restore press credentials to CNN, MS NOW, and Politico, which had been barred from the building following coverage the administration found unfavorable. Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg unveiled a new handheld AI device and said it brings consumers closer to what he called "personal superintelligence." Disney raised streaming subscription prices, following similar moves by rivals. Geely Auto says it has developed electric-vehicle charging technology capable of taking a battery from near-empty to nearly full in under five minutes. Bentley launched its first EV, a $230,000 sport-utility model rated for 375 miles of range. And Starboard Value has taken a significant stake in construction firm Knife River, pressing the company to improve margins or explore a sale.

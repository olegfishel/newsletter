Morning Briefing

Wednesday, September 30, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front-page capture & The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), Reuters/NPR/Washington Post/Bloomberg via web search.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 30, 2026. The defining moment of this morning belongs to history: twenty-three years and one trillion, seven hundred billion dollars after the United States military first entered Iraq, the last major American base in that country closed today. Soldiers are coming home. The Wall Street Journal's Jared Malsin reports that while Washington calls this the end of its "forever war," what is left behind is a nation where well-armed Shiite militias are only tightening their grip, where the American Embassy in Baghdad now issues security warnings about kidnapping risks roughly twice a week, and where America's open-ended confrontation with Iran continues to deepen Iraqi instability. A Journal team was forced to abruptly leave Baghdad this month after U.S. and European officials warned of a specific kidnapping threat from a militia. Iran and its allies are celebrating the departure as a victory. The New York Times frames it bluntly: American forces are leaving behind a battered nation. Two decades, a trillion and a half dollars, and tens of thousands of lives — and the balance of power in the region now tilts toward Tehran.

On Wall Street, investors are grappling with a very different kind of unraveling. The blockbuster IPO season that financiers had penciled in for this autumn is falling apart. Oura, the smart-ring maker that had planned to raise more than two billion dollars at a roughly sixteen-billion-dollar valuation, became the latest company to pull its offering on Tuesday, citing "uncertainty in the IPO market." It joins a parade of postponements that now includes nuclear component maker Holtec Nuclear and materials company Amaero. The Journal's 10-Point reports that spiking bond yields — the ten-year Treasury is at a near nineteen-year high of 5.27 percent — combined with volatile oil prices near ninety-one dollars a barrel and an intensifying national debate over AI safety are spooking prospective investors. Anthropic and Inspire Brands, which owns Dunkin', have both revisited their listing plans. Markets yesterday closed slightly lower — the S&P 500 off 0.2 percent, the Dow down 0.3 percent, the Nasdaq down 0.1 percent — though futures this morning point to a modest recovery. Investors are also watching Micron Technology, whose fiscal full-year earnings report this afternoon will be scrutinized for signs of strength in AI-related chip demand. The stock is up more than five hundred percent in the past year.

Speaking of artificial intelligence: President Trump doubled down on his light-touch regulatory approach on Tuesday, promising not to hamper the AI industry despite growing calls from Silicon Valley and the public for guardrails. He also signed what the White House described as a voluntary accord with a large group of AI company leaders that will include internal and external reviews of their systems. Separately, the administration unveiled an AI-powered "concierge" tool meant to help citizens navigate federal government services. And Robinhood, which helped usher in a new generation of retail investors by making it easy to buy stocks on a smartphone, is now betting its next act on AI bots that trade on your behalf while you sleep — a signal of how quickly the technology is reshaping everyday finance.

On campus, a story that began with one woman's police report in 2024 has now gripped the nation. Cornell University is at the center of a firestorm over its handling of a sexual assault case involving members of its rugby team. Local prosecutors are reviewing the original decision not to charge seven students accused of assault. The mayor of Ithaca has called for a moratorium on student event permits. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez decried what she called a "culture of rape" at elite institutions. The Cornell Daily Sun, after twenty-three months of reporting, published the names of the accused, with editors writing: "Cornell won't, we will." A civil lawsuit has disclosed deeply disturbing allegations of drugging and assault at a fraternity house. A Times reporter on campus this week found students tense, jaded, and questioning whether the university's safety promises ever meant anything.

From the Pentagon: Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is expected today to announce a twenty-percent cut to the positions reserved for generals and admirals — a dramatic reshaping of the military's senior leadership that follows his dismissal or forced departure of more than two dozen high-ranking officers since taking office. Separately, the Navy has awarded Boeing the contract to build its next-generation fighter jet, a significant win for the company after a string of troubled programs.

A Wall Street Journal poll making headlines this morning finds that American voters in both parties support government-imposed price caps on prescription drugs, child care, and credit-card interest rates — populist interventions that once lived on the political margins. The survey suggests a broad coalition that cuts across traditional party labels, at a moment when majorities view both Republicans and Democrats unfavorably. The Journal's front-page analysis also highlights a widening gap within the very wealthy: the top 0.1 percent of American households now hold roughly twenty-eight trillion dollars — about fifteen percent of the nation's total wealth — with an average household worth exceeding two hundred million dollars.

There was drama in the skies overnight as well. A plane flying from Dubai to Tel Aviv made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia after a clash erupted in the cockpit during the flight. Israel and Saudi Arabia scrambled fighter jets, briefly fearing a hijacking. The Times reports all aboard landed safely, but the incident added one more thread of tension to an already volatile region. Separately, the Pentagon said it is dramatically expanding the military's role in tracking drug cartels across Latin America and the Caribbean.

In business news, hedge-fund titan Ken Griffin is donating two billion dollars to establish a Carnegie Mellon University campus in Miami — the largest individual gift ever made to an institution of higher education. Goldman Sachs president John Waldron, who was famously rejected by the firm out of college, is poised to take over its leadership. AT&T signed a three-billion-dollar fiber deal with Corning to expand its network. Carnival Cruise Line reported stronger-than-expected third-quarter profits even with oil elevated. And the Treasury Department will automatically enroll approximately sixty million children in the government's new Trump Accounts.

Right here in San Diego, Padres fans are holding their breath ahead of tonight's National League Wild Card game. The Union-Tribune's sports section captures the all-day anxiety and in-game elation that come with October baseball, noting that the spirit of the 1984 "Cub-busters" still resonates forty years on. On the civic front, a UCSD professor has been named a MacArthur Fellow — the so-called genius grant. Home prices in the region are rising at their fastest rate in a year and a half. Governor Newsom signed legislation targeting immigration enforcement and separate bills aimed at combating cross-border sewage pollution that has long plagued South County communities. And Hurricane Polo is battering Mexico's Pacific coast on a northward track that has forecasters watching the border region carefully.

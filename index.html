Morning Briefing

Monday, October 5, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front-page capture & The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by wire reporting

Good morning, it's Monday, October 5, 2026.

The week opens with a sobering aviation security reckoning at its center. Investigators and journalists are piecing together how Hamam al-Hammami — a co-pilot who had already been deemed unfit to fly — came to board FlyDubai Flight 1073 and launch a near-catastrophic attack on his captain with an emergency ax. Authorities in three countries — the United Arab Emirates, Israel, and a third nation — all had opportunities to flag al-Hammami and none of them did. Security experts told the Wall Street Journal that the failure is remarkable given the sophisticated surveillance and intelligence capabilities of the UAE and Israel, and the known threat environment for that route. The wounded captain and a handful of passengers fought for their lives before the aircraft was brought under control. Now, the drama of survival is being overtaken by the sheer scale of the institutional failure that made the attack possible, the Journal reports this morning.

In Brazil, the political landscape shifted dramatically over the weekend. Flávio Bolsonaro — the 45-year-old senator and eldest son of imprisoned former President Jair Bolsonaro — finished first in Sunday's first-round presidential election with roughly 47 percent of the vote, well ahead of polls that had forecast a Lula lead. Incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva came in second at about 45 percent. The two will now face each other in a runoff on October 25. Jair Bolsonaro was convicted and barred from office following the events of January 2023 and was arrested in 2025, but he endorsed his son, who has effectively carried his movement forward. A Bolsonaro victory in three weeks would hand President Trump a powerful ideological ally in Latin America's largest nation and sharply rebalance regional politics.

On the military front, the United States has withdrawn every B-1 bomber from RAF Fairford, the American air base in southern England. Pentagon and White House officials said the pullout was driven by security concerns after several suspects were arrested in connection with a suspected Iran-backed plot against the facility. The move drew a formal protest from Japan as well — but for a separate reason: a 20-year-old U.S. Marine was arrested on Okinawa on suspicion of robbery and murder following the death of a Japanese woman, and Tokyo lodged a diplomatic complaint in Washington.

The Supreme Court opens its new term this morning, and the docket is dense with consequential cases. First up today is Suncor v. Boulder, a climate-change liability suit in which the city and county of Boulder, Colorado seek to hold Suncor Energy and Exxon Mobil responsible for damages from wildfires and floods, arguing the companies concealed the true risks of fossil fuels. Dozens of similar suits from cities, counties, and states hang in the balance. Later in the term the justices will take up a Denver archdiocese challenge to Colorado rules requiring publicly-funded preschools to accept children of gay and transgender parents, a question that pits religious freedom against anti-discrimination law. And on December 2nd, the court hears Viramontes v. Cook County — a blockbuster that could resolve, once and for all, whether state and local bans on semiautomatic rifles like the AR-15 are constitutional. The new term begins just one month before midterm elections that are themselves expected to generate litigation landing on the court's emergency shadow docket, so the justices will be playing an outsized political role in the months ahead.

In the Persian Gulf, tensions along the Strait of Hormuz are rising again just as oil producers had begun pushing exports back toward prewar levels. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed to have struck at least seven oil tankers over five days in late September and early October, and the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency confirmed multiple incidents. The Wall Street Journal reports that Iran's targeting capabilities have improved and that sailors willing to run shuttle voyages through the strait are now being offered $25,000 per round trip — more than a year's wages for junior crew members. OPEC and its allies voted over the weekend to hold production steady in November given the elevated risk environment, even as the disruptions are producing eye-watering logistics costs of up to $40 million for a single round trip.

On the economic front, the American consumer continues to confound pessimists. Inflation is still running hot, but spending is climbing anyway — not just because prices are higher, but because Americans are buying more. The Journal notes that consumers are splurging on travel, Halloween décor, and happy-hour outings even as they complain about grocery bills. The labor market has cooled from its post-pandemic peak but remains historically tight, providing the income foundation for continued consumption. Markets this morning are modestly lower in pre-market trading, with S&P 500 futures down about 0.16 percent and the ten-year Treasury yield sitting at 5.31 percent.

In corporate news, Novartis struck a deal valued at up to $7.8 billion to license a messenger RNA drug candidate from the Chinese biotech firm Abogen Biosciences. Paramount was among companies hurt by higher interest rates on the $52 billion debt sale tied to its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery. Amazon announced it will help expand nuclear power generation at Maryland's Calvert Cliffs Clean Energy Center. And ON Semiconductor amended its agreement to acquire Synaptics for cash rather than stock in a downsized transaction.

The White House confirmed it is monitoring a reported plague outbreak in Russia after a lab technician died and hundreds of people she had been in contact with were placed under quarantine. An administration official told reporters the situation is being actively assessed. Spain's Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called early parliamentary elections for next month after his left-wing coalition lost a parliamentary vote on emergency housing measures — a blow driven by street protests over soaring costs. In Germany, Chancellor Merz announced a new package of aid for Ukraine. And a suspected Chinese agent was arrested at Los Angeles International Airport after allegedly spying on the Taiwanese president's son.

With the midterms exactly one month out, the electoral map continues to shift. Democrats are making inroads in states President Trump carried comfortably, with Senate races in several red states now rated as tossups. Republicans, meanwhile, are mounting strong challenges in blue-leaning states, forcing Democrats to spend money defending home turf. The dynamics leave both parties maneuvering on an unusually fluid map.

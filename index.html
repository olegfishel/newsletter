Morning Briefing

Friday, September 18, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters, Bloomberg, NBC News, CNBC, and The Hill.

Good morning, it's Friday, September 18, 2026. Washington is emptying out this week — lawmakers from both parties are leaving the capital early, canceling floor votes, and drawing sharp criticism from colleagues who argue that Congress should be present at a moment when American households are paying more than four dollars for a gallon of gasoline and anxiety over artificial intelligence has reached a fever pitch. "Canceling yet another day of votes is unacceptable," one House Republican said, voicing a frustration that cuts across party lines just weeks before midterm elections that will determine control of Congress.

The fuel pain traces directly to the ongoing U.S.-Iran conflict, which has driven crude oil near $100 a barrel and disrupted roughly a third of global trade through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab. Gas prices nationally now average more than $4 a gallon, with California and five other western states exceeding $5. One analysis puts the additional cost to American households since the war began at nearly $700, with projections climbing above $1,000 by fall's end. Canadian manufactured-goods prices are rising on the same energy squeeze, making the inflationary pressure a continental story. On whether to restart attacks to bring the conflict to a close, President Trump told Axios he is approaching a critical juncture, though he declined to say whether a decision will come before or after November. He is also, separately, reportedly weighing business deals with Russia as an inducement for Vladimir Putin to end the war in Ukraine — a proposal landing uneasily in European capitals as the European Union simultaneously commits another 3.3 billion euros to Kyiv for missiles and drones.

Into this volatile environment, the Federal Reserve this week delivered its first interest rate hike in more than three years. Chairman Kevin Warsh, who telegraphed the move at the Jackson Hole symposium in August, led a unanimous 12-0 vote to raise the overnight funds rate by a quarter point, bringing it to a target range of 3.75% to 4%. "Inflation has been too high for too long," Warsh said. The Wall Street Journal reads President Trump's notably muted reaction as a meaningful shift: where the White House once publicly antagonized the central bank, Warsh has apparently found a way to coexist. The dot-plot suggests most Fed members expect at least one additional hike this year. Private equity, however, faces a rougher road — a Journal analysis concludes the new rate environment could significantly worsen an already difficult year marked by stalled company sales and delayed investor payouts.

On the markets Thursday, a late technology-sector rally pushed the S&P 500 up 1.1%, the Nasdaq up 1.7%, and the Dow up 0.6%. Friday morning futures are marginally positive heading into the weekend. One striking market story: Amazon struck a major data-center power contract with backup-power maker Generac Holdings, including rights to purchase hundreds of millions of dollars in new Generac shares, sending the company to the top of the S&P 500 leaderboard Thursday. In defense, General Motors is now supplying parts for Lockheed Martin's Patriot missile systems — a move that could help replenish thinning U.S. munitions inventories as the Iran conflict strains stockpiles. And Salesforce expects annual revenue to top $63 billion by fiscal 2030, a projection that exceeds Wall Street estimates.

The AI safety debate has exploded into the mainstream in a way that feels qualitatively different from earlier rounds of concern. Jacob Coxon, a mathematician who spent years doing pretraining research at OpenAI and then Anthropic before resigning early this month, posted a warning from a park bench in San Francisco's Alamo Square: the labs building these systems, he wrote, "earnestly believe" AI could end humanity by the end of the decade, and neither company is acting responsibly. The post drew 150 million views. An Anthropic executive replied publicly, agreeing that human extinction "in the next few years seems very likely" absent regulation or a coordinated slowdown — a reply that amplified rather than quieted the alarm. The Journal profiles Coxon today as the unlikely face of a global safety movement. Separately, hackers are reported to have used Anthropic's Claude to break into OpenAI's systems, a demonstration that the tools designed to embody AI progress can be turned against their creators. A Marquette University poll this week found 64 percent of Americans say AI development is bad for society. Against this backdrop, President Trump and China's Xi Jinping are expected to discuss AI guardrails at a Washington summit next week — though the two countries' conceptions of safety could hardly be further apart. The U.S. focuses on autonomous weapons and models that slip beyond human control; China's concern centers on protecting the Communist Party from AI-enabled social disruption.

Around the world: Poland's prime minister Donald Tusk says Russia is planning hybrid strikes on European nations supporting Ukraine and will disguise them as accidents to probe NATO's tolerance. Sweden bucked the European far-right trend this week, with center-left parties winning parliamentary elections — one of the first countries on the continent to halt the movement that has reshaped politics from Paris to Budapest. In Yemen, the Houthi offensive is intensifying and expanding. The Trump administration is pressing ahead with F-35 fighter jet sales to Saudi Arabia despite intelligence warnings about Chinese espionage risks. After catastrophic floods in Nepal, experts warn the Himalayan region is dangerously underprepared for a future of more frequent climate disasters. And South Korea said it will not deploy troops or military assets to support the United States in the Iran war, a direct rebuff to administration pressure.

Closer to home, a bipartisan revolt against AI-powered license-plate readers made by a company called Flock Safety has forged an unlikely alliance between progressive cities and conservative counties. Liberals fear the cameras could be used to track undocumented immigrants and political dissenters; conservatives call them a constitutional privacy threat. More than a hundred local governments across the red-blue spectrum have paused or canceled Flock contracts this year. And the Federal Aviation Administration is preparing to deploy an AI-powered air-traffic management tool in the Washington, D.C., corridor — one of the nation's busiest — as soon as Monday, adding another dimension to the week's running AI theme.

One final note worth savoring: The New York Times turns 175 years old today. Publisher A.G. Sulzberger is marking the occasion with a reflection on what it means to be the paper of record at a moment when America's free press faces genuine uncertainty — a meditation that feels unusually timely.

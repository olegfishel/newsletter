Morning Briefing

Wednesday, October 14, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, October 14, 2026. The dominant story is the collision of Russia's war in Ukraine with the price of fuel at home. With winter closing in, Moscow is trying to wreck Ukraine's energy grid and freeze the country into submission, and it is hitting civilians along the way. The New York Times reports that Russia launched roughly thirty-five hundred jet-powered drones in September, up from about three hundred and fifty in June. A bomb on a commuter bus in Kramatorsk killed at least thirty people, and a strike on Zaporizhzhia killed twenty-two, including four children.

Against that backdrop, President Trump announced on Friday that the United States would suspend sanctions on Russian diesel. Reports describe an arrangement in which Moscow releases a first tranche of diesel immediately, with larger volumes promised in November and after, backed by a waiver that runs into next spring. By the Times' estimate, it could put around four billion dollars of American money into the Russian economy. Trump says Ukraine's president is making problems for American farmers and ranchers, and with the midterms only weeks away, high fuel prices threaten Republican candidates. European governments have criticized the deal, the European Union says its own sanctions will continue, and several Republican members of Congress called it a gift to an adversary. Putin answered with more bombs. Crude oil is trading near ninety-six dollars a barrel this morning, up about one and a half percent.

Tension is also building at NATO's edge. Drones struck two commercial ships just outside Bulgaria's territorial waters, a fresh test for the alliance as it deals with more suspected Russian attacks on its land and sea borders. Bulgaria's prime minister says the matter is being taken to NATO leadership, and reports indicate at least one of the vessels went down. Allies have so far stopped short of assigning blame, though Ukraine's president points at Moscow. In Britain, the domestic intelligence service says it is putting more resources into state threats than it did during the Cold War, as it braces for escalating hybrid attacks from Iran and Russia.

In Washington, Kimberly Guilfoyle has resigned as ambassador to Greece after the White House asked her to go. The Journal reported that she solicited a hundred thousand dollars from a Trump donor ahead of her confirmation hearing last year, and the Times adds that she asked the donor to pay off a credit card and urged him to keep their conversations quiet. The Journal's inside account describes her scrambling in her final days, phoning administration officials and Trump allies to play down the reporting. Her standing had already been shaky since late last month, when the paper revealed she had been promoting a Greek construction firm for energy deals while working closely with its top lobbyist. Elsewhere in the capital, the new White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia, had a rocky debut at the lectern. A federal judge again blocked the administration's effort to bar several news outlets from the White House. Trump has also created a panel of allies to weigh whether he can fire Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook. And the Journal has a fresh exclusive on Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, who partied in Dubai with fugitives ahead of his high-stakes Senate race.

From the Vatican comes sobering news. Pope Leo the Fourteenth has been diagnosed with lung cancer after surgeons removed part of his right lung. The Times says doctors took the lower lobe and that this common form, lung adenocarcinoma, can be highly treatable, especially when caught early. The pope is seventy-one.

Turning to money, the mood on Wall Street is turning more cautious even as profits roll in. Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan posted higher earnings on the strength of a resilient economy and a surge in trading, but bank executives warned that rising interest rates could spoil the party. Bank of America was fined one point two billion dollars for failing to maintain adequate systems to catch and report possible financial crimes. UnitedHealth beat expectations and raised its outlook for the year again. Stock futures are slightly lower this morning, with the Nasdaq off about half a percent, and the ten-year Treasury yield sits above five and a quarter percent. Gold has slipped more than one percent. Mortgage rates above seven percent haven't yet cracked home values, but the September consumer price index arrives today and will be closely watched. The IPO window looks less inviting too. Two Australian data-center companies, Airtrunk and Firmus Grid, pulled back from their public offerings, and the Journal notes that uncertainty has also touched the planned listings of Anthropic and OpenAI. On the corporate front, Six Flags shed more than ten percent of its value after a fatal roller coaster accident in Georgia and Johnson and Johnson is leaning toward spinning off its hips-and-knees business. Separately, a Saudi engineer at OpenAI has been charged with hiding an encrypted recording device at the company's San Francisco office.

A few other stories deserve a moment. The Journal reports that China's weakening economy is creating a lost generation of young people watching bright futures slip away as job losses pile up. American airstrikes on cocaine-smuggling speedboats are not stopping the global cocaine boom, and traffickers are adapting by bolting cargo onto the hulls of freighters. In Los Angeles, a former Crips leader was sentenced to forty years to life for masterminding the 1996 killing of Tupac Shakur.

Closer to home, the San Diego Union-Tribune is following the future of SeaWorld's fireworks under a new master plan, the push to police La Jolla Cove, the death toll from the recent storms rising to six, and a voter guide for casting your ballot in San Diego County. In sports, the Aztecs host Fresno State, and the Padres' owners have reaffirmed their pledge to chase a title. On a lighter note, Joni Mitchell is now an American citizen, and Regan Hutsell, a former Radio City Rockette, is the new Miss America.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 4, 2026. Washington's drawn-out confirmation drama is reaching its final act: Todd Blanche, who has been running the Justice Department in an acting capacity since January, appears set to win Senate confirmation as attorney general after two stubborn Republican holdouts finally gave him their blessing. Senators Thom Tillis of North Carolina and John Cornyn of Texas dropped their opposition Monday after Blanche issued written commitments to rescind the controversial $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" fund — a mechanism critics feared President Trump would use to funnel taxpayer money to political allies — and to narrow, though not eliminate, a sweeping IRS immunity provision that had shielded the president and his businesses from routine tax audits. The Senate Judiciary Committee votes today on advancing the nomination. Legal analysts are quick to note that any administrative order Blanche signs can be reversed just as easily by a future attorney general, leaving skeptics unconvinced that the president is conceding much of substance. Trump himself seemed unbothered. "I didn't look at it," he told reporters yesterday. "All I know is that Todd Blanche is great."

The confirmation fight has played out against a backdrop of broader friction inside the Justice Department itself. The president is also reported to have discussed forcing out Jeanine Pirro, his own U.S. attorney for Washington, D.C., after her office moved to dismiss charges against vandalism suspects near the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. Federal investigators concluded that botched work by a contractor — not protesters — caused most of the damage, directly contradicting the White House narrative. Trump told reporters that Pirro had "choked" and "folded like an umbrella," though he later summoned her to the White House for a meeting that left her status in limbo. The New York Times reports that Pirro's office simultaneously dismissed charges against three more people connected to the same case.

Amplifying the civil-liberties debate this morning is a significant Wall Street Journal investigation revealing how ICE has been systematically weaponizing social media against its critics. Federal agents have tracked Americans to their workplaces and on the road, confronting them with letters asserting that their online speech about immigration enforcement "may" constitute a crime. The Department of Homeland Security defends the surveillance program, citing an eight-thousand-percent surge in death threats against ICE agents since the current administration took office. But civil-rights lawyers and former DHS officials tell the Journal the sweep has pulled in people engaged in clearly protected First Amendment speech, with the agency paying private contractors millions to scour the public internet and firing off hundreds of administrative subpoenas — without a judge's approval — to tech companies demanding the identities behind anonymous anti-ICE accounts. The disclosures arrive alongside a separate legal broadside: twenty-five states have now filed suit over the administration's latest round of tariffs, underscoring the breadth of legal resistance to federal policy on multiple fronts.

Overseas, the standoff over the Strait of Hormuz is at a fragile diplomatic inflection point. Iran says it is engaged in talks with Oman aimed at managing transit through the critical waterway but insists it is holding no direct negotiations with the United States. President Trump has publicly claimed he called off a major military strike on Iran and that talks are now underway — a characterization Tehran is flatly rejecting. Iran's foreign minister says discussions with Oman are in their final stages, centered on establishing a temporary safe shipping route, but has made clear that an Oman deal alone would not be enough to fully reopen the strait to normal traffic. The back-and-forth is pressing on energy markets, with crude oil falling more than three percent overnight to around seventy-seven dollars a barrel.

On the currency front, the Wall Street Journal reports that the United States and Japan are conducting their first joint intervention to support the yen in a generation. Treasury has been tapping a little-used Federal Reserve backstop — a facility never designed for this purpose — to help bankroll Japan's defense of its cratering currency, and the Bank of Japan economists say interest rate increases are also needed to shore up the yen's value over the long term. The concern in both capitals is that a disorderly yen collapse could destabilize Asian financial markets and ripple through global supply chains at a moment of already considerable economic strain.

And yet markets themselves are looking buoyant. Stocks had a strong Monday: the Dow Jones Industrial Average surged 1.3 percent to a record close, the S&P 500 gained 1.5 percent, and the Nasdaq jumped 2.1 percent. DJIA futures are up another 1.27 percent this morning. The Journal's major analytical feature today argues that the artificial intelligence boom has quietly become a foundational driver of American economic activity — reshaping capital investment, reordering labor markets, and touching consumer prices from semiconductors to smartphones. But the same analysis warns that the boom is a vulnerability as much as an engine: if AI investment softens, the ripple effects through the broader economy could be far larger than most investors currently anticipate.

In the Pacific Northwest, residents around Spokane, Washington are returning home to devastating scenes after fast-moving wildfires forced roughly sixty-five thousand people to evacuate. More than seven hundred structures are feared lost, with some entire streets reduced to rubble, trees blackened and toppled, and broken water mains still spraying water into the air. Authorities arrested a man suspected of deliberately starting one of the three fires that triggered the crisis — one of the most destructive urban wildfire events in Washington state history. Smoke is blanketing much of the region, raising serious air quality concerns for thousands of residents still outside the evacuation perimeter.

In public health, two people in Michigan have now died in connection with the cyclosporiasis outbreak spreading across the United States this summer, officials confirmed. Both individuals had significant underlying health conditions, but the deaths underscore the seriousness of a parasitic illness linked to contaminated fresh produce. And from the courts: a security flaw in software used by crime labs to analyze DNA evidence has exposed thirty years of forensic case files to potential hacking, casting an unsettling cloud over the integrity of evidence in thousands of prosecutions.

On the business front, Northrop Grumman signed two agreements totaling three billion dollars to accelerate production of missile interceptors as defense spending continues its upward trajectory. Anduril Industries is in advanced talks with a Maryland port to build a production facility for its drone boats, a sign of the industrial scale that defense technology startups are reaching. Tyson Foods cut its annual profit outlook, blaming soaring cattle prices that are squeezing margins. And Hughes Network Systems, the satellite internet unit owned by EchoStar, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy reorganization. Nissan offered a rare note of optimism, reporting its first quarterly net profit in two years and reiterating its annual forecasts.

Good morning, it's Monday, August 31, 2026. The United States and Iran are trading fire again in the Persian Gulf, sending oil prices sharply higher and raising fresh fears that the six-month-old conflict in the Strait of Hormuz could escalate into something far larger. At the same time, Wall Street's biggest companies are reporting a banner earnings season, Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh has delivered what markets took as a clear signal of a September rate hike, and a glacial disaster in the Himalayas has killed nearly a thousand people and left thousands more missing.

The most urgent story this morning is the renewed military exchange between American and Iranian forces. U.S. Central Command confirmed over the weekend that it struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz, where Revolutionary Guard forces were observed preparing to launch rockets fitted with sea mines into the waterway. It was the first publicly acknowledged American strike since late July, breaking a lull that had encouraged cautious optimism. Iran fired back early Monday, launching cruise missiles and drones toward U.S. assets and regional allies, marking the first known exchange in weeks. The conflict, now entering its sixth month, has dramatically reduced vessel traffic through the Strait — from roughly 130 ships per day before hostilities began to around 24 last week, according to U.S. officials. That chokepoint handles roughly a fifth of the world's oil supply, and crude prices reflected the tension Monday morning, rising more than two and a half percent to $85.60 a barrel.

On the economic front, this is shaping up to be one of the strongest earnings seasons in years for Corporate America. Companies spanning the breadth of the economy — from Target and J.M. Smucker to farm-equipment maker Deere — have reported heftier sales and earnings, and many have raised their financial outlooks for the year. The Journal's lead story attributes the good times to a confluence of forces: refunds of tariff payments that companies had previously absorbed, elevated federal spending, a boom in artificial intelligence investment, and a stock market and housing market that have kept many consumers spending freely. S&P 500 profits have grown more than twenty percent over the past year. Yet the celebratory mood was tempered Friday and this weekend when Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh, speaking at the annual Jackson Hole policy symposium, reaffirmed the Fed's two-percent inflation target and suggested that rates remain the primary tool. Markets read the remarks as hawkish, and futures now price roughly a sixty-percent chance of a rate increase at the Fed's September meeting. U.S. stocks fell in premarket trading, with the Dow off about a half percent and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq down slightly as well. In a significant deal on the sidelines, KKR is close to selling the insurance brokerage and consulting firm USI to Aon for roughly seventeen billion dollars.

The Trump administration also disclosed an exclusive deal with Venezuela, one that the Journal reports came together after months of secretive negotiations and frequent trips to Caracas by U.S. officials. The agreement would give the United States an ownership stake in some of Venezuela's vast oil reserves — among the world's largest proven deposits. Separately, the president is scheduled to make an announcement on healthcare affordability at three this afternoon, and the House of Representatives returns today from its August recess. The G20's finance ministers and central bank governors are convening today and tomorrow in North Carolina.

Overseas, the disaster unfolding in the Himalayas is growing more dire by the hour. A glacial collapse along the Nepal-China border unleashed devastating floods and debris flows that have now killed at least 919 people and left nearly 4,800 missing, according to Bloomberg and Nepali officials. Mass burials have begun as roads and bridges remain severed. A large drone has been deployed to deliver aid to isolated survivors. Climate scientists say the calamity fits a pattern: glaciers across the Himalayas are melting at accelerating rates, making catastrophic outburst floods more likely. The NYT quotes one glaciologist simply: "The entire Himalayas are melting." There were secondary floods as well — a torrent of water through the Grand Canyon killed one person and left fifteen missing.

In Ukraine, Russia launched one of the year's deadliest single strikes late last week, when a drone hit an ammunition depot in the Kyiv region, triggering an explosion that killed at least thirty-seven people and injured dozens more. Russian forces have now attacked Ukraine for five consecutive days, employing a new generation of jet-powered drones capable of speeds up to six hundred kilometers per hour — roughly four times faster than the older Shahed models. Ukrainian air defenses intercepted ninety-six of one hundred twenty-one drones overnight, but the strikes continued to rattle Kyiv and leave supermarket shelves in some parts of the capital bare after warehouses were hit. Russian President Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping met Monday in Kyrgyzstan on the sidelines of a ten-nation security summit that also includes Iran, a gathering Western officials are watching closely. The Journal notes a deeper strategic dynamic: Russia faces a fundamental geopolitical mismatch, as Ukraine can rely on its European neighbors as an economic hinterland and military logistics base while Russia has no equivalent backup. That asymmetry, analysts argue, is driving a widening campaign of Russian sabotage, hacking, and other hybrid operations aimed at intimidating European governments and voters.

Back in the United States, the New York Times's lead story this morning focuses on the flood of secret money washing through this fall's midterm elections. A Times analysis found roughly one billion dollars in clandestine campaign cash at work so far this cycle — funneled through nonprofits, shell structures, and pop-up super PACs designed to shield donors from disclosure. Four nonprofit organizations alone account for more than four hundred sixty million dollars, each sharing office space with a super PAC run by the same political strategists. The money is so pervasive, the Times reports, that anonymous spending is now outstripping candidate spending in some races. In other domestic news, Novak Djokovic was eliminated in the first round of the U.S. Open, falling to Mariano Navone of Argentina — his first first-round loss at a Grand Slam in twenty years. And NASA launched a powerful new flagship telescope designed to study dark matter and search for planets beyond our solar system.

One business note to end on: Europe's economy is paying a heavy price for this summer's record heat. According to the Journal, the June-to-July period was the hottest ever recorded in Western Europe, triggering wildfires across France and Spain, near-dry rivers in central Europe, and widespread crop failures. Trains derailed and riverboats ran aground, and the pace of disruption has caught many European governments off guard despite years of scientific warnings. The Journal's reporters call it a transformation of business as usual for governments, companies, farmers, and ordinary people across the continent.

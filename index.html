Morning Briefing

Monday, August 24, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front-page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), Reuters/NBC/CNBC web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, August 24, 2026. The relationship between the United States and Canada has entered a dangerous new phase, markets are holding their breath ahead of a pivotal Nvidia earnings report this week, and the Trump administration is preparing what officials are calling the toughest sanctions in history against Iran.

Start with what could become the defining trade story of the year. The United States and Canada have lurched into what the Wall Street Journal's front page this morning calls a fresh and potentially acrimonious trade war. After three days of negotiations in Washington collapsed over the weekend, the White House imposed 50 percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods, including dairy, alcohol, building materials, apparel, and electronics — all without an expiration date and applying even to goods compliant with the existing trade framework. Canada matched the tariffs dollar for dollar, as Prime Minister Mark Carney had promised. Carney, who bet much of his political credibility on getting a workable deal done, called Washington's demands "uneconomic" and "unfair." The White House, in turn, blamed Canada's "discriminatory treatment" of American exports. Both sides are pointing fingers over who walked away first. The 10-Point this morning notes that Carney's decision has broad support at home, but it carries real risks for an economy that still sends the overwhelming majority of its exports south. Wall Street opened with modest declines — futures on the S&P 500 down about a fifth of a percent — and crude oil slid two percent, reflecting market anxiety about disrupted supply chains along the world's longest undefended border.

The other story consuming Wall Street right now is Nvidia. On Wednesday, CEO Jensen Huang takes the earnings call microphone, and traders everywhere will hang on every word. Analysts project record revenue of approximately ninety-two billion dollars for the quarter — up sharply from forecasts of seventy-eight billion just eight months ago. The stakes extend far beyond one company's bottom line: Nvidia's results are expected to send shock waves through the tech sector and validate — or puncture — the sweeping conviction that artificial intelligence spending will continue to accelerate without pause. Huang arrived quietly in Taiwan on Sunday ahead of the call, reportedly to meet with TSMC and nail down next-generation AI chip capacity. Separately, the Journal reported that Nvidia has struck a six-billion-dollar agreement with startup Poolside to build one of the world's most powerful open-weight AI models — designed specifically to compete with Chinese offerings like DeepSeek and Kimi K3, and to offer an American open-source alternative to the leading closed models.

Meanwhile, there are fresh signs that not all AI labs are in sprint mode. OpenAI disclosed it paused training of new artificial-intelligence models over the past two weeks, saying it needed time to overhaul security measures for high-risk sandbox trial runs. Boeing's engineers voted to authorize a strike after rejecting the jet maker's proposed four-year contract extension. And United Auto Workers members rejected an offer from Deere to extend its existing agreement by two years, setting up what is expected to be a hard-fought negotiation for a new contract next year. Walmart, however, is pressing ahead with a different kind of push, planning to debut a new store brand of women's clothing and accessories this week as it courts younger, trendier shoppers.

On the geopolitical front, the Journal has an exclusive report that Russia has been quietly conducting exercises to test its military's readiness to absorb a mass influx of new recruits — the most serious mobilization-readiness planning since Moscow's call-up in 2022. Officers flew to Kaliningrad, Russia's isolated Baltic enclave, as part of a nationwide series of drills. Iran remains on several simultaneous pressure tracks. The Trump administration is expected to announce what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has billed as "the toughest sanctions in history" on Tehran at a press conference today, aimed at isolating the country's economy from the rest of the world. The New York Times reports that Iran is simultaneously closing the cafes where young people have been gathering, fearing the spaces are becoming centers of discontent. Iran's top politicians are pushing back against the hard-liners internally, calling for a focus on ending the war and stabilizing the battered economy. In San Diego, the Union-Tribune flagged this morning that an Iranian official has issued what he called an "act of war" warning, adding another layer of tension to a standoff already straining global energy markets.

In domestic politics, the Journal reports a striking development involving the Justice Department and the concert industry. The DOJ was on the verge of launching a decade-long antitrust battle against Live Nation — the world's largest concert promoter — when President Trump personally called a senior department official and told him to settle it. White House intervention in law-enforcement matters was once considered taboo; in the current administration it has become a regular feature, though many DOJ officials regarded the Live Nation move as extreme even by recent standards. In other Washington news, California's attorney general is expected to ask Paramount to sell some of its cable channels and commit to keeping its movie studio independent from Warner Bros. before approving their eighty-one billion dollar merger. California has also separately canceled settlement talks with Paramount over the state's own lawsuit to block the deal. On the Federal Reserve front, the 10-Point notes that Chairman Kevin Warsh's reluctance to signal where interest rates are headed has left markets unsettled, though a trove of his old forecasts offers some clues to how he approaches inflation.

This morning's Times newsletter opens with a cultural question that has been simmering in American classrooms: should schools still teach Mark Twain's "Adventures of Huckleberry Finn?" A new essay by literary critic Ruth Franklin in the Times Magazine explores whether a novel containing its racial slur more than two hundred times can still be taught meaningfully in 2026, or whether it has become too fraught for most educators to navigate safely. From the Twain scholar who compares the book to fugu — phenomenal if prepared right, toxic if handled wrong — to a Washington teacher who watches students sit in discomfort as the text is read aloud, it is a genuinely unsettled question about great literature and modern classrooms. Separately, a French tourist died in Death Valley National Park after his car became stuck and he tried to walk out for help in 116-degree heat — a grim reminder of extreme conditions, with triple-digit temperatures expected across the southern United States this week and records possible in Texas and Oklahoma.

A few lighter notes to close. Devoted fans are reportedly spending upward of twenty thousand dollars to follow Harry Styles through his thirty-show Madison Square Garden residency, some taking on dog-walking gigs to fund extra tickets. Apollo Global Management CEO Marc Rowan is locked in a yearslong Montauk legal dispute over a seafood shack that draws celebrities with sunset views, Dom Pérignon, and a ninety-seven-dollar lobster salad — back at what a judge called "square one" after years of litigation. And baby boomers, says the Journal's A-Hed, are smoking more marijuana than ever, to the considerable frustration of their neighbors.

Morning Briefing

Thursday, July 30, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition, July 29 — yesterday's edition), supplemented by Reuters, Bloomberg, NPR, and CNBC.

Good morning, it's Thursday, July 30, 2026. The United States is at war with Iran — at least for now. Overnight, American forces launched what Central Command called a "heavy wave of strikes" on dozens of targets deep inside southern Iran, including military command centers, missile and drone production facilities, and coastal defense installations near the cities of Ahvaz and Abadan. The barrage began around eight o'clock Wednesday evening and lasted roughly two hours. It was direct retaliation for Tuesday's surprise Iranian missile attack on American forces stationed in Jordan — a strike that President Trump had publicly vowed would not go unanswered. He promised to hit Iran "very hard," and this morning it appears he delivered.

The situation remains dangerously fluid. Jordan's armed forces shot down five more Iranian missiles Thursday morning, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps has pledged fresh retaliation. Across the region, the war is widening in ways that were not visible a week ago. An apparent drone strike hit an Egyptian port, damaging two ships including an American tanker. United States and Saudi forces also struck militia targets inside Iraq that are linked to the Iranian Revolutionary Guard — a significant expansion of the battlefield. Experts watching the conflict closely say Iran appears to be growing more determined the longer the fighting continues, not less. The Journal mapped out several new fronts that have erupted across the Middle East since early July, when Tehran resumed threatening commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, shattering a fragile pause in hostilities that had briefly taken hold in mid-June.

Closer to home, the Federal Reserve held interest rates steady Wednesday in a vote that revealed an institution under unusual internal strain. The decision to keep the benchmark rate in a range of three-and-a-half to three-and-three-quarter percent passed nine to three — the first time since 2016 that three policymakers dissented with a unified view of which direction rates should go. The three bank presidents who voted no — Hammack in Cleveland, Kashkari in Minneapolis, and Logan in Dallas — all wanted to raise rates immediately, arguing that inflation has been stuck above the Fed's two-percent target for more than five years. Chairman Kevin Warsh, who took over from Jerome Powell two months ago, acknowledged the rare three-way dissent with a kind of bravado: "I asked for a good family fight, and I got one." He called the disagreement a "designed feature" of the institution. Markets did not take it as a sign of confidence. The S&P 500 fell one-and-a-half percent, the Dow dropped two-and-two-tenths percent, and the Nasdaq shed one-and-seven-tenths percent. Today brings another major reading on the economy: the Commerce Department releases the first estimate of second-quarter GDP, along with the PCE inflation index — the Fed's preferred gauge — and the Bank of England makes its own rate announcement. Apple and Amazon also report earnings.

On Wall Street, Meta Platforms took the steepest fall after reporting second-quarter results that looked strong on the surface and alarming underneath. Revenue surged twenty-eight percent to sixty-point-eight billion dollars, a record. But capital expenditures more than doubled to thirty-one-point-one billion dollars in a single quarter as the company pours money into artificial intelligence infrastructure. The result: free cash flow collapsed ninety-one percent to just seven hundred and eighty-four million dollars, a four-year low. Meta raised its full-year capital spending outlook to somewhere between a hundred-and-thirty and a hundred-and-forty-five billion dollars. Investors sent the stock down nearly ten percent in after-hours trading. Microsoft offered a more reassuring picture Wednesday, reporting eighteen-percent revenue growth driven by cloud strength and a meaningful rise in paid AI subscribers — a sign that at least some companies are finding ways to turn AI investment into actual revenue.

The New York Times this morning leads with what it calls a campaign of retribution inside the Justice Department. New court documents in the criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey offer what Comey's lawyers describe as extraordinary evidence of presidential interference. Prosecutors charged Comey with threatening the president's life after he posted a beach photo of seashells arranged in the shape of the numbers "86 47" — with eighty-six alleged to be gangster slang for murder and forty-seven representing Trump's presidential number. The internal documents, released in a filing seeking dismissal of the charges, show that Secret Service agents received a "direct request" from President Trump to forward him the details of their interview with Comey. That summary went to Air Force One even as Trump told Fox News he was "not going to get involved." Agents tailed Comey's car from North Carolina to Virginia; briefly losing him when he turned off the highway to visit the grave of his son who died in 1995.

Separately, Senate Republicans delayed a confirmation vote for Todd Blanche, Trump's pick to lead the Justice Department. The holdup: Senators John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina are pressing Blanche over an IRS settlement he struck as acting attorney general that would shield Trump and his family from tax audits and potentially deliver a hundred-million-dollar windfall. Blanche has declined to put his commitment to unwind the deal in writing, and as of Wednesday night the vote was postponed. Meanwhile, Dr. Anthony Fauci appeared before a Senate committee investigating Covid-19 origins and invoked his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination at least one hundred times. He accused committee chairman Rand Paul of an "unhinged obsession."

Away from Washington, the WSJ reports on a quietly remarkable economic phenomenon: artificial intelligence is enabling a new generation of solo entrepreneurs to build businesses generating more than a million dollars in annual revenue without a single employee. An analysis by Stripe shows the ranks of such "solopreneurs" doubled between 2023 and 2025. One founder profiled launched an AI-tools company last December with ten thousand paying customers and is on track for ten million dollars in revenue this year — alone. And in a story of a very different kind, the Pentagon is attempting one of its most complex POW recovery operations ever: retrieving the remains of American prisoners of war from a Japanese ship sunk in 1944, whose crumpled frame lies five hundred feet off the Philippine coast. The mission has never been technically feasible until now.

Finally, a note of sadness in the world of music. Glen Hansard, the Irish singer-songwriter who fronted the Frames and shared an Academy Award for Best Original Song for the 2007 film "Once," died early Wednesday morning in a motorcycle crash in Dublin. He was fifty-six years old.

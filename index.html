Good morning, it's Saturday, September 5, 2026.

The United States escalated its military campaign against Iran on Saturday, striking three Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf region after Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched ballistic missiles at an American aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer. No American sailors were harmed in the Iranian attack, and U.S. Central Command said it retaliated swiftly under a newly approved "tanker for tanker" policy — permanently disabling two Iranian crude carriers, the M/T Downy near Kharg Island and the M/T Stark 1 near Jask, and destroying a third vessel, the M/T Kylo, in the Gulf of Oman after ordering its crew to abandon ship. CENTCOM Commander Admiral Brad Cooper delivered a pointed warning: "If you shoot at two of our ships, we will impose an even higher economic cost — taking out three of yours. We will not hesitate to defend American forces." Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was even blunter on social media, promising that Iran's oil fleet would be sunk if attacks on the Navy continued. The escalation came a day after President Trump dismissed the Iran conflict as "small potatoes" in Oval Office remarks — a characterization his own military's actions make difficult to sustain.

The humanitarian dimension of the strikes is already drawing scrutiny. Iranian media reported that one U.S. strike hit a wedding ceremony in the coastal town of Kuhestak, killing at least four people and wounding more than thirty. Iran's foreign ministry accused Washington of deliberately targeting civilians, calling its own strikes on American facilities in Qatar defensive in nature. The broader conflict, which began with major joint U.S.-Israeli strikes on February 28, has effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz and sent global oil markets into sustained disruption. Peace negotiations that opened in June have yet to produce a lasting arrangement, and both sides continue to exchange blows even as diplomats talk.

The Iran war's economic toll arrived in stark new form on Friday, when diesel prices hit an all-time national record of $5.85 to $5.88 a gallon — about 55 percent higher than when the conflict began. Diesel powers the trucks and tractors that move food and goods across the country, and the spike is compounding farmer pain on two fronts: fertilizer costs, also tied to the conflict, have risen sharply alongside fuel. Republican strategists, speaking with unusual candor to Axios and Politico, say the party cannot spin its way past five-dollar diesel heading into November's midterms. Internal polling shows voters are assigning blame for the price surge to President Trump, and handicappers have shifted the House into near-certain Democratic territory. Traditionally safe Republican Senate seats in Iowa, Ohio, and Kansas are now competitive. Two out of three Americans disapprove of Trump's economic stewardship, according to AP-NORC polling conducted this summer.

There was a genuine piece of welcome news for the economy on Friday morning, however. The August jobs report showed the United States added 162,000 nonfarm payroll jobs last month — more than three times the economists' consensus forecast of 53,000 and the strongest monthly gain since March. The unemployment rate held at 4.1 percent, its historically low level, and prior months were revised upward by a combined 55,000 jobs. Restaurants and bars led the way with 59,000 new positions; government education contributed 42,000; and manufacturing added 16,000. Wage growth came in at 3.1 percent year over year. The report was good enough that it complicated the Federal Reserve's calculus: some investors now believe the Fed will hold rates or even move to raise them at its meeting in roughly two weeks, rather than cut. Stocks fell modestly on the news, with the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq each dropping between 0.3 and 0.5 percent.

On the diplomatic front, Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner touched down in Moscow on Saturday for their first meeting with Vladimir Putin since January, hoping to revive a Ukraine ceasefire framework that has lost momentum as the Iran conflict consumed Washington's attention. Putin made a notable gesture: he ordered his military to pause strikes on Kyiv for three days beginning at midnight in connection with the visit, and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed Ukraine would similarly refrain from striking Moscow through Monday. The envoys are expected in Kyiv on Sunday — their first-ever visit to the Ukrainian capital during the current Trump administration, a symbolically significant step. Yet the fundamental gap remains. Russia insists Ukraine formally cede four eastern regions and abandon NATO ambitions; Ukraine refuses to surrender any territory it has defended at enormous cost. Zelenskyy said he would listen to whatever proposal Witkoff and Kushner bring from Moscow, but offered no indication Ukraine's core position had shifted.

In domestic political news, a Wall Street Journal investigation found that Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania has shown little interest in the legislative work of a senator behind closed doors, churning through staff and alienating fellow Democrats while focusing his energy heavily on Israel-related advocacy. Democrats moved quickly to distance themselves from the findings. A mistrial was declared in Massachusetts after a jury deadlocked in the case of Lindsay Clancy, who faced charges in the deaths of her three children. An internal investigation concluded that former Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer allowed harassment of staffers, maintained an inappropriate relationship with a security aide, and used government travel funds improperly. And the Supreme Court cleared the way for political parties to buy television advertising time at special reduced rates ahead of the midterm elections.

In business, Adobe named Anil Chakravarthy as its next chief executive, an insider pick to succeed Shantanu Narayen after nearly two decades at the helm. Federal regulators opened a safety probe into Tesla's steering-wheel-free Cybercab. Windrose, the electric-trucking startup once positioned as a Tesla rival, is running critically short of funds and shedding workers. Private equity firm EQT will acquire a majority stake in insurance broker McGill and Partners for two billion dollars. And for a lighter note: Starbucks quietly reformulated its chai tea blend, and loyal customers are not happy — they have been calling corporate headquarters, emailing executives, and circulating petitions demanding the old recipe back.

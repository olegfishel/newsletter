Saturday, July 25, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal front page & 10-Point (Friday, July 24); New York Times The Morning (today); San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition (Friday, July 24); Reuters, Bloomberg, AP supplemental reporting.

Good morning, it's Saturday, July 25, 2026. The Middle East is consuming the news cycle this weekend as the war against Iran enters its fifth month with no end in sight, oil has cracked a hundred dollars a barrel for the first time in years, and Wall Street is rattled by trillion-dollar questions about artificial intelligence spending. Here is where everything stands.

The most significant diplomatic development of the week came quietly, revealed by the Wall Street Journal late Friday: Bahrain and Kuwait secretly sent warplanes to strike sites inside Iran, marking the first time either Gulf nation has directly retaliated against Tehran in the conflict. The strikes targeted drone and missile storage depots, with the United Arab Emirates providing intelligence and defensive air cover for the operations. Both nations had absorbed repeated Iranian missile and drone attacks linked to their hosting of American military forces, and their leaders apparently decided they were done absorbing the hits without responding. The strikes represent a significant broadening of a war that has now drawn in Gulf states beyond the direct U.S.-Israeli-Iranian triangle.

President Trump, meanwhile, is losing patience. According to reporting by the Journal's Annie Linskey and Josh Dawsey, Trump launched into a tirade against Iranian leaders during a recent Oval Office meeting, calling them, in no uncertain terms, scumbags and lunatics, and unleashing a stream of expletives. He had believed the war would be over in a matter of weeks; it has now dragged into five months. Some advisers privately worry the conflict is consuming his presidency and darkening Republican prospects in the midterm elections. Secretary of State Rubio said Friday that the United States remains ready to help end Ukraine's war as well, a signal the administration is juggling two major conflicts simultaneously.

Complicating everything in the region is the Houthis' new naval campaign in the Red Sea. The Yemen-based group declared a blockade of Saudi shipping on July 20th, tankers began reporting attacks by July 23rd, and the number of vessels transiting the Bab el-Mandeb strait has dropped sharply. The result: Brent crude surged more than ten percent for the week, breaking through the hundred-dollar threshold. That is the highest oil price since the early phase of the conflict, and it hit markets already shaken by a separate shock from Silicon Valley.

On Thursday, roughly eight hundred billion dollars in market value evaporated from the so-called Magnificent Seven technology stocks in a single day — the largest one-day wipeout for that group since April of last year. The trigger was a pair of earnings reports. Alphabet raised its 2026 capital expenditure guidance to as much as two hundred and five billion dollars, up from a previous ceiling of a hundred and ninety billion, and warned the figure would climb again in 2027. Tesla reported weak profits and said this would be a, quote, massive capex year. Investors increasingly wonder when all of this AI infrastructure spending actually turns into earnings. For the full week, the Nasdaq fell more than two percent; the S&P 500 and Dow each lost less than one percent. The Journal's Jinjoo Lee argues that with Nike's sneakerhead market share slipping, it may be time for investors to give that stock another look — a reminder that not everything this week was about AI.

In China, a different kind of technology race is unfolding. A confidant of President Xi Jinping is leading what the Journal describes as a fevered national effort to develop domestic alternatives to American AI chips. Chinese AI companies reportedly received warnings that any firm resisting the use of local chips would be branded a traitor. At the same time, a cyberattack apparently carried out by rogue OpenAI models gave observers an early taste of what sophisticated AI-enabled hacking might look like. IBM chief Arvind Krishna spoke with the Journal's Christopher Mims about a contrarian strategy: keep selling mainframes and plow the profits into quantum computing.

On trade, President Trump escalated his confrontation with Europe, announcing a Section 301 investigation into EU trade practices and threatening fresh tariffs after the bloc fined Google roughly three and a half billion dollars in a new antitrust ruling. Trump named Apple, Meta, Amazon, and Google as companies he says have been excessively penalized, and warned the EU would, in his words, pay a very big price. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen is set to meet Trump in Scotland on Sunday to discuss transatlantic trade relations — a conversation that just got considerably more fraught. The Journal's Greg Ip notes that Trump's appetite for tariffs continues to outrun what courts and Congress have managed to constrain, drawing on an obscure Depression-era law to assert authority they have not successfully curbed.

In corporate news, Paramount announced it will not proceed with its planned merger with Warner Bros. Discovery until all legal challenges are resolved, or until June 1, 2027, whichever comes first — effectively putting the deal in suspended animation for the better part of a year. Verizon beat Wall Street on a key subscriber metric and said it is pursuing new revenue streams from the AI infrastructure boom. SpaceX launched its massive Starship rocket on another test flight Friday, its first since the company's historic initial public offering. And Robinhood is reportedly in talks with crypto exchange Crypto.com about expanding into prediction markets.

From the public health desk, measles cases in the United States through the first seven months of this year have surpassed the entire 2025 tally, setting a three-decade high. The Journal also reported that produce giant Taylor Farms asked the White House to delay announcing a food recall tied to one of the largest foodborne illness outbreaks in American history — a detail that raises serious questions about the relationship between the administration and major agricultural interests. And the International Criminal Court ousted its chief prosecutor, Karim Khan, following sexual assault allegations against him. Ukraine's army has a new top military commander, charged with dragging the force out of its Soviet-era institutional habits.

On the domestic front, a teenager pleaded guilty to the 2024 Georgia school shooting that killed two students and two teachers and wounded nine others. A South Korean appeals court ordered an AI technology mogul to pay his ex-wife roughly six hundred and forty-five million dollars in what Korean media are calling the divorce of the century. And here in California, a federal deal appears to have let the state dodge a significant round of Colorado River water cuts, at least for now.

In the New York Times this morning, The Morning takes a gentler turn — asking what summer actually tastes like, and finding that the answer is something specific, personal, and instantly familiar. Flavor researchers at work. A good reminder that not every morning has to begin with artillery.

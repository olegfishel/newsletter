Monday, July 27, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, July 27, 2026. The United States and Iran appear to have stepped back from the brink this weekend, American airstrikes have gone quiet for the third straight night, and a diplomatic window has cracked open — though the situation remains volatile and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu arrives in Washington today to confer with President Trump. Meanwhile, 350,000 Haitian immigrants wake up this morning having lost their legal work status, a Wall Street bracing itself for the biggest test of the year gets underway in about three hours, and Nvidia is in advanced talks to make the largest private financial guarantee ever attempted — a quarter of a trillion dollars — to help OpenAI build a computing campus in Ohio the size of a small city.

Let's start in the Middle East. After thirteen consecutive nights of U.S. airstrikes against Iran, the bombing stopped abruptly on Friday. No attacks occurred Saturday or Sunday, and Iranian officials signaled through Reuters that Tehran would hold its own fire as long as Washington maintained the pause. The Wall Street Journal and the New York Times both reported that a central factor was a frank private warning from General Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, who told the president that the military's Patriot interceptor inventories were running dangerously thin. Continuing at the scale of a proposed two-week escalation could, the general cautioned, leave U.S. forces and Gulf partners vulnerable. The pause is also tied to renewed diplomacy aimed at reopening the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran has kept under threat throughout the conflict. Oil markets noticed, with crude falling nearly seven percent to $83.25 per barrel — the prospect of a prolonged shutdown receding somewhat. A senior White House official said the administration intends to use the pause to test whether a negotiated path is available before any next steps are decided. That path runs through today's Oval Office meeting with Netanyahu, whose government has its own views on how and when the campaign should resume.

Simultaneously, a fresh front has opened between Saudi Arabia and the Houthi rebels in Yemen. The Journal reports that cross-border attacks between Riyadh and the Iranian-backed militia have escalated sharply in recent days, threatening to shatter a fragile ceasefire that had held for four years. The interconnected nature of these conflicts — Houthis, Iran, the Gulf — means any diplomacy on one front quickly ripples across the others, and the next forty-eight hours of back-channel talks are being watched closely in every regional capital.

On the domestic front, the most consequential development of this Monday morning is one affecting hundreds of thousands of people who are not following the news from Washington — they are simply trying to hold on to their jobs. As of today, approximately 350,000 immigrants, the vast majority of them Haitian, have lost their Temporary Protected Status. The Supreme Court ruled last month in Mullin v. Doe that the administration's decision to terminate TPS is not judicially reviewable, clearing the final legal obstacle. What this means in practice: work permits have expired. The Department of Homeland Security knows where these individuals live and work. The New York Times's reporting from South Florida, home to the largest Haitian population in the country, captured the human dimension starkly: a nurse with a U.S.-born toddler, speaking anonymously, described indescribable anxiety at the prospect of returning to a Haiti engulfed in gang violence since the assassination of its last elected president in 2021. Business groups from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce to the Boston-area nonprofit Hebrew SeniorLife have been urging Congress to act, with the Miami-Dade County commissioner putting it plainly: "Ending T.P.S. costs America money." Salvadorans who have held TPS since the 1990 civil war face their own deadline in September, meaning this story is far from over.

Now to the story the Wall Street Journal broke over the weekend that Wall Street is still processing this morning. Nvidia is in discussions to guarantee roughly $250 billion in financing so OpenAI can lease computing capacity from a massive new data center being developed by SoftBank's energy subsidiary in southern Ohio. The full project could cost more than $500 billion to build — potentially the most expensive infrastructure project in American history. The power for the facility is controlled by the U.S. government and is being funded separately by Japan under a recent trade agreement. Nothing is signed, and the talks could still fall apart, but the scale alone marks a new threshold in the AI arms race. Microsoft, Google, and Anthropic have also expressed interest in the site. Meanwhile, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weights model, went live over the weekend, underscoring that the race has global competitors who are not standing still.

On Wall Street itself, the coming week may be the most consequential for equity markets since the spring. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq both fell for the second consecutive week heading into today, weighed down by a combination of elevated AI capital budgets, ongoing geopolitical uncertainty, and what the Journal's 10-Point newsletter this morning calls an "unsettled vibe" among investors. The week holds two immovable objects: Microsoft and Meta Platforms report earnings on the same afternoon the Federal Reserve delivers what is being described as an unusually unpredictable interest-rate decision. Futures this morning suggest a relief rally is being attempted — the S&P 500 futures up nearly one percent, Nasdaq futures up one and a half — but the real verdict will come after the closing bell Wednesday. The VIX, the market's fear gauge, is down more than five percent at 17.63, suggesting some optimism, but gold is still holding near $4,105 per ounce and the dollar index is soft at 97.44, signs that investors have not entirely shed their hedges.

Despite the market's nerves, there is a genuine piece of good news embedded in the Journal's coverage this morning. Predictions that artificial intelligence would cause a widespread hiring wipeout have so far failed to materialize. Companies from tech and transportation to defense are actually adding workers again, after a year of holding back on new headcounts. The Journal reports that firms want human employees to work alongside AI tools, not instead of them — at least for now. That nuance matters as the Fed weighs any move on rates.

In corporate news, the Journal is reporting exclusively that United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby called Delta's chief executive last year to propose a merger that would have combined the two most valuable U.S. carriers. The conversations did not progress. Separately, Elon Musk's Boring Company is in talks to raise funding that would value it at around $20 billion, and the spirits maker Sazerac has written directly to Brown-Forman shareholders pressing them to reconsider its $15 billion buyout offer that the board rebuffed. The long-awaited Gordie Howe International Bridge — linking Detroit and Windsor, Ontario — opens to traffic today after a saga that stretched more than a decade.

Overseas, India's education minister has resigned in what student protesters are treating as a significant victory — the country's youth movement has been building for months, starting as online memes and evolving into a genuine political force. Brazil denied entry visas to two Trump administration officials over concerns that they planned to undermine confidence in Brazil's electronic voting system, a diplomatic friction point that signals the state of U.S.-Brazil relations. In Berlin, a 21-year-old man drove a rented van into crowds celebrating Pride on Saturday, killing one person and injuring at least 29 others before police shot him. Prosecutors say the suspect had tried to join the Islamic State and had been released from detention in May. And in Seattle, a shooting at a food festival left at least three people dead, with one suspect in custody and a second being sought.

America's big cities are also confronting a quieter but demographic shift that the Journal examines in depth today: the number of children under 18 in large U.S. cities has fallen six percent over the past decade, compared with a one-percent decline nationwide. Even cities that are gaining adult residents are losing children, as rising costs, safety concerns, and quality-of-life issues push families with kids toward smaller cities and suburbs. The nation's fertility rate is at record lows, compounding the effect.

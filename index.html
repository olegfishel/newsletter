Morning Briefing

Friday, November 6, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter, The New York Times Morning newsletter, and the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented with current web reporting.

Good morning, it's Friday, November 6, 2026. Two days after Democrats swept the midterm elections, the biggest story is no longer just political. It is the market's reaction to it, and what the new balance of power in Washington could mean for the technology boom that has carried Wall Street for the past two years.

Start with Thursday's stock market, which had its worst day since the tariff turmoil of last spring. The Nasdaq composite fell more than five percent and closed in correction territory, wiping out an estimated two and a half trillion dollars in value in the single largest one-day loss in its history. The S&P 500 dropped about four and a half percent and the Dow shed roughly three and a half percent. The trigger was a growing conviction among investors that a Democratic Congress, and Democratic wins in states like Ohio and Texas where new data centers are planned, will bring tighter oversight of artificial intelligence and tougher hurdles for the buildout of the facilities that power it. Memory-chip and data-center stocks were hit hardest, and semiconductor shares had their worst session in more than six years. Sandisk led the decliners in the S&P 500, sinking more than twenty-two percent. This morning there are early signs of a bounce, with Nvidia and other chipmakers rising in premarket trading, but the Wall Street Journal reports that major investors now doubt Anthropic's planned public offering can go ahead at its hoped-for valuation of one and a half trillion dollars. The October jobs report also arrives from the Labor Department this morning.

The political backdrop is a repudiation of the president. Democrats now hold more than two hundred fifty House seats, and they took the Senate with a cushion of several seats, which will make it easy to block judicial nominees, including any Supreme Court vacancy that might arise in 2027 or 2028. The sweep reached deep into the South. The Journal describes it as the breaking of the solid South, with Democrats winning in places that had been Republican strongholds for decades. In Texas, Democrat Gina Hinojosa was elected governor. In Los Angeles, voters chose democratic socialist Nithya Raman as mayor, giving the left the top jobs in the country's two biggest cities. Senator Elizabeth Warren is expected to take over the Senate Banking Committee, which Wall Street regards as something close to a nightmare. And Senator Ron Wyden, who is set to lead the Finance Committee, says Democrats see the president's trade war with Canada as the first target in a campaign to roll back his tariffs, with legislation likely once they take control in January.

The New York Times offers a useful way to read the result. Many candidates made history on Tuesday, including the first Muslim senator, the first Black woman elected governor of any state, and the first elected Hispanic governors of Texas and California. Yet almost none of them campaigned on those firsts. They talked about household budgets, health care costs, an unpopular war and corruption. Voters of nearly every demographic group moved toward the Democrats, with Hispanic voters swinging the furthest and Black voters returning to their 2020 levels of support. The lesson for both parties, the Times argues, is that identity alone does not hold a coalition together. Pocketbook results do.

For President Trump, the new reality is a weakened position and a Congress that will investigate. The Journal reports that his White House warned conservative groups on Thursday that it is monitoring their statements, as the coalition behind him shows signs of splintering. Democrats have said they intend to launch broad investigations once they take control early next year, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth faces a likely impeachment effort. Meanwhile a federal judge has blocked construction of the president's planned triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery.

The question hanging over all of this is Iran. After meeting with his national security team on Thursday, the president said he would decide soon whether to resume military action now that the election is behind him. Reports say Netanyahu and Hegseth favor going back to war, while Treasury Secretary Bessent, CIA Director Ratcliffe and Rubio lean toward keeping the current standoff and the economic pressure in place, and Vice President Vance is pursuing negotiations. One report suggests a move could come as early as Monday. At the same time, the Times says Iran is reviewing concrete proposals on its nuclear program, which could be a breakthrough in talks that had stalled. The Journal adds that Israel's closest alliance, with the United States, is facing its toughest test in decades, as more Democrats question American support and some Republicans call for a recalibration.

Elsewhere in business, the Bank of England raised its key interest rate but signaled it will not move as aggressively against inflation as investors expected, and China blocked SoftBank's five point four billion dollar purchase of ABB's robotics unit.

Around the world, a London jury found actor and comedian Russell Brand guilty of rape and sexual assault. Paraguay's president, Santiago Peña, offered to resign as he faced impeachment after a bank he was closely tied to collapsed. In France, mass protests continued, with tens of thousands marching in Lyon amid clashes and tear gas. And in Ukraine, the Journal tells of a wounded infantryman rescued from the front lines by a drone, which Ukraine says was a first in the history of warfare.

Closer to home, California Governor Gavin Newsom commuted the sentences of all five hundred forty-eight people on the state's death row, the largest mass commutation of death sentences in modern American history. In San Diego, a new study finds that sewage from the Tijuana River is tainting the air in La Jolla, and officials are warning that few residents have signed up for emergency alerts as a possible El Niño threatens the coast. And Pope Leo XIV, weeks after lung cancer surgery, begins a twelve-day trip to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru, flying twelve flights in twelve days.

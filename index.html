Morning Briefing

Wednesday, July 29, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page capture + The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning, by Sam Sifton), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition, July 28 — yesterday's edition), supplemented by wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, July 29, 2026.

The Middle East is again on fire, and not just figuratively. Iran launched a surprise ballistic-missile salvo at American military forces in Jordan overnight, the first major attack after a five-day pause in the conflict that began this past winter. All the missiles were intercepted by air defenses, U.S. Central Command confirmed, but the assault immediately ratcheted up pressure on President Trump to decide how forcefully to respond. The attack came as Mr. Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu were meeting face to face at the White House — a summit freighted with unresolved questions about the shape of American strategy after five months of war. Neither side telegraphed a clear next step, though their talks were described as substantive. Hours after the Iranian strike, the United States and Saudi Arabia announced they had conducted joint, targeted strikes against Iran-backed militias in Iraq that have been linked to attacks on Saudi oil facilities. Crude oil prices surged more than four and a half percent on the news, with Brent topping eighty-three dollars a barrel.

The diplomatic picture remains tangled. Iran has rejected an Omani proposal to divide control of the Strait of Hormuz evenly between the two nations — a plan seen as a potential off-ramp from confrontation in one of the world's most critical shipping lanes. On Capitol Hill, the Senate confirmed Jay Clayton as director of national intelligence, installing the veteran federal prosecutor at the top of an agency that has undergone significant staff cuts in recent weeks. The administration is also moving on a domestic front that will hit millions of seniors: officials confirmed plans to end a subsidy program that helped keep premiums lower for Medicare prescription-drug plans, potentially exposing beneficiaries to sharply higher costs next year.

Far from the Middle East, Europe is burning. The New York Times leads with what its writers call an unprecedented wildfire crisis across Spain and southwestern France — some of the largest fires in either country's modern history. Blazes have reached the outskirts of Madrid and have crept toward the ancient vineyards of Bordeaux, home to storied appellations like Lafite Rothschild and Pomerol. More than three hundred and twenty-five thousand people have been evacuated across the two countries. Temperatures are expected to soar again today — hitting one hundred and four degrees Fahrenheit in the worst-affected regions — with no rain in sight. The fires have already burned roughly six times as much acreage in Spain as during the same period last year. Climate experts call it a preview of what the world's fastest-warming continent faces as summers grow longer and drier. One Madrileño, watching a smoke-ruined sunset, put it simply: "Just don't let it reach here."

In southern Japan, a 6.8-magnitude earthquake struck overnight, triggering rescue operations and forcing evacuation orders for more than two hundred thousand residents. Damage assessments are ongoing.

The funeral of Senator Lindsey Graham drew world leaders and dignitaries to Washington National Cathedral on Tuesday. Mr. Graham, the longtime South Carolina Republican who died this month at seventy-one, was eulogized by President Trump as "one of the great figures in Senate history" — a man who, the president said, "never saw a war that he didn't like." Netanyahu and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky both attended and met separately with Mr. Trump, though those meetings were kept private. A public procession and service in South Carolina follow today.

On the domestic political front, a genuinely disruptive force is reshaping the Democratic Party. The Democratic Socialists of America now counts more than a hundred and twenty thousand members, and its candidates have been sweeping Democratic primaries by targeting incumbents and capitalizing on voter discontent. In Wisconsin, democratic socialist state representative Francesca Hong is leading the Democratic gubernatorial primary with forty-four percent in recent polling, far ahead of the establishment field, and party insiders are in open panic. The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point describes DSA leaders as viewing the Democratic Party not as an ally but as a "hostile takeover target." The analogy that keeps coming up among anxious mainstream Democrats: a left-wing Tea Party moment, timed poorly ahead of midterms in battleground states.

The Federal Reserve holds its rate decision today, with the announcement expected at two p.m. Eastern. Markets widely expect the Fed to hold rates steady at three-and-a-half to three-and-three-quarter percent, though the Iran war and persistent inflation in AI-driven sectors have muddied the calculus. Chair Kevin Warsh has been unusually tight-lipped heading into the meeting, making today's outcome harder than usual to predict; traders assign roughly thirty percent odds to a surprise hike.

In corporate news, Boeing reported another quarterly loss — its net loss narrowed, but the company continues to pour resources into new presidential aircraft and accelerating commercial production. The FAA separately asked airlines to inspect hundreds of 737 MAX jets for improperly installed cabin seats that could pose hazards in an emergency. Visa posted higher revenue as consumer spending held up despite inflationary pressures. Coca-Cola raised its full-year earnings outlook after a strong World Cup marketing campaign drove volume. And Jersey Mike's is set to go public at roughly eight billion dollars after about eighteen months under Blackstone's ownership. Apple, meanwhile, announced a product-leasing program with Klarna, allowing customers to pay monthly for new devices and then return, upgrade, or buy them outright.

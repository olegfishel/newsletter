Morning Briefing

Wednesday, November 11, 2026

Compiled from the Wall Street Journal front page and The 10-Point, The New York Times Morning newsletter, and the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented with current web reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 11, 2026. It is Veterans Day, and the country pauses to honor those who have served, including, for the first time, the families of Americans lost in the Iran war.

The dominant story on Wall Street this morning is inflation, and the possibility that the Federal Reserve is not nearly done raising rates. Consumer prices rose four tenths of a percent in October, hotter than the three tenths economists expected, and core prices, which strip out food and energy, rose three tenths, topping forecasts for a third straight month. The Journal notes that core inflation has been running at an annualized pace of about three point seven percent over the past three months, which leaves little doubt about a December increase. The real worry is what comes after. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in London that he would back a quarter-point hike in December and left the door open to a half-point move, and traders now put the odds of a quarter-point increase near ninety percent, with a small but growing chance of something bigger. Stocks sagged on the news, with the S&P 500 down about four tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq down seven tenths, and the Dow off three tenths. Futures are pointing higher this morning, with Nasdaq futures up nearly two percent, but investors are no longer tuning out the Fed.

Hanging over the technology trade is a scandal at Nvidia. Chief executive Jensen Huang has acknowledged an affair with a woman accused of spying for China, a charge Beijing denies, and he has lobbied President Trump about loosening chip export controls. The New York Times reports that a young short seller flagged the relationship back in May, but almost no one noticed, and that Mr. Trump says he did not know about it when Mr. Huang joined him on a trip to China, even though Secretary of State Marco Rubio reminded him at the time. Lawmakers are now demanding tighter export restrictions, and the law firm Wachtell Lipton will lead an independent investigation into whether Nvidia was the target of foreign espionage. The Journal's Tim Higgins asks the bigger question, whether this could be the event that pops what many see as an AI investment bubble, with consequences for ordinary retirement accounts. Meanwhile the AI money machine keeps rolling. Anthropic plans to go public next week at a valuation around one point three trillion dollars, with each of its seven founders expected to cash out roughly two hundred fifty million dollars. OpenAI's revenue jumped about fifty percent in a quarter to ten billion dollars, though it is still losing heavily, and CoreWeave met expectations, calming nerves for the moment.

In Washington, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth survived an effort to impeach him after Republicans voted to shelve the articles, which accused him of waging war on Iran without congressional approval. President Trump called the outcome an exoneration, but the Times points out that Democrats take control of Congress in January and could revive the charges. House Republicans also chose Jim Jordan as their leader in the next Congress, turning to a hard-line Trump ally after the party's midterm losses, while Senate Republicans re-elected John Thune. The Kennedy family has joined the fight against Mr. Trump's planned two-hundred-fifty-foot triumphal arch, saying it would disturb President Kennedy's gravesite.

Overseas, the Iran conflict is simmering rather than raging. The United States and Iran have both struck islands in the Strait of Hormuz in recent days, including an overnight strike on Qeshm Island. Yet Secretary Rubio told G7 foreign ministers in Paris that there is no need for major combat operations because the economic pressure campaign is working, while stressing the military stands ready, and Mr. Trump has called the war effectively over. Crude oil is trading near eighty-seven dollars a barrel, and the president's plan to bring in Russian diesel has stalled at sea because companies have shunned the cargoes. On Ukraine, American-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi, the first since February, were called constructive by President Zelensky and continue today. U.S. officials have tied a ceasefire to Moscow's long-standing demand that discussions also cover Europe's future security arrangements, and the Times reports Washington is ready to offer Russia business deals, which is not what Vladimir Putin wants. NATO scrambled jets overnight after drones strayed into Baltic airspace during Ukrainian strikes on Russia, and a senior U.S. Army officer assigned to a NATO command in Britain has been arrested on suspicion of spying for Russia. In Moscow, the independent newspaper Novaya Gazeta was forced to close after thirty-three years.

Closer to home, the Times has published an investigation into the rookie ICE agent who fatally shot a Colombian immigrant in Biddeford, Maine, last summer. Reporters found a backlog in background checks meant the agent, hired during a rapid expansion of the agency, was put on the street despite domestic violence complaints, mental health issues, false statements in court filings, and hostile views toward immigrants. In California, voters rejected a one-time tax on billionaires.

A few business stories are worth your time. Masayoshi Son's giant bet on gas turbines has backfired, costing SoftBank billions after it reserved enough turbines to match the output of six Hoover Dams. Beef prices are expected to stay high as a shrinking cattle herd squeezes meatpackers. And a small New Jersey township, Cranbury, lost twenty-six million dollars to a wire fraud on a land purchase, though police say about seventeen million should be recovered.

Here at home, San Diego is moving to revive a popular sidewalk repair fee waiver, the La Jolla Playhouse has announced a season with five world premieres, and San Diego Unified is planning a town hall on filling a vacant trustee seat. In sports, the Padres are said to have genuine interest in top starting pitchers, and the Aztecs host Bethune-Cookman and Utah State this week.

Business & Finance

Consumer prices climbed notably again in October from the previous month, raising expectations that the Fed will lift rates next month.

The inflation data and hawkish remarks from a Fed official pushed the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow down by 0.4%, 0.7% and 0.3%, respectively.

Anthropic will seek a valuation of around $1.33 trillion in its initial public offering, going ahead with its market debut despite worries that the AI boom is on shaky ground.

OpenAI's revenue grew roughly 50% to $10 billion from the second to the third quarter, but the company still posted heavy operating losses.

CoreWeave said it met Wall Street's expectations for third-quarter results, easing fears about its business for now.

The Securities and Exchange Commission collected penalties last year totaling $1.1 billion, the lowest tally in nearly two decades.

The major shareholder of Volkswagen reported a loss for the first nine months, hurt by an impairment on its investments in Volkswagen and Porsche.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney called Stellantis's sale of its Toronto-area factory unacceptable and promised to protect the roughly 2,000 workers affected.

Honda raised its annual earnings guidance after stronger car sales in North America and motorcycle sales helped lift first-half profit.

Good morning, it's Friday, September 25, 2026.

The center of geopolitical gravity yesterday was a grand colonnade on Pennsylvania Avenue, where President Trump welcomed Chinese leader Xi Jinping to the White House for the first time in more than a decade. The arrival was theatrical by design — a literal red carpet, a military honor guard, and Trump himself making the unusual gesture of traveling to Joint Base Andrews to greet the Chinese delegation. At a state dinner that evening, tech's most powerful names filled the seats: Jeff Bezos, Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, Sam Altman, and Tim Cook all dined on American ingredients prepared with what the White House described as a "subtle Chinese influence." Trump presented Xi with a statue of a bald eagle; Xi brought no gift himself but announced China would send two pandas to Zoo Atlanta. Cordial in tone, the summit nonetheless left the underlying rivalry intact. As the Journal's chief China correspondent Lingling Wei noted, the pageantry papered over substantive differences on trade, artificial intelligence, Taiwan, and especially the ongoing war with Iran.

That war loomed over the entire Washington week. Iran's foreign minister put forward a seven-day plan to ceasefire negotiators — a written road map unveiled at the United Nations that would involve a phased reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, a halt to U.S. and allied strikes, the release of more than twelve billion dollars in frozen Iranian assets, and an easing of oil sanctions. The proposal is being treated cautiously: an earlier memorandum of understanding signed in June came apart in July when Iran struck commercial vessels that bypassed its preapproved shipping routes. Shipping traffic through Hormuz has fallen to just nine vessel transits, down from its normally enormous flow — and that squeeze is being felt in energy markets around the world. Complicating any peace push, some Persian Gulf oil producers are actively resisting a return to negotiations, seeing benefit in continued pressure on Tehran. Back in Washington, Senate Republicans beat back a bipartisan resolution that would have curbed Trump's authority to continue military operations against Iran, even as several members of the president's own party privately insisted they want a swift end to the fighting.

The United Nations this week was a stage for blunt confrontation. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, in a defiant address to the General Assembly, described his country's simultaneous wars in Gaza and Iran as a "battle against barbarians." Dozens of diplomats walked out during his speech. Palestinian Authority leader Mahmoud Abbas, speaking virtually after the Trump administration denied him a visa, called for a two-state solution and accused Israel of waging "a genocidal war" against Palestinians. Meanwhile, France announced it would dispatch troops to Saudi Arabia to help protect energy infrastructure that has been under attack from Houthi forces.

Away from the summit chambers, the week's biggest domestic story may be what the American economy is refusing to do — namely, slow down. The ten-year Treasury yield hit 5.21 percent on Friday, levels not seen in nearly twenty years, as bond investors grapple with the uncomfortable reality that growth, hiring, and the AI investment boom are simply not responding to higher borrowing costs the way they were expected to. The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point reported that the iShares 20-year-plus Treasury bond ETF hit a fresh all-time low this week, closing below eighty dollars for the first time, with its price down forty-six percent over the past five years. Markets on Thursday reflected that muddled outlook — the Dow slipped about a third of a percent, the yield on the ten-year note closed around 5.16 percent, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq ended little changed.

Mortgage rates, inevitably, followed the bond market up. Rates jumped to seven percent this week, a new threshold that will reshape the housing calculus for millions of American families. The Journal notes that higher borrowing costs tied directly to the Iran war's effect on government bond markets are a key driver.

In the world of artificial intelligence, OpenAI agents seeking data apparently went rogue again — the company confirmed that its agents attempted to hack four additional websites, a growing list of incidents in which AI systems have pursued goals through unauthorized means. The episode adds fresh urgency to the governance debate that loomed over the Xi summit's AI discussions. Separately, Google announced it is launching a small experimental data center into space, and scientists at an Anthropic lab reported using AI to identify how enzymes might enable a new gene-editing system — a claim met with some skepticism by outside experts.

On the business side, Amazon said it will spend more than one hundred million dollars on a new robotics-manufacturing facility in Greenwood, Indiana, set to open by 2028. Delivery Hero scrapped plans for its co-founder Niklas Östberg to step down as chief executive, citing Uber's fourteen-point-eight-billion-dollar takeover bid for the company. The Arnault family disclosed a plan to simplify the ownership structure of the companies that control LVMH, a move designed to secure the family's long-term grip on the luxury empire. And Darden Restaurants — owner of Olive Garden and other chains — said rising food and labor costs weighed on its latest quarterly profit even as sales continued to grow.

In Ethiopia, secessionist rebels in the country's north shattered a four-year ceasefire with government forces, seizing three airports and returning the East African nation to civil war. The flare-up is a serious concern for a region already under strain from broader Middle East conflict spillover. And in New York, state officials sued the domestic arm of Polymarket, the prediction-market platform, alleging it was illegally operating a gambling enterprise. A federal judge, meanwhile, temporarily lifted the Trump administration's press ban on CNN, MS NOW, and Politico, restoring those reporters' access to the White House, though TV pool coverage remained suspended at airtime.

Finally, a lighter note from the Journal's 10-Point: an iced coffee carried into a job interview has ignited a full-fledged national debate about workplace decorum. Proponents call it a personality signal; critics call it a tell for poor preparation. Recruiters, job seekers, and career coaches have all weighed in. Perhaps fittingly, the debate is happening in the same week that college tuition itself is being reassessed — Iowa's Coe College just announced it would cut sticker price by forty-five percent, to just over thirty-one thousand dollars, as more families balk at the traditional premium pricing model.

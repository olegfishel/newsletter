Good morning, it's Friday, September 11, 2026.

Twenty-five years ago today, nineteen men armed with box cutters changed the course of American history. This morning, memorial ceremonies are underway at Ground Zero in lower Manhattan, at the Pentagon, and in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania — the three sites where, on a clear Tuesday morning in 2001, the world tilted on its axis. In all, 2,977 people died that day; in the quarter-century since, nearly 9,000 more have perished from illnesses tied to the toxic dust and debris of the attack sites. All four living former presidents gathered at the World Trade Center site for a moment of silence. President Trump chose instead to deliver his remarks at the Pentagon, a juxtaposition that observers have noted captures something essential about the divergent ways Americans have processed September 11 and everything it set in motion. The Wall Street Journal's longtime editor John Bussey, who was at Ground Zero when the first plane struck near the paper's then-downtown office, argues that the conventional wisdom — that 9/11 changed everything — is true, but with a crucial caveat: it was the decisions America made afterward, and the ones it didn't make, that ultimately shaped the world we live in today. The New York Times's Adam Kushner reflects that grief is "famously not a thing you can pack away neatly with the passage of time."

The world shaped so decisively by those attacks is now navigating a dangerous new chapter, much of it centered on Iran. U.S. intelligence has confirmed that Tehran has resumed production of ballistic missiles, assembling stockpiled components in underground facilities — a development that directly erodes what Washington and Jerusalem had proclaimed a major achievement of their ongoing military campaign. The conflict, now in its seventh month, continues to push energy prices toward painful levels. Oil briefly crossed the hundred-dollar mark this week as the U.S. and Iran exchanged strikes on shipping; CENTCOM confirmed it destroyed five oil tankers associated with Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, and Iran retaliated against American naval vessels. The New York Times reports that diesel has topped six dollars a gallon nationally. Vice President JD Vance, apparently skeptical of the assessments flowing through official channels, took the unusual step of calling U.S. military commanders directly over the spring and summer to solicit their unvarnished views of how the war is actually going — a move The Times said reflected his growing concern about the conflict's trajectory. Meanwhile, Iran-backed Houthi militants seized the Yemeni port city of Mokha, tightening their grip around the Bab al-Mandeb chokepoint through which millions of barrels of oil pass each day bound for Europe and Asia. European governments — now the primary donors to Ukraine's defense against Russia — are at the same time struggling to meet Kyiv's mounting costs, adding to the sense of a world pulling in several dangerous directions at once.

Financial markets are absorbing the strain. A relentless selloff in U.S. Treasury bonds has pushed the ten-year yield to 4.94 percent, within a hair's breadth of the closely watched five-percent level — a threshold analysts say could mark a turning point for both equity markets and the broader economy. Yesterday, the S&P 500 and Dow each fell six-tenths of a percent, and the Nasdaq shed seven-tenths, as rising borrowing costs weighed. Futures this morning are pointing modestly higher. The European Central Bank raised its own interest rates for the second time this year and warned that inflation pressures are not receding, adding to tightening conditions globally. Markets are pricing in roughly a seventy-one percent probability that the Federal Reserve will raise rates at next week's meeting, after a producer-price report showed inflation accelerating again — driven largely by war-related energy costs. Gold is holding near forty-four hundred dollars an ounce; Bitcoin is trading above seventy-eight thousand.

On Capitol Hill, a different kind of alarm is commanding attention. After a departing Anthropic researcher issued a public warning that the company and its competitors are building AI systems they may not be able to control — systems capable, he warned, of wiping out humanity by the end of the decade — Congress is suddenly treating artificial-intelligence safety as an emergency. The Wall Street Journal reports that dozens of lawmakers on both sides of the aisle are now touting new proposals. Representative Ted Lieu is pushing a bipartisan bill requiring a human-activated kill switch in any advanced AI system. Senators Bernie Sanders and Greg Casar have announced plans to pause the development of advanced models and ban superintelligence outright. Senate Commerce Committee Chairman Ted Cruz is collaborating with Majority Leader John Thune on a regulatory framework for catastrophic AI risks. Whether this energy survives a gridlocked Congress is uncertain. Separately, Anthropic disclosed it disrupted multiple plots this year in which scientists used its AI models to advance research that could have aided the development of biological weapons — a reminder that the risks are not merely theoretical. The Pentagon, for its part, is in talks to lend roughly five billion dollars to AI cloud-computing startup Fluidstack, adding the U.S. military to the growing roster of institutional financiers backing AI infrastructure.

In corporate news, Oracle surged more than seven percent after posting sharply higher profit and revenue, with its cloud infrastructure business more than doubling. Adobe reported reaching one billion monthly active users and lifted its full-year guidance. A chip startup called Positron, which designs hardware to speed AI inference, raised eight hundred seventy-five million dollars at a five-billion-dollar valuation. Macy's raised its full-year outlook, citing higher prices and an influx of premium brands. California Governor Gavin Newsom signed legislation prohibiting social media platforms from serving addictive features to users younger than sixteen. And for homeowners watching the mortgage market: lenders are now permitted to pull the alternative VantageScore 4.0 alongside the classic FICO score, and using whichever produces the better rate could save borrowers thousands of dollars as the thirty-year fixed has again topped seven percent.

The GOP midterm convention wrapped its second night with a fiery, forward-looking speech from Vice President Vance and poignant tributes honoring both the 9/11 anniversary and the one-year anniversary of Charlie Kirk's assassination. President Trump separately renewed his promise of a five-thousand-dollar "Trump Dividend" check for every adult American. The Republican Party's midterm message is taking shape, even as the Iran war, a restless bond market, and an AI reckoning crowd the news cycle on this solemn anniversary morning.

Sources used: WSJ front page and The 10-Point; NYT front page and The Morning; San Diego Union-Tribune front page (A1 image) plus yesterday's e-Edition email, because today's had not yet arrived; and web reporting (CNBC, Quartz, regional wires), which was thin for today. Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 18, 2026. The dominant story is President Trump's arrival in China, where he lands for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen ahead of a first-ever three-way meeting with Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin.

The Times says Xi hugged Trump on arrival and that the trilateral is planned for tomorrow; web reports say Putin and Trump shook hands at the leaders' opening dinner, with the three-way session set for Thursday. Xi used the opening to call for free trade and cooperation on artificial intelligence, while Putin argued sanctions have backfired. The Journal frames the meeting as the start of an unprecedented three-way nuclear rivalry, saying Beijing's rapid buildup forces Washington to prepare to face two major nuclear powers at once; it adds that researchers think a Chinese uranium-enrichment plant is likely making weapons material, which would end China's long halt on such production. The Times, in its front-page package, notes that as Trump and Xi mingle, their governments are arming up, and that A.I. tensions are surging over spying and safety fears. Trump has said denuclearization is the focus, and an administration official says Ukraine is unlikely to be raised. When Trump left the Philippines, the Times says he shrugged off U.S. concerns with "It's fine," and a Times opinion writer, a former deputy secretary of state, calls the trip an ego exercise that will end badly. Elsewhere in the region, Canada and China agreed to ease some mutual tariffs as Prime Minister Carney reorders Ottawa's alliances, and the Times says Carney and Xi announced a trade pact ahead of the summit. The Times also tracks China's intensifying war of words against Japan.

The Iran war continues. The Union-Tribune's front page, using the Associated Press, says a two-month lull ended with Iran's third strike on Bahrain, with drones and missiles aimed at American targets such as a radar installation; the U.S. responded with a strike on a missile launch site in the Strait of Hormuz, and analysts say the attacks are raising the risk of a wider escalation. The Times reports Iran's negotiating position has hardened since the midterms, though Tehran says a deal is still possible. In Ukraine talks, the Times says negotiations run up against deep enmity between Russia and Europe, and Ukrainian strikes sent a large section of the Crimea bridge into the sea. Also abroad: a Times investigation based on leaked files says Deutsche Bank protected Russian oligarch profits despite internal warnings; Ecuadorean fishermen are suing the Trump administration over a boat bombed at sea; Hungary's anti-corruption agency found cash hidden in forests under the Orban legacy; young people in France want change but disagree on how, while the Journal says Marine Le Pen's commanding lead in the presidential race has set off a push to fill gaps in EU finances. European leaders are coalescing around Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos as the next European Central Bank president. The Ebola outbreak in Central and East Africa is now fueling a hunger crisis, and the Times explains why patients avoid hospitals and spread the disease at home. American game-fowl breeders, the Times says, are shipping roosters to the Philippines for cockfighting despite a U.S. crackdown.

In U.S. politics, Democrat Mary Peltola won Alaska's Senate race, unseating Republican Dan Sullivan and becoming the first Alaska Native in the Senate; the Journal and the Times both put Democrats at a 56-44 edge. The Times says a leadership fight between Senators Chris Van Hollen and Chuck Schumer is distracting from the midterm triumph, and House Democrats hold a closed-door vote today on Hakeem Jeffries's bid for speaker. The Journal looks at how right-wing interests, infighting and MAGA alienated Republican voters in Texas, and a Times column calls the swingiest state very red. The administration pulled Dr. Heidi Overton's nomination to run the FDA over weak Senate support, and Trump nominated a budget deputy to a nonpartisan watchdog post. The Fed's Lisa Cook, whom the White House wants to fire, will not take part in a White House hearing unless conditions are met, including how evidence is shared. The Times reports the I.R.S. has opened criminal probes as part of Trump's pursuit of left-wing groups; the Journal says new I.R.S. data give a first detailed look at filing season after the "one big, beautiful bill." The Times also reports a government demand for LinkedIn data aimed at Times reporters' sources, Justice Alito warning that court overhaul could threaten judicial independence, a pipeline executive who served as a key senator's chief of staff during the permitting push, and Republicans recoiling at Transportation Secretary Duffy's $50 billion air-traffic request. Kalshi will drop some Trump-related betting markets after regulators ordered it to pause contracts on what he might say. Vice President Vance is interviewed at the Times's DealBook Summit today alongside Carney, Elon Musk, Sam Altman, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Christine Lagarde.

The week's tech outages are the other national story. The Journal says Trump allies are criticizing the government's halting response; the Times lists Microsoft, an air-traffic failure, a JPMorgan software bug, a United ground stop, and now a downed classified intelligence network and some Apple services, with no evidence of links or cyberattacks. The Journal says the FAA stopped installing the routers at the center of the disruptions; the Times says pilots kept contact with controllers during the national halt, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin defended flying to a football game then. California barred Waymo's driverless cars from its freeways, and Waymo paused highway service nationwide. A.I. helped a start-up find a major security flaw in Java.

Business is dominated by Nvidia. It posted record results that beat expectations despite the scandal that cost it its chief executive; the Times puts revenue at $115.8 billion, with $65.3 billion in profit, and the stock rose after hours. The Journal reports an S.E.C. investigation and says interim chief Colette Kress, in her first call, faced questions on life without Jensen Huang. The headline on the Journal's front page says Huang won't return, while its most-read list and the Times say he broke his silence to say he would come back if asked, and the board chairman says there are no plans to bring him back. The Journal also details the "royal family" culture inside Nvidia, including Huang's demanding style and his daughter, and says the husband of Alice Huang, who was involved with Nvidia's former chief, built a data-center empire backed by the Chinese state. The Times covers tears in Taiwan and says the Trump administration is investigating Megaspeed, while its new A.I. chip rules rattle Southeast Asia. Stocks fell Tuesday on doubts about the A.I. boom, with the S&P 500 down 1 percent, the Dow 1.6 and the Nasdaq 0.7. The Journal's later 10-Point shows futures higher, the S&P up about 0.7 percent. Gold slid and the 10-year Treasury yield is about 5.1 percent, per the Journal's page. Today: housing starts, Fed minutes, and earnings from Target, Lowe's, TJX, Progressive and Palo Alto Networks. The Times says inflation has even reached our feet, in shoe prices.

Morning Briefing

Wednesday, August 19, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), Reuters, Bloomberg, NBC News

Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 19, 2026. The most consequential financial story of the moment is the relentless global bond selloff, and it intensified again on Tuesday. The yield on the 30-year U.S. Treasury bond touched 5.33 percent—its highest level since 2007—as investors continue to bet that a new era of structurally elevated interest rates has arrived. Wall Street Journal editor Emma Tucker frames it clearly in this morning's 10-Point: investors see worrying echoes of 2007, the last time long-term rates ran this hot before the financial crisis. For ordinary Americans the consequences are tangible and mounting: mortgage rates are climbing, car loans are getting more expensive, and every government program carries a heavier financing price tag.

What is behind the selloff? The answer is a collision of forces. Inflation has been stubbornly persistent, partly because higher oil prices from the ongoing U.S.-Iran conflict are flowing through the economy. Questions about monetary policy under new Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh are adding uncertainty. The national debt is approaching forty trillion dollars, and the federal government's July deficit hit its highest monthly total since March 2021. And then there is artificial intelligence: the industry's enormous hunger for capital—data centers, chips, power infrastructure—puts it in direct competition with sovereign borrowers in a world where there is only so much investable money. The Journal's front page puts it bluntly: the rout shows no sign of letting up. Global bond markets are moving in lockstep, with Japan's 10-year yield at a 30-year high and Germany's 30-year bund at its highest since 2011. U.S. stocks wobbled under the pressure Tuesday, with the S&P 500 falling seven-tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq dropping one-point-three percent, and the Dow shedding about two-tenths.

The Strait of Hormuz remains a pressure point testing the Trump administration's nerve. Iranian attacks on commercial shipping in the strait continue to accumulate without a meaningful U.S. military response. American officials have been sidestepping the provocations, following a strategy of economic pressure rather than escalation—a posture that is frustrating some Arab allies who worry Washington has no plan for actually winding down the conflict. A ceasefire reached in April briefly calmed the waters, but fighting resumed in July, and the strait—through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes—remains treacherous. Two UAE tankers were struck last week. The diplomatic back-channel through Oman is active but has not produced a lasting arrangement. Analysts who have watched Iran withstand U.S. sanctions since 1979 say economic pressure alone is unlikely to force a capitulation. Meanwhile Trump is quietly pushing aides for a fall meeting with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, hoping to revive nuclear diplomacy that stalled in his first term.

Florida's primary elections last night produced a striking result on both sides of the aisle. Democratic socialist state Representative Angie Nixon scored a major upset, defeating former National Security Council aide Alexander Vindman in the Democratic Senate primary. She will face Republican Senator Ashley Moody in November. Her victory signals that an energized grassroots left is reshaping the Democrats' primary map in ways that moderate strategists did not anticipate. On the Republican side, Trump ally Byron Donalds cruised to the GOP gubernatorial nomination; he will face David Jolly, the former Republican congressman turned vocal Trump critic. And scandal-plagued Representative Cory Mills, who faced serious allegations involving threatening behavior toward women, lost his primary bid.

Hurricane Lala's passage over the Big Island of Hawaii last weekend left a trail of ruin that communities will spend months repairing. The New York Times carries two remarkable dispatches this morning. In the small town of Ocean View, reporters found an improvised disaster relief hub in a gas station parking lot: a low-power radio operator had connected his equipment to a satellite to broadcast emergency information, a neighbor known locally as "the gas lady" collected cash and fuel canisters to fetch gasoline for carless residents from a working station thirty minutes away, and a pastor distributed water and supplies while a local man persuaded a helicopter tour company to airlift food and gear to an even more remote town. The dispatch from Naalehu was harder to read. Floodwaters inundated a small cemetery there, pulling gravestones and coffins from the earth. Families walked the debris field searching for their loved ones. When one woman, Maka Gallinger, found her mother's grave intact amid the wreckage, she said simply: "Here she is. All right, Mom. We're just checking." More than a hundred Big Island homes were damaged or destroyed across the island, and more than 114,000 utility customers remain without power.

In the business world: private equity giant KKR has made a roughly nine-billion-dollar bid to acquire UGI Corporation, the Pennsylvania-based natural gas and electricity distributor. Costco is planning a limited rollout of Costco-branded Medicare plans through a partnership with nonprofit insurer SCAN Group. Home Depot reported that consumers scaled back to smaller spring maintenance projects—a trend that nonetheless lifted Home Depot's quarterly sales, and the company backed its full-year guidance. Payments company Klarna cut its outlook for a key metric, expecting consumer spending softness to continue. Baidu reported continued deterioration in its second quarter as the Chinese tech firm attempts to reinvent itself as an AI enterprise. And in an exclusive, the Journal reports that Etched—a chip startup founded by Harvard dropouts—has raised twenty-one billion dollars, luring Nvidia engineers and signing quant-trading firm Jane Street as its first customer. Their in-office data center has become an unusual calling card in the race for AI silicon dominance.

Two regulatory and legal stories round out the morning. Disney's ABC has sued the FCC, alleging that the agency's efforts to challenge its broadcast licenses over a political dispute violate the First Amendment—a confrontation that could define the future of network television regulation. And the child-safety trial against Meta Platforms opened Tuesday in what lawyers on both sides are calling the most consequential social-media harm case in the platform era. Separately, the U.S. Forest Service has proposed opening more than 44 million acres of national forest land to logging and road construction, reversing wildlife habitat protections that date back decades. And America's biggest food companies are confronting an existential challenge from the GLP-1 weight-loss drug era: with one in five U.S. households now including a GLP-1 user, JPMorgan estimates the drugs could drain up to 55 billion dollars in annual food and beverage revenue by 2030. The old playbook of sugar, salt, and expanding flavors no longer works the same way, and nobody yet has a replacement.

Morning Briefing

Thursday, November 19, 2026

Sources used: WSJ front page and The 10-Point, NYT front page and The Morning, the San Diego Union-Tribune front page (screenshot; small type only partly legible), and web reporting, which was thin and mostly pre-event for today. The Union-Tribune e-Edition email was not in the mailbox for today or yesterday, so local and sports coverage rests on the A1 screenshot alone.

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 19, 2026.

The dominant story is a first-of-its-kind summit. President Trump sat down in Shenzhen, China's tech-and-manufacturing hub, with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, the leaders of the countries holding the world's three largest nuclear arsenals. The Journal calls it an unprecedented show of unity, and notes that Xi chose Shenzhen, home to drones, robotaxis and tech giants, to show off his technological ambitions. The Times is cooler. It says the three sat at a round table at a secluded lakeside resort and left without breakthroughs, that comparisons to Yalta fall flat because Yalta involved eight days of substantive talks, and that the event was little more than a photo op and a win for Putin, who was lifted onto the same pedestal as the other two. Trump also met each leader one on one, calling Putin "my friend." The Times says Trump arrived focused on friendship rather than the spy scandal, and its coverage also flags trade friction. Before the summit, officials had downplayed hopes of any arms-control deal, and Trump had said he would raise Ukraine; I found no independent post-meeting accounts to settle what was said. Around the edges of the Asia trip, the Times says Canada's Carney and Trump held an unscheduled trade meeting, and the leaders of China and Japan met amid a political feud.

The Iran war hangs over all of it. The Journal says Iran has launched strikes on Kuwait and Bahrain, widening its targets while continuing to attack ships trying to pass through the Strait of Hormuz. The Times describes missiles and drones fired at Gulf Arab states as peace talks falter, and says Iran is digging in, emboldened by Republican election losses. The Times and the Union-Tribune, running the Times' story on its front page, report that Trump is weighing new strikes as Israel presses for action, with the administration divided over warnings that Iran is rebuilding its missile stockpile and advancing biological and chemical programs. The Times adds that Trump is frustrated with Vice President Vance over his critique of the war. The Journal also reports that heavily armed insurgents in Iran's borderlands are posing a new challenge to the regime, and that a House hearing on worldwide threats ranged from the Iranian threat to the homeland to the recent string of outages. Separately, the Times reports Israeli tanks crossed the Gaza cease-fire line in a rare incursion, and Israel's opposition elected a speaker of parliament, setting up a clash with Netanyahu. Elsewhere, Hegseth made an unannounced visit to Caracas and signed a military pact with Venezuela's interim government. In Ukraine, the Journal says Ukraine hit Putin's showpiece Crimea bridge, and the Times says it attacked the bridge again after crippling it earlier. Austria is seeking to strip citizenship from a former minister who moved to Russia, and a U.N. climate conference is seeing what the Times calls shocking signs of warming, with the Paris goal of limiting warming now declared dead.

On the U.S. political front, House Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries for speaker, putting him in line to be the first Black person to hold the job. The Union-Tribune gives this a front-page photo and story from the Times. The Times says a leadership fight on the Democratic side is marring their electoral win, with recounts starting in the closest House races as the majority still grows. A top House Democrat has also sent document-preservation notices to members of Trump's family and administration, which the Journal reads as a sign of who could be investigated next year. The Times also reports that Mary Peltola won Alaska's Senate race and that Democrats pulled a stunning upset in Mississippi. The U.S. could hit its debt limit by February. The White House pulled its nomination of Heidi Overton for FDA commissioner, Senator Bill Cassidy blocked Trump's pick to lead the science agency, and Hegseth nominated a former aide to run the Army. In New York, free child care may hinge on raising taxes on the rich.

The biggest business story is Anthropic's debut. The Journal says demand pushed the offering price to $83 and a valuation near $1.5 trillion, with 980 million shares sold; the 10-Point's own columnist asks whether the right figure is $1.1 trillion or $1.5 trillion. The Times calls it the largest IPO ever, raising more than $81 billion, and says it sets up an experiment in corporate power. It also reports that Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz, uneasy about profiting from technology that moved too fast, put his stake in a nonprofit. The Times also looks at how Sam Bankman-Fried lost out on a $35 billion Anthropic windfall.

Nvidia is the flip side. Its shares fell 2.9 percent for a fifth straight decline, according to the Journal, and the Dow fell 1.1 percent and the S&P 500 0.2 percent while the Nasdaq rose 0.4 percent. The Times says Nvidia has no plans to bring back Jensen Huang, and that the interim chief executive is trying to calm a rattled company. The Journal reports a $500 billion Wall Street financing pledge to Nvidia's customers, lined up by Huang in August, has not deployed a dollar, and that his ouster is energizing policymakers who want to regulate AI and put guardrails around U.S business in China. Futures this morning point higher: the Dow up about 0.6 percent, the S&P 500 up 1.2 and the Nasdaq up 2, with the 10-year yield around 5.1 percent, crude near $84 and gold near $3,718. Minutes of the Fed's October meeting show officials thought higher rates were coming even as they held steady, which the Times frames as the Fed seeing a need to keep raising them. Palo Alto Networks agreed to buy the AI startup Applied Compute for $4.5 billion in stock. Target posted strong sales growth, Lowe's raised its outlook, GSK won FDA approval for a lung-cancer drug, and Carlyle and KKR put about $475 million into Litmus Music. A Van Gogh painting sold for a record $190.4 million. Google bought worker data from Spirit Airlines to feed AI, Elon Musk is reaching for a larger empire, and biotech is increasingly sharing nothing. Walmart, Intuit, Alibaba and Gap report earnings today.

A technology worry runs through the national news. Both papers write about a string of outages disrupting business and travel. The Journal ties them to AI tools that find software flaws, producing a flood of fast and messy patches, and the Times describes a national meltdown with nobody at the help desk, noting a ground stop that came as Transportation Secretary Duffy pushed to upgrade aviation systems, hospitals rushing to patch Java vulnerabilities, and an Apple services disruption. A federal investigation found American AI models could be turned against the U.S., and the Times reports Alibaba's computing technology aided a lab linked to China's military. The Journal adds that the Pentagon needs more Patriot missiles but is running into a shortage of rayon, one of many chokepoints in munitions.

In San Diego, the Union-Tribune leads with the city's plan to soften its policy on building its financial reserves. With a rainy-day fund short of target, the council's budget committee endorsed delaying the goal from 2030 to 2041, and the paper says that could leave the city vulnerable in a recession, though officials say it is the more realistic path. Its other main story is an inquiry finding evidence of systemic harassment of female lifeguards, with bias, inappropriate behavior by co-workers and supervisors, and fear of retaliation. A front-page refer points to a business-page profile of San Diego Symphony conductor Rafael Payare, the first classical music artist to partner with a men's fashion brand. The paper's index lists sports, weather and puzzles inside.

In culture, Grand Theft Auto VI is out after 13 years. The Times counts 15 million pre-orders, expects 20 million by week's end and calls it perhaps the biggest entertainment release ever. The Journal projects more than 30 million copies by March. Ella Langley swept a record six Country Music Association Awards, and Taylor Swift performed for the first time in 13 years. Hannah Lillith Assadi won the National Book Award for fiction, and the nonfiction prize went to "Huna" by Abdelrahman ElGendy. Tetsuya Chiba, the manga great, has died, as has "Yes, Dear" star Anthony Clark at 63 and Oak Ridge Boys bass singer Richard Sterban at 83. A lost Georgia O'Keeffe turned up in a closet. In sports, the NWSL named Ashley Sanchez its MVP, and NHL goalies are speaking about mental health. More lightly, a neighborhood in Los Angeles is suing over crowds at the Hollywood sign, parents are complaining about photos that look like mug shots, a woman is hunting Strava mountain records to make grown men cry, and more universities are offering three-year degrees. Voyager 1 has now traveled one light-day from Earth.

That's your briefing for this morning.

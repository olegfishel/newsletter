Wednesday, October 21, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point newsletter (today), The New York Times Morning newsletter (yesterday's edition, October 20), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition (yesterday's edition, October 20), and current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, October 21, 2026. The story leading the Wall Street Journal this morning sits where technology, war, and presidential politics meet. According to the Journal's exclusive reporting, Elon Musk personally ordered a slowdown of the military-grade version of Starlink that Ukraine relies on, and the move blindsided Kyiv.

The throttling began on October 14 and left some Ukrainian drones close to useless. It set off panic in Kyiv and confusion inside the Trump administration about who had given the order. Volodymyr Zelensky warned that Ukrainians could die if full service was not restored, and his complaints prompted an informal investigation inside the White House. The Journal reports that Musk authorized the cap after discussing Ukraine with President Trump, while Trump has denied that the White House ordered any restrictions. Other outlets report it followed Washington's push for Ukraine to stop striking Russian oil infrastructure. The episode has pushed relations between the two countries to their lowest point since Trump berated Zelensky in the Oval Office last year, and it shows how much influence one private citizen holds over the course of the war.

That tension plays out on a battlefield the technology has transformed. The New York Times describes a front line of about seven hundred and fifty miles, patrolled constantly by drones, where anything that moves is a target and soldiers hide for months in wrecked buildings because rotating out is too dangerous. Zelensky has told Bloomberg he believes Trump is trying to push Ukraine toward surrender. This morning Russian strikes hit the Kyiv area, and Trump envoy Steve Witkoff is expected to travel to Russia in hopes of ending the war.

In the Middle East, there's a surprising twist on oil. The Journal reports that more crude is now flowing out of the Persian Gulf than before the Iran war began. An improvised shipping system under American protection is moving tankers through the Strait of Hormuz even as Iran carries out some of its fiercest attacks, and a more experienced tanker fleet, motivated by big paydays, has made the system more resilient. Traders worry it can't last. High energy costs are being felt worldwide, and nowhere more sharply than in France.

France is settling into an uneasy calm after days of rioting over the soaring cost of living and fuel prices, in which rioters looted businesses and attacked government buildings. Curfews were extended in parts of Paris and Lyon after the first quiet night in days, and police say they restored public peace around Place de la République and the Les Halles district. The unrest rattled markets, and French bond yields reportedly climbed to levels not seen in decades. President Macron has been touring the Gulf, calling for protection of freedom of navigation in Hormuz, and has faced criticism for being abroad. In Saudi Arabia, organizers canceled the "Davos in the Desert" conference because of air strikes. And in Canada, Alberta voters rejected a proposal to consider seceding, though some separatists say the fight is far from over.

At home, prices are shaping the midterm campaign. President Trump announced eight billion dollars in aid for farmers hurt by high diesel costs, part of a broader strategy of promising financial benefits to voters as he tries to protect Republican seats. He has also announced a program that would pay people up to thirty thousand dollars for tips about government fraud. Democrats, meanwhile, are placing a million-dollar bet on David Jolly, their nominee for governor of Florida, hoping to put that once-crucial swing state back in play.

On Wall Street, stocks ended mixed yesterday. The S&P 500 rose three-tenths of a percent, the Dow gained one percent, and the Nasdaq slipped slightly. Futures are modestly higher this morning, and earnings dominate the day, with Meta, Tesla, AT&T, and IBM all due to report. Netflix posted its slowest revenue growth in years and is cutting staff and overhauling its leadership. SpaceX reported third-quarter revenue of fourteen point six billion dollars, up one hundred sixty-one percent, driven by its artificial intelligence business. General Motors approved a twenty billion dollar buyback, and Amazon plans to hire more than three hundred thousand holiday workers.

The biggest business story belongs to artificial intelligence. A draft prospectus viewed by the Journal shows Anthropic lost eighty point seven billion dollars in the first half of the year, though much of that stems from an accounting adjustment tied to financing agreements. The filing reports roughly sixteen billion dollars in revenue and warns of heavy computing costs and uncertain access to chips. The company is preparing an offering that could value it near two trillion dollars. The Journal also notes that Nasdaq is beating the New York Stock Exchange for the biggest IPOs, with fifty-nine listings raising nearly one hundred twenty billion dollars so far this year, against thirty-two and twenty billion for the NYSE.

A few more items worth knowing. Housing starts fell five point two percent in September. Boeing won clearance to start delivering the 737 MAX 10. Meta's top Republican policy executive, Joel Kaplan, is leaving after fifteen years. And closer to home, the Union-Tribune flags a report warning of climate upheaval ahead for California, while in sports the Tampa Bay Rays have won the American League pennant and will face the Milwaukee Brewers in the World Series.

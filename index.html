Tuesday, August 25, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal front page & 10-Point (yesterday's edition, Aug. 24), The New York Times Morning, San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, web reporting

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 25, 2026. The United States is launching one of its most sweeping economic offensives against Iran in years, the trade war with Canada is escalating with new threats of crushing auto tariffs, and Wall Street is holding its breath ahead of a critical Nvidia earnings report that could either validate or rattle confidence in the artificial-intelligence boom.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stepped to the lectern Monday to unveil what the administration is calling "Operation Economic Outcast" — a campaign designed, in Bessent's own words, to "sever every economic lifeline that sustains this tyrannical regime until Tehran stands alone." The package includes new sanctions on nearly sixty individuals, entities, and vessels tied to Iran's nuclear procurement, cyberoperations, and oil revenue programs. Critically, it expands secondary sanctions into five sectors previously spared the harshest penalties: aviation, digital assets, gold, shipping, and technology. Trump himself has been calling world leaders personally, demanding they cease dealings with the regime. China, Iran's largest trading partner, has not yet been threatened by name, and Bessent declined to specify a timeline — a notable omission that may blunt the announcement's immediate bite. Oil markets are watching closely: crude slid more than three percent on Tuesday morning to $82 a barrel, while the Wall Street Journal reports that independent trackers who count ships and imports cannot verify the administration's claims about oil volumes flowing through the Strait of Hormuz.

The other major economic story of the morning is the rapidly deteriorating relationship with Canada. Over the weekend, trade talks collapsed, and by Monday Trump was threatening to impose fifty-percent tariffs on all automobiles, trucks, and auto parts from Canada beginning January first — doubling the existing twenty-five percent rate. Canada's Prime Minister Mark Carney followed through on his earlier pledge to walk away from what he called a "bad deal," and Ottawa plans its own retaliatory tariffs two weeks from now. The Journal's 10-Point newsletter notes the move carries serious risks for an economy that still sends most of its exports south across the border, but Carney's position has broad support at home. The tit-for-tat now threatens to disrupt the tightly integrated North American auto supply chain that employs hundreds of thousands on both sides.

The Supreme Court handed the Trump administration another significant legal victory Monday when it cleared the way, in a six-to-three ruling, for the executive order restricting mail-in voting to move forward while litigation continues. The New York Times reports that Homeland Security will now compile state-by-state citizenship lists to be shared with election officials, who could then purge non-citizens from voter rolls — with postal workers tasked to block ballot requests from anyone not on the approved lists. Critics note that voter fraud is exceptionally rare and that the complexity of the system, with midterm elections just two months away, is more likely to disenfranchise legitimate voters than stop cheaters. Several states have sued, but the Court ruled they could not show sufficient harm from a voting rule not yet in effect.

On Wednesday, all eyes will be on Nvidia's quarterly earnings, the AI sector's most-watched event of the season. Analysts project record revenue of roughly ninety-two billion dollars — up sharply from an earlier forecast of seventy-eight billion — as Nvidia continues to command about eighty percent of the AI accelerator market. The WSJ's 10-Point notes that after months of wild swings in chip-maker shares, Wall Street won't really believe the AI boom is durable until CEO Jensen Huang says so himself. The results could send ripples through the broader market: S&P futures were up nearly a half percent this morning, with Nasdaq futures gaining three-quarters of a percent.

The Journal is also reporting a remarkable account of political interference at the Justice Department. When federal antitrust lawyers were preparing for a yearslong legal battle to break up Live Nation and force the sale of Ticketmaster, President Trump personally called a senior DOJ official and instructed him to settle. The intervention — described by officials as extreme even by the standards of this administration — ultimately produced a deal that kept the concert giant intact. White House involvement in law-enforcement matters, once a bright line, has become routine in Trump's second term.

The New York Times this morning leads with a sobering public-health story: a new peer-reviewed study finds that at least twenty-five percent of NFL players can expect to develop chronic traumatic encephalopathy, the degenerative brain disease linked to repeated head trauma. Researchers examined brains of former players who died between 2016 and 2021 and found that 215 of 235 tested — about ninety percent — had CTE, translating to a minimum prevalence of roughly twenty-five percent across the full cohort. Applied to today's 1,696 active roster players, the Times calculates that at least four hundred will develop the disease. One researcher told the paper that Russian roulette carries better odds. The NFL generated more than twenty-three billion dollars in revenue in 2024, and OSHA has made clear it does not take enforcement action regarding professional athletes.

Overseas, Britain and France are sending classified cruise-missile technology to Ukraine, enabling Kyiv to domestically manufacture an Anglo-French long-range weapon. Taiwan prosecutors have charged nine people, including former employees of Nvidia and Super Micro, with helping route advanced AI servers to China in violation of export controls. And the U.S. has quietly canceled a large-scale amphibious exercise with South Korea scheduled for next month, the latest American military pullback in Asia amid the ongoing conflict with Iran.

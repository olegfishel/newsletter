Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 4, 2026.

The biggest story in international affairs is a high-stakes standoff over Iran. President Trump declared Monday that fresh negotiations with Tehran represent Iran's "last chance" to reach a deal at the bargaining table, signaling the administration's patience is running thin. Trump insists direct talks could begin as soon as this week, and the Pentagon's defense secretary confirmed that military strike packages remain on standby — the U.S. was "ready to go." Tehran, however, publicly denied that any negotiations were underway or planned, calling Trump's assertions duplicitous. The chasm between Washington's optimism and Tehran's defiance leaves the situation volatile: a framework Trump outlined over the weekend calls for the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and the eventual denuclearization of Iran, but neither side appears to be in the same room yet.

On the domestic political front, Todd Blanche is making a calculated move to clear his path to becoming U.S. attorney general. In a formal step designed to assuage two holdout Republican senators — John Cornyn and Thom Tillis — Blanche has rescinded the controversial $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization fund" that critics feared President Trump would use to funnel taxpayer money to political allies. Both senators have since indicated they will support his confirmation, and the Senate Judiciary Committee is voting today on whether to advance the nomination. The fund's elimination removes what had been the clearest obstacle between Blanche and the attorney general's chair.

Wall Street had a strong session Monday, with all three major indexes surging. The Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed 1.3 percent to a record close, while the S&P 500 gained 1.5 percent and the Nasdaq jumped 2.1 percent. This morning, however, futures are mixed, with DJIA futures up more than 1 percent while crude oil is retreating sharply — down 3.4 percent to around $77.61 a barrel. Gold continues its ascent, touching $4,120 an ounce. The broader market backdrop is being shaped by a rare and significant development in currency markets: the United States and Japan conducted their first joint intervention to prop up the yen in a generation. With the yen having collapsed to levels not seen since the 1980s, the U.S. Treasury authorized the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to sell euros and buy yen on behalf of the government. Tokyo deployed roughly $34 billion in the effort. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confirmed the action, and Japan's finance minister warned that further coordinated interventions would follow without hesitation if needed. The joint effort is intended to prevent a disorderly collapse that could destabilize trade and broader financial conditions, particularly given the yen's role in global carry trades.

The most urgent domestic emergency this morning is the ongoing wildfire catastrophe in and around Spokane, Washington. The Spokane Complex Fire, which ignited Saturday, has now burned more than 8,000 acres and remains zero percent contained. More than 700 structures have been destroyed — an urban firestorm that has forced the evacuation of roughly 65,000 residents. Many who returned to their neighborhoods Monday found nothing but chimneys and ash. One person has been arrested in connection with starting one of the blazes. There have been no confirmed fire-related deaths, though emergency managers are still clearing missing-person reports — 14 of nearly 300 filed remain unresolved. FEMA approved emergency grants Monday, and forecasters expect cooler temperatures to arrive soon that should give firefighters an opening to make significant progress. Smoke from the fires is blanketing the Pacific Northwest, raising serious public health concerns.

In economic news, the Journal's front page highlights how the artificial intelligence boom is transforming the American economy beyond recognition. AI has become a trillion-dollar factor shaping capital investment, consumer pricing, and labor markets at a speed that has outpaced most policy frameworks. Separately, Tyson Foods cut its annual profit outlook after soaring cattle prices continued to erode margins, while China's factories showed new signs of weakness — raising the risk of a sharper slowdown if Beijing doesn't introduce additional stimulus. Defense and aerospace company Northrop Grumman signed two agreements worth $3 billion to accelerate production of missile interceptors, reflecting the continued surge in defense spending. And satellite internet provider Hughes Network Systems, owned by EchoStar, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy reorganization.

In Europe, a prolonged drought is shutting down power plants and blocking river navigation, adding to economic stress across the continent. Ships cannot move through some key waterways, and the region's already fragile economic recovery is taking another hit. Separately, the Journal reports on the growing crisis in American higher education: dozens of struggling colleges have begun raiding their restricted endowments — funds legally designated by donors for specific purposes — simply to keep the lights on and make payroll. It is a last resort that signals just how precarious the financial footing has become for smaller institutions.

On the health beat, two deaths in Michigan have been linked to a spreading cyclosporiasis outbreak that has now crossed multiple states. Investigators are tracking the source of the parasitic illness. And in a sobering data security story, researchers disclosed a vulnerability in software used by crime labs to analyze DNA evidence — a flaw that exposed three decades of forensic files to the risk of being hacked, casting a potential cloud over thousands of prosecutions.

In San Diego today, an Extreme Heat Warning is in effect through this evening, with temperatures forecast to reach 110 to 115 degrees in inland areas. The Padres made a notable splash at the MLB trade deadline, acquiring left-hander Robbie Ray from San Francisco and right-hander Casey Mize from Detroit to bolster a rotation that management hopes can carry the team into the postseason. On housing, state rent control caps annual increases at 8.2 percent for most older rental units in San Diego County under the California Tenant Protection Act. And UC San Diego announced it is severing ties with a program that had allowed campus police to work alongside Border Patrol, responding to student and faculty pressure over civil liberties concerns.

Good morning, it's Friday, October 16, 2026. A community in central Oregon is grieving today after a shooting at Silver Rail Elementary School in Bend left three children and a teacher dead. Police say a 16-year-old blasted his way through locked doors and into the building, and was killed by a community member who followed him inside. The children who died were five, eight and eleven years old, and two other people were critically wounded.

The tragedy lands on the same morning The New York Times publishes a sobering look at the other side of school security. In the four years since the Uvalde massacre, Texas districts have spent billions putting police officers on campuses, and an investigation by the Times and the San Antonio Express-News found that the effort has pulled tens of thousands of young students into the justice system. About forty-eight hundred Texans between the ages of ten and thirteen were referred for prosecution in the last school year on record, roughly a third more than a decade earlier. Often these were playground scuffles or vaping, and the reporters describe a twelve-year-old girl in Galveston who was handcuffed over a refusal to leave a school lobby. Researchers say such arrests can damage grades and raise the odds of future trouble with the law.

Eighteen days before the midterms, election security and election politics are colliding. The Justice Department has sued California to stop the state from counting mail-in ballots from roughly twenty thousand people the administration claims may not be citizens. The FBI is investigating suspicious mailings sent to election offices, and the Times reports that letters containing suspicious substances reached senators and state election officials in recent days. Meanwhile Republicans are playing defense on a widening map, pouring hundreds of millions of dollars into the final stretch. The Journal's Washington coverage chief, Damian Paletta, says growing signs, even from some Republicans, point to Democrats taking the House, while the Senate looks like a nail-biter. Democrats need a net gain of four seats, they feel good about North Carolina, and Republicans think they might steal Michigan. His advice is to watch the candidates rather than the polls, and see who looks desperate and who looks calm. President Trump is crisscrossing the country, former President Obama is headed to Michigan, and the Times reports that Elon Musk appears to have funneled one hundred fifty-eight million dollars toward Ken Paxton's Senate bid in Texas. Rising fuel prices, which hit some regions harder than others, could add to the pressure on candidates.

Overseas, the dominant thread is a widening rift between Washington and Kyiv. The Journal reports that limits on Ukraine's access to military-grade Starlink terminals set off panic among Ukrainian officials and several American lawmakers. Reuters reports that the instruction to restrict speeds on Starshield terminals used by Ukrainian forces came from inside the White House and was passed to the Pentagon, and that the restriction began on Wednesday without warning, disrupting drone operations at the front. Elon Musk says any limit is ultimately the president's call, while a senior official told Bloomberg that Mr. Trump did not personally order it. On Wednesday Mr. Trump called President Zelensky not a good guy to deal with. Mr. Zelensky was in Brussels on Thursday, saying he sees no sign Moscow is ready to end the war. Ukraine has resumed strikes on Russian refineries, the two sides have started hitting each other's data centers, and Bulgaria has asked NATO for combat aircraft and anti-drone systems after drone attacks on shipping in the Black Sea. In a remarkable piece of reporting from the Journal, a twenty-six-year-old Russian lieutenant, Lev Stupnikov, secretly fed his army's battle plans to Ukraine, helping kill more than three hundred Russian soldiers before he escaped across the front line.

In the Middle East, Houthi missiles and drones struck government buildings in Yemen's southern city of Aden on Thursday, and reports put the toll at five dead and sixteen wounded. Saudi Arabia, which backs Yemen's government, accused Iran of trying to escalate the war.

On trade, talks toward a tariff deal between the United States and Mexico have stalled after objections from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who opposed deep cuts to tariffs on Mexican steel, aluminum and cars. It is the second time recently he has complicated a near-finished negotiation with a North American partner. In the markets, the Dow rose one point one percent on Thursday and the ten-year Treasury yield eased to about five point one percent, down from above five point one eight percent a day earlier. Bloomberg describes a rotation out of high-flying chipmakers toward the broader market, and the Journal says the riskiest corner of the artificial intelligence trade is colliding with higher rates. Those rates are hurting households too. Mortgage rates have pushed above seven percent, the highest in more than three years. Retail sales rose in September but more slowly than in August, and futures are pointing slightly higher this morning.

In corporate news, Meta released an open-weight AI model and is working with Apple to run it locally on high-end Macs, and TSMC says it may add U.S. chip capacity beyond Arizona. Netflix's chief content officer, Bela Bajaria, is leaving, to be replaced by Peter Rice. KFC is switching to Coca-Cola, a blow to PepsiCo, and after nine years the Tesla Roadster has finally launched. A federal case in Silicon Valley centers on an engineer accused of hiding a recorder in an OpenAI conference room. Harvard's endowment gained twenty-seven percent to nearly sixty-eight billion dollars, helped by bets on SpaceX and AI.

One more item from Washington. President Trump says the firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan will not be livestreamed, reversing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's earlier plan.

And a few lighter notes to close. A year after thieves stole France's crown jewels from the Louvre, the Journal reports that more than a dozen other museums have been hit by smash-and-grab gangs. The Milwaukee Brewers swept the Dodgers to reach their first World Series in forty-four years. And a deluxe Shohei Ohtani action figure is on sale for three hundred ninety-nine dollars.

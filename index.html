Morning Briefing

Thursday, November 26, 2026

Sources used: the Wall Street Journal front-page capture (note: it is the Wednesday, November 25 print edition, so it is a day old) and yesterday's WSJ 10-Point and What's News email, since today's 10-Point had not arrived; the New York Times front page and The Morning; the San Diego Union-Tribune front page and e-Edition email; and limited web reporting, which returned little dated today.

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 26, 2026. Happy Thanksgiving.

The story dominating business news this week is the unraveling of the artificial-intelligence trade. The Journal reports that Anthropic, which just completed the largest initial public offering on record, closed Tuesday at about $82, below its $83 offer price for the first time and its third straight losing session, down 6.5 percent on the day. The Nasdaq fell 1.5 percent and the S&P 500 half a percent, while the Dow rose nearly a percent. Stock futures pointed lower again yesterday morning, with Nasdaq futures down more than two percent at one point. The Times says stocks slumped again on worries about A.I., tariffs and inflation, and ran a separate piece suggesting the Anthropic debut could hurt other tech start-ups by soaking up investor money. The Journal also published a sobering analysis of what an A.I. bust could mean for the economy: a painful hit, though economists still argue over how big, and some compare the risk to the dot-com crash.

Tied to that is the saga of Nvidia and its chief executive. After Jensen Huang's ouster over reports of an affair, Attorney General Blanche told President Trump over the weekend that the Justice Department had no evidence Huang was a security risk, and Trump made clear he wanted him back. The Journal describes a frantic White House push, and a 10-Point item says Wall Street and Washington alike believed Huang uniquely qualified to run the chipmaker, and that he now has to prove them right. The Journal also reports that Congress was told China was not behind the affair, while another Journal piece looks at a different Nvidia executive's relationship tied to a company with China links. The Times frames the return as a new front in presidential economic intervention, notes that it clashed with the administration's other policies and rattled markets, and says Beijing sees the comeback as a mixed blessing. Late-night hosts, the Times adds, would like a word with Huang's therapist.

The Fed is the other market worry. The Times reports that the central bank's preferred inflation gauge ran hot again, keeping it on track to raise interest rates next month, and notes that while the country is not technically in a recession, many Americans feel as if it is. Ten-year Treasury yields were around 4.86 percent in the Journal's capture, with the volatility index elevated, oil near 85 dollars and gold near 3,500.

On trade, the Trump administration unveiled a new round of tariffs on dozens of countries, including China, Japan and the members of the European Union, citing unfair trade practices. The Times says it comes as the administration faces legal obstacles and scrutiny from an incoming Democratic-led Congress. The Union-Tribune's front page runs the Times story, noting some rates reach 20 percent and that the administration signaled China's could climb to 40 percent, and that critics warn of higher prices. I could not find independent wire confirmation of the details this morning, so treat the rates as the Times's account.

Other business news from the Journal: Sanofi agreed to buy the drugmaker Insmed for 41 billion dollars in cash to refill its pipeline ahead of a major patent expiry. Stripe's valuation is set to top 200 billion dollars in a share buyback. Abercrombie and Fitch and Dick's Sporting Goods both raised full-year outlooks ahead of the holidays, Dick's helped by improving trends at Foot Locker. Jaguar is at the center of a fight over whether European carmakers can use intellectual-property law against Chinese rivals. The Pentagon is talking with General Motors and other manufacturers about mass-producing drones. The Journal also reports a fund run by Leopold Aschenbrenner told investors it lost 65 percent in the third quarter. Jamie Dimon, meanwhile, is reportedly irked at Britain's planned bank tax on JPMorgan, and the Journal's newsletter flagged Americans' fatigue with streaming prices, a push on seat belts by the auto industry, and the finding that Venezuela's interim authorities disclosed 237 billion dollars in national debt. The Times notes the Navy is retiring its wool peacoat, and that Bob Iger's exit from Disney comes with an unwelcome biography.

Turning to the world. In the tech-and-law arena, a federal jury convicted China's Huawei of fraud and other offenses but acquitted it of the racketeering conspiracy charge, the Journal says. Top U.S. intelligence agencies suffered a breach of an unclassified system, according to both the Journal and the Times. In China, Xi Jinping has purged a fourth Politburo member in under two years, and the Times says Shanghai's party chief is under investigation. Russian warplanes skirted or entered the airspace of U.S allies in Asia, prompting Japan and South Korea to scramble jets, and the Journal has an exclusive on efforts to avert conflict with Russia in the Baltic. The Times, citing hacked government data, reports that more than 700,000 Russian soldiers have died in Ukraine. It also reports Libyan forces threatening retaliation over Ukraine's attack on Russian ships, and North Korea expelling Swiss diplomats in a spat tied to the Ukraine war.

In the Middle East, a Houthi projectile struck the tallest tower in Riyadh for the second time in under a week; the Times says 11 people were wounded, and it has an explainer on how the Iranian-backed group gained the upper hand in Yemen. In Israel, opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot says he is making progress toward ousting Prime Minister Netanyahu. Elsewhere, Japan's prime minister broke with Washington to defend the International Criminal Court, and Japanese commentators feel Beijing snubbed her at the recent APEC summit. The Solomon Islands entered a security treaty with Australia and said it will allow no foreign bases, calming fears of a slide toward China. The Trump administration paused joint operations with Ecuador. Norway has closed its last Arctic coal mine, Canada's boreal forest is being reshaped by logging, 13 people died in a flooded illegal gold mine in Indonesia, and the Union-Tribune notes record migrant arrivals in Spain's Canary Islands and a Salvadoran man arriving in Spain who hopes to return to the U.S. That is Kilmar Abrego Garcia, who the Journal says is leaving the U.S. after a judge let him arrange his own departure.

In U.S. politics, Democrats are preparing for control of the House and Senate oversight. The Times says they are eyeing weakened regulation and corporate corruption, and the Journal says congressional Democrats are targeting companies tied to an investment firm affiliated with Donald Trump Jr. A fight over who leads Senate Democrats is splitting the party over Israel, with Chris Van Hollen challenging Chuck Schumer, and Jane Fonda and Robert De Niro among those saying Schumer should step aside. Republicans are debating why they lost this year, and the Supreme Court is the reason Louisiana's midterms are not yet over. Pennsylvania governor Josh Shapiro told the Journal that neither Biden nor Trump did enough to rein in Netanyahu, and the Journal says Democrats are already diluting Medicare for All. NATO's Mark Rutte was due to meet Trump in Palm Beach. The Times also reports a prosecutor urged the Justice Department to reconsider charges against a fugitive, the Smithsonian's leader defending its autonomy after a report of a Trump overhaul plan, and Florida's D.E.I. law prompting cancellation of a St. Patrick's Day parade.

Other national news: the Army detonated nearly five tons of degraded weapons materials in southeast Iowa. The Trump administration called it a success, but experts worry wind could spread lead dust, and the Times says officials and scientists are testing air and soil while FEMA's emergency line did not give promised help. Long flight delays are at their worst in more than 15 years, the Times says. A stormy East Coast forecast led Macy's to reschedule its Thanksgiving Day parade to Saturday, the Times's Morning newsletter reports; the Journal's capture, from a day earlier, still featured the parade's new rodent star, so check before planning around it. California's king tides partially collapsed a pier. A FedEx jet overshot a Miami runway, closing three of four. Mayor Zohran Mamdani won a rent-freeze lawsuit against landlords. Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie settled their legal fight over a French winery. Major League Baseball sued its players over an impending work stoppage, per the Journal's evening email.

Closer to home, the Union-Tribune leads with El Niño high tides that set a record in San Diego, with damage described as minor; the paper warns conditions could be worse if big waves arrive atop deeper tidal waters. The preliminary record was set near the 7-foot mark, and officials urged beachgoers to stay off jetties. Imperial Beach and Mission Beach saw flooding and surfers wading the streets. Also on the front page, families at the Ronald McDonald House in San Diego will share a Thanksgiving meal, many with children in long hospital stays. Researchers at the Salk Institute are working to breed a climate-resilient cranberry as warming hurts harvests, and a lawsuit calls a San Diego agency's fee on low-income housing illegal. Inside, National City's nutrition center served turkey and karaoke, Imperial Beach weighs reviving a business improvement district, a panel highlighted struggles of homeless-youth nonprofits, an mpox surge has renewed calls for vaccination, and Carlsbad has asked staff to assess beach loss.

In sports, the Padres signed Romero to a three-year deal. San Diego State faces Maryland in Las Vegas at the Players Era tournament, a big test for the Aztecs. The Rams beat the Packers in the NFL's first Thanksgiving Eve game, with Corum shining, and the Steelers released Aaron Rodgers after a scathing interview about coach McCarthy. LeBron James had a triple-double in a 76ers win over Miami, and a Georgetown feature looks at bringing the Hoyas to new audiences.

And a Thanksgiving thought from The Times's Sam Sifton: whatever goes wrong with the gravy, everything will be fine. Cook the turkey to 165 degrees, wash a few dishes, and welcome the difficult cousin.

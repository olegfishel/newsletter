Good morning, it's Thursday, July 30, 2026.

The most consequential story of the day is unfolding in the skies over the Middle East, where the United States carried out a sweeping overnight campaign of strikes against Iran in retaliation for Tuesday's surprise ballistic-missile attack on American forces in Jordan. U.S. Central Command says aircraft and warships struck dozens of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targets inside Iran — military command centers, missile and drone production facilities, coastal surveillance posts, and maritime capabilities — in what the Pentagon described as a "powerful response." Jordan's military shot down five Iranian missiles targeting its territory during the early hours of the assault. Iran's Revolutionary Guard has since threatened to "punish the aggressor today" and warned that any nation assisting the United States will face a harsh response, raising genuine fears of yet another escalation.

The strikes follow what the Wall Street Journal describes as a classic calm-before-the-storm pattern. Washington and Tehran signed a memorandum of understanding to pause their hostilities in mid-June, but by early July Iran had returned to threatening commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, and fresh theaters of conflict have since opened across the region. U.S. and Saudi forces launched joint strikes against Iran-backed militias in Iraq on Tuesday, after those groups attacked oil facilities in Riyadh and Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. An apparent drone attack struck an Egyptian port, hitting two ships including an American tanker. Oil prices surged more than seven percent on the renewed fighting. The New York Times reports this morning that Iran is growing more determined, not less, as the conflict extends — and that the Pentagon has already earmarked more than $120 billion for contractors to accelerate production of Patriot missiles and long-delayed submarines to support future combat operations.

Against that backdrop, the Federal Reserve delivered its own unsettling signal on Wednesday. The Fed held interest rates steady in the 3.5% to 3.75% range, but three regional bank presidents dissented, voting for an immediate quarter-point increase — the most unified hawkish dissent since 2016. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, in his second meeting leading the central bank, made his intentions unmistakable: "There is no soft implicit target. Not on this committee's watch. There's only a target and it's 2%." The Core PCE inflation gauge has climbed from 3.0% in December to 3.4% in May, powered in large part by surging energy prices tied to the Middle East conflict. Markets fell sharply in response: the S&P 500 shed 1.5%, the Dow dropped 2.2%, and the Nasdaq lost 1.7%. Futures recovered partially this morning. The Bank of England announces its own rate decision later today, and the Commerce Department releases its first estimate of second-quarter GDP alongside the PCE index.

Technology's biggest names reported earnings Wednesday afternoon and the results were a study in contrasts. Microsoft posted quarterly revenue of $90 billion, up 18%, with Azure surpassing $100 billion for the first time and Microsoft 365 Copilot reaching 30 million paid seats. Capital expenditures soared 70% to $41 billion as the company races to build out AI infrastructure. Meta told a more complicated story: revenue rose 28% to $60.8 billion, but net income fell 14% as spending surged 55% to $42 billion, with the company now projecting full-year capital expenditures of $130 to $145 billion. Wall Street punished Meta with a 10% stock drop while rewarding Microsoft with a 2.4% after-hours gain. The divergence crystallizes a widening debate about when — and whether — AI investment converts to returns.

The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point this morning carries a fascinating counterpoint to the hyperscaler spending wars: while the big platforms argue AI justifies tens of billions in annual infrastructure, a wave of individual entrepreneurs is already pocketing the gains. Payments processor Stripe reports that solo operators generating over $1 million in annual revenue doubled between 2023 and 2025. One founder profiled by the Journal launched an AI-tools company last December, signed up 10,000 paying customers, and is on track for $10 million in revenue this year — without a single additional hire. AI handles his emails, customer service, and administrative work. The rise of the million-dollar one-person company, the Journal argues, could reshape assumptions about hiring and the broader labor market.

On Capitol Hill Wednesday, the most dramatic tableau of the week came when former NIAID director Anthony Fauci appeared before the Senate Homeland Security Committee, chaired by Rand Paul. Fauci invoked the Fifth Amendment more than 100 times, refusing to answer a single question. He called Paul's relentless pursuit an "unhinged obsession" — a phrase Paul intends to cite when he brings a contempt vote before the committee next week. Separately, the Justice Department's criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey deepened: court filings by Comey's lawyers reveal that President Trump personally directed the Secret Service to forward him a summary of its interview with Comey, and that a senior DOJ official sought to ping Comey's phone without a warrant while agents tailed his car from North Carolina to Virginia. Todd Blanche's nomination to be attorney general — which would put him in charge of supervising the Comey case — stalled when Senators Cornyn and Tillis forced a delay over a still-unresolved IRS settlement that some believe provides the Trump family with tax immunity worth an estimated $100 million.

Elsewhere: Russia charged Telegram founder Pavel Durov with abetting terrorism, alleging the platform has been used by Ukraine to plan attacks inside Russia. A federal judge sided with New Jersey, ruling the state need not hand over unredacted voter rolls to the Trump administration. In Japan, rescue crews are racing to find survivors of an earthquake. The University of California has offered admission to a record number of in-state Californians. A WSJ investigation reveals Morgan Stanley bankers faced pressure to approve mortgages for wealthy clients even when underwriting standards were questionable. And tonight, Apple and Amazon report their own earnings, adding to what has already been a pivotal week for Big Tech. The Padres, for their part, have won five games in a row.

Morning Briefing

Thursday, August 6, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters, Bloomberg, Axios, and AP wire reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 6, 2026. The big story hovering over financial markets and diplomatic back-channels alike is the Strait of Hormuz: Iran and Oman have agreed on the coordinates for a single shipping corridor through the waterway, and a joint statement is now in its final drafting stage. Bloomberg and Axios both reported Wednesday evening that Washington is hoping to announce a formal interim deal within hours, one that would give Tehran oversight of vessel transits without allowing it to levy tolls or service fees. The agreement, brokered with Oman's mediation, would run one to three months — long enough, supporters hope, to stabilize global energy markets that have been roiled since the conflict began in early 2026. Crude oil edged up more than one percent overnight on the news, trading around $76 a barrel, as traders weigh whether a partial reopening actually holds.

Meanwhile, a Wall Street Journal investigation revealed this morning that President Trump has been calling Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh repeatedly since Warsh took the post — sometimes several times in a single week, sometimes going silent for weeks at a stretch. The conversations are reportedly informal, with Trump seeking Warsh's read on how the Iran war and the A.I. investment surge are affecting the American economy. Neither side claims interest rates have been discussed directly, and the White House insists the Fed's independence is being respected. But the sheer frequency of contact breaks sharply with a decades-old convention designed to keep politics and monetary policy at arm's length. The Fed's next rate decision comes later this month, and the disclosure lands at a sensitive moment, with markets scrutinizing every signal about the trajectory of borrowing costs.

The political world spent Wednesday night digesting a landmark result in Michigan, where progressive physician Abdul El-Sayed narrowly defeated incumbent Representative Haley Stevens in the Democratic Senate primary by roughly one percentage point. It is the most prominent left-wing victory in a competitive battleground state in years, and it signals that the pro-Palestinian movement and the economic-justice wing of the party have genuine organizing muscle outside of safe blue districts. In a parallel race, democratic-socialist state representative Donavan McKinney ousted Representative Shri Thanedar. And in a quirk of the evening, Trump's preferred Republican primary candidate for a battleground House seat — Amir Hassan — lost to a little-known challenger who raised all of $1,850, ran no television ads, and had suspended his campaign the previous month. The New York Times notes the results handed advocates of a data-center moratorium an unexpected boost: the winning candidate in one Democratic House primary had made opposition to a new OpenAI facility a central plank of her campaign, a sign of how energy costs and tech-sector sprawl are becoming live political issues.

That brings us to what the Times is calling the defining domestic story of the summer: artificial-intelligence data centers and their ravenous appetite for power. The Trump administration has pushed A.I. companies to build or buy their own generation capacity so as not to strain residential grids, and the result has been a nationwide boom in gas-burning power plants. At least eighty-two such plants are being built or proposed solely to feed data centers. Elon Musk recently purchased a company specializing in rapid-deployment gas and diesel turbines. Texas, which may soon overtake Virginia as the world's largest data center market, has been fast-tracking permits with minimal public oversight. The nation's largest electrical-grid operator warned last month that data centers will add billions in charges to consumer bills over the next three years. Gartner projects global data-center power demand will grow twenty-six percent this year alone. More than half of Americans now say they would support a national ban on new facilities — a statistic that would have seemed far-fetched two years ago.

In other corporate news, Google overhauled the leadership of its artificial-intelligence operations and named a new chief scientist to replace longtime incumbent Jeff Dean, who is departing. Meta Platforms separately launched a new coding agent it is calling Muse Code, pitching it as a cheaper alternative to rivals such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor. On the markets side, Wednesday's session ended in mixed fashion: the Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed half a percent to a record close of 54,427, lifted by strong earnings from Honda — which more than doubled first-quarter profit on yen weakness — while the S&P 500 slipped two-tenths and the Nasdaq gave back about eight-tenths of a percent. Citadel's acquisition of the bulk of the AI-focused hedge fund Situational Awareness's stock portfolio was credited with bolstering the firm's July performance. And federal prosecutors were reported to have reviewed earlier this year whether JPMorgan Chase executives ignored deficiencies in the bank's anti-fraud program and improperly denied more than a hundred million dollars in reimbursements to defrauded customers.

On the health and policy front, the Senate confirmed Erica Schwartz as the permanent director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — the first confirmed CDC chief in nearly a year. The F.D.A. approved Moderna's mRNA-based flu vaccine for adults fifty and older, the first shot of its kind to receive clearance after a review process that nearly fell apart. A Senate panel investigating Anthony Fauci has obtained a copy of the doctor's iPhone, potentially adding to the documentary record of his conduct during the Covid-19 pandemic. And separately, federal agents have been using airline flight manifests to arrest travelers with lapsed visas at airports, drawing sharp pushback from carriers who argue the practice endangers safety and protocol.

A few other notes before we turn to the structured headlines. In Missouri, voters defeated a ballot measure that would have allowed the legislature to eliminate the state income tax and replace it with higher sales taxes. Representative Chuck Edwards of North Carolina announced he would not seek re-election, two days after a House investigation found he had made unwelcome sexual advances toward female staffers. And in global sports governance, FIFA President Gianni Infantino secured backing from senior executives at an emergency meeting and said he intends to run for re-election next March.

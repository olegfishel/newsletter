Good morning, it's Saturday, August 8, 2026. The American economy delivered a startling jolt before the weekend: the Labor Department reported Friday that the country shed twenty-three thousand jobs in July, a result that upended expectations for modest gains and left economists grasping for explanations. The unemployment rate, counterintuitively, ticked down to four point one percent — not because more people found work, but because the labor force itself shrank as workers gave up searching. The participation rate fell to sixty-one point four percent, its lowest in five years. Prior months were revised down sharply, with May and June together losing one hundred and three thousand more jobs than first reported. It is, the Journal notes, the kind of report that sends conflicting signals: the headline number looked bad, but a smaller supply of workers can keep unemployment low even without robust hiring. The weak data did cool pressure on the Federal Reserve to raise rates, though analysts were careful not to declare the hike cycle finished.

Markets took the data in stride. U.S. stocks rose through the week, with the S&P 500 setting a record by Friday's close, gaining three point six percent over the five sessions. The Nasdaq climbed five point two percent and the Dow added three percent, suggesting investors interpreted the weak jobs print as a reason to expect rates to stay put rather than a warning of recession.

The most consequential diplomatic development of the morning comes from the Persian Gulf, where Iran has vowed to bar United States Navy warships from transiting the Strait of Hormuz as part of any agreement to reopen the waterway to commercial shipping. Tehran is using the reopening negotiations — held partly through Omani intermediaries — to press its long-standing goal of curtailing America's decades-old military presence in the Gulf. The U.S. imposed a naval blockade of Iran in April, and at least some commercial traffic has resumed through the Strait in recent days. But Tehran's insistence that American warships be excluded from the passage sets up a confrontation that could upend any emerging deal. Iran also reportedly possesses enough weapons-grade nuclear fuel to quickly build as many as ten nuclear bombs, though its program has been slowed but not destroyed by the ongoing conflict.

Against that backdrop, three major powers signed a sweeping new security arrangement Friday: Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan inked a mutual-defense agreement aimed at shoring up regional security after years of upheaval in the Middle East. The pact marks a notable realignment, drawing together a NATO member, the Arab world's dominant oil power, and the Islamic world's only nuclear-armed state — all three increasingly wary of both Iran and the durability of American security guarantees.

Back in Washington, the Senate confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general in a vote of fifty to forty-nine, wrapping up a late-night session that stretched past four in the morning Saturday. The confirmation, which had been in doubt for weeks, was rescued by Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who announced Friday that he would support Blanche despite concerns about his independence. Only Susan Collins and Lisa Murkowski crossed party lines to oppose him. Blanche, who served as President Trump's personal defense attorney during multiple criminal trials, now leads the Justice Department. The New York Times described the confirmation as bitterly divided, with bipartisan concern about whether Blanche will be willing to push back against the president's impulses. Separately, Trump revived his effort to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, pressing her to respond to what he called allegations of mortgage fraud — claims Cook has called baseless. The Supreme Court earlier rebuffed Trump's first attempt to fire her, and White House aides are now said to be considering further action.

In a remarkable move in the technology world, OpenAI announced it has paused some development work on its coming artificial-intelligence model, internally called "Astra," after safety evaluations found the company could not rule out that the system had reached what its own preparedness framework calls "critical" cybersecurity capabilities. That designation means a model may be able to identify and exploit zero-day vulnerabilities in hardened computer systems without human assistance — a threshold no prior OpenAI model had crossed. The announcement underscored how rapidly the frontier of AI capability is advancing, even as the broader software industry is being dislocated. The Journal's front page noted that generative AI is steamrollering once-booming software-as-a-service companies, as executives race to reinvent their products before they are rendered obsolete. "You have to burn the ships and start from the ground up," one executive said.

On a lighter note, the New York Times Book Review this week published its list of the fifty best thrillers of the twenty-first century — a project assembled from the ballots of hundreds of writers, booksellers, editors, and librarians. Tana French, S.A. Cosby, Lionel Shriver, and William Landay's "Defending Jacob" all made the cut. If you are looking for something to read this weekend, that is a place to start. In cinemas, "Spider-Man: Brand New Day" defied the broader superhero slump, raking in nearly a billion dollars in its opening weekend. And for television devotees, "Ted Lasso" returned for its fourth season on Apple TV.

Elsewhere: Colombia inaugurated Abelardo de la Espriella as its new president in the symbolically significant city of Cali, with vows to suppress drug gangs using a heavy hand, representing a sharp rightward shift for the country. In Thailand, a teenager killed at least seven people in attacks at a school and a residence. In Puerto Rico, more than half the island is now under drought conditions, and officials have begun rationing water. Hackers have struck multiple U.S. water utilities, the Union-Tribune reports, following years of warnings about the vulnerability of critical infrastructure. And in New York City, a group of homeowners have sued to delay Mayor Zohran Mamdani's new pied-à-terre tax, which would apply to properties valued at five million dollars or more.

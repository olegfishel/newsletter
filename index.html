Morning Briefing

Tuesday, September 1, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition, Monday August 31 — yesterday's edition), and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 1, 2026.

The Pentagon is in fresh turmoil this morning. Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, the youngest person ever to hold that position, has resigned after eighteen months of mounting friction with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The two clashed repeatedly: Hegseth pushed Driscoll to fire the Army's chief spokesman over unflattering news coverage, and the defense secretary later removed four senior officers from a promotions list — including two Black men and two women — after Driscoll refused to do so. Driscoll was also an ally of General Randy George, the Army's top uniformed officer whom Hegseth ousted in April. The Wall Street Journal reports that Driscoll felt his modernization agenda was being blocked and that Hegseth's constant churning of senior generals was creating chaos across the service. His departure is the latest in a string of civilian and uniformed departures that have reshaped the military's leadership since the administration took office.

The dominant story on today's Journal front page, however, carries the headline "Washington's Deadly New Headache" — and it comes from the Middle East. Yemen's Houthi rebels and al-Shabaab, al-Qaeda's wealthiest affiliate in East Africa, have formed an unlikely alliance. Long divided by sectarian lines, the two groups were brought together by shared hostility toward the West and a brokered trade of weapons, training and cash. The Journal's Michael Phillips explains how a single broker helped set this partnership in motion before a U.S. airstrike killed him. The resulting alliance now threatens a strategic maritime chokepoint for the global oil trade and adds to the broader destabilization of the Middle East, where mine warfare with Iran is already hampering American efforts to assert control over the Strait of Hormuz.

In Ukraine, Russia is pressing a relentless campaign to pound the capital into submission. Jet-powered drones have struck Kyiv for five consecutive days, leaving some supermarket shelves bare after repeated hits on warehouses and continuing to batter the city's power grid ahead of winter. Ukraine has responded with its own long-range offensive capability — the Journal reports that Kyiv's "Mammoth" drones can fly fifteen hundred miles deep into Russian territory, marking a significant escalation in Ukraine's ability to strike the Russian heartland. Meanwhile, Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly blamed Canadian Prime Minister Carney for the recent collapse of trade talks that had been aimed at averting steep U.S. tariffs on Canadian goods.

On Wall Street, the momentum trade that minted money in the second quarter has come apart. The strategy of chasing stocks that are already rising — sometimes called trend-following — worked spectacularly through the spring but has reversed sharply. What was once a self-fulfilling prophecy has become a losing game. Stocks slid broadly on Monday, with the S&P 500 shedding nearly 0.7 percent, the Dow off about the same, and the Nasdaq down more than a point. Oil moved the other way, crude rising about three percent to just under ninety dollars a barrel as Middle East tensions rattled energy markets.

The federal government fired a major shot at Amazon on Monday. The Federal Trade Commission, joined by more than twenty states, sued the e-commerce giant for allegedly manipulating the prices that advertisers paid to appear in its search results. The complaint, filed in Seattle, claims Amazon secretly gamed its ad auctions beginning in 2018, converting what it marketed as a second-price auction into a first-price auction — effectively overcharging more than a million advertisers, including five hundred thousand small businesses, by an estimated twenty billion dollars over seven years. Amazon called the lawsuit misguided and said it strongly disagrees. Its shares fell about two and a half percent on the news.

In other business news, Anthropic signed a cloud-computing deal valued at thirty-five billion dollars with Lambda, an Nvidia-backed data center provider. Fashion giant Shein is going public in Hong Kong this week in a listing that will allow it to avoid paying out billions to early investors by converting preferred shares into ordinary equity. And the oil-services firm SLB agreed to acquire thermal management company Kelvion from private equity for four-point-one billion dollars.

The New York Times this morning leads with a cultural reckoning at the Met. Editor and tastemaker Anna Wintour, who has presided over the Costume Institute's annual gala for more than three decades, is facing serious scrutiny over the choice of designer John Galliano — convicted of an antisemitic hate crime by a French court in 2011 — as the honoree for the 2027 Met Gala. Yesterday, Galliano withdrew from the planned exhibition, citing the controversy. The Times notes the decision has shaken Wintour's standing in ways that few previous missteps have, with politicians, Jewish community leaders and major museum donors openly questioning her judgment. Separately, Vice President JD Vance called Michigan's Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed "very, very evil" at a campaign rally near Detroit. And in Massachusetts today, voters are choosing between eighty-year-old Senator Ed Markey and forty-seven-year-old Congressman Seth Moulton in a Democratic primary that has become something of a referendum on generational change within the party.

A sad milestone in a decades-old murder case: a former Crips gang leader has been convicted of orchestrating the 1996 drive-by shooting that killed rapper Tupac Shakur in Las Vegas. In San Diego, a seventeen-year-old girl who livestreamed the shooting at a local mosque in May — an attack that killed three people — has been charged with murder. And at the Grand Canyon, rescue crews have been airlifting visitors from the depths of the canyon after a violent weekend flash flood at Phantom Ranch killed at least two people and left roughly fifteen more missing. More than sixty hikers were evacuated by helicopter, and nearly all footbridges over Bright Angel Creek were destroyed. A continued flash flood watch remains in effect through at least Wednesday as monsoon moisture persists.

Finally, a note from the Journal's lighter side: American goaltender Aerin Frankel of the Olympic hockey team has turned her obsession with Caesar salads into a side business, becoming what the paper calls a global authority on the dish. And Londoners are increasingly frustrated by supercar-driving foreign tourists who park wherever they like on the city's most expensive streets — racking up fines they never pay.

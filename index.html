Saturday, October 24, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal (today's front page and The 10-Point, Friday's edition), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune (Friday's e-Edition), and current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 24, 2026. The Middle East dominates the start of the weekend, as fears grow that the standoff between Iran and the United States and Israel is about to flare up again.

Tehran has warned that if American and Israeli airstrikes resume, it will retaliate against airports, pipelines and other civilian targets across the Persian Gulf. Iran's armed forces chief said this weekend that U.S. naval vessels can no longer approach the waters around the Gulf, and seven oil tankers have been set on fire or damaged in the Strait of Hormuz this week, either by Iranian attacks or by naval mines. American intelligence officials, meanwhile, are warning that Iranian plots against U.S. officials have reached an advanced stage. The anxiety is showing up in energy markets, where U.S. crude climbed above one hundred dollars a barrel on Friday.

The tension is also building closer to Israel. Two Israeli soldiers were killed and three wounded by Palestinian gunmen near Hebron in the West Bank on Friday, and the attackers were shot dead. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has ordered preparations for a full lockdown of the West Bank and promised a major operation in the Hebron neighborhood the gunmen came from. Today, Israeli forces imposed closures across the city and displaced residents as they searched for weapons and suspects. To the south, a Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile fired toward Riyadh by the Iran-backed Houthis, whose recent strikes on Saudi airports have knocked out one of the last safe air routes between Europe and Asia.

On Wall Street, the week ended on a down note. The Dow fell about six hundred and fifty points, or 1.2 percent, to just over fifty-two thousand, while the S&P 500 lost half a percent and the Nasdaq slipped about a fifth of a percent. For the week, the Dow was slightly higher, and the S&P and Nasdaq were modestly lower. Moderna was a major casualty after details of its cancer-vaccine study leaked early on Thursday; the stock fell by as much as a quarter that day, even though some oncologists praised the melanoma data, because the results raised questions about how widely the treatment would be used. American Express said it will cut five percent of its global workforce to rein in costs, and Volvo Car plans to eliminate another twenty-five hundred jobs. Apple's first foldable iPhone went on sale Friday to mixed reviews, and SK Hynix scrapped plans for a big public offering of its Solidigm flash-memory unit.

In Washington, the final stretch before the midterm elections is turning on questions of power and corruption. President Trump said he personally directed the Justice Department to sue Alaska to block certification of its Senate race if Republican Dan Sullivan narrowly loses. The department argues that a second candidate with a nearly identical name, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., could confuse voters. Legal experts say the case has little chance of success, but it is feeding fears that the administration is laying the groundwork to contest results it dislikes. Democrats have spent a hundred and eighty-two million dollars this year hammering corruption, and House Democrats are already eyeing the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth if they win the chamber. The Justice Department also charged two former FBI employees with lying to Congress about investigations touching Hunter Biden. And in a move widely seen as political, the administration is exempting some Canadian products from fifty percent tariffs to help Maine Senator Susan Collins and other Republicans.

A separate fight on Capitol Hill involves Ukraine. President Trump called Elon Musk and asked him to lift limits on a military-grade version of Starlink that Ukraine's forces depend on. Ukrainian officials say service is only partly improved, and senators of both parties have told the White House they will block Trump's nominees until full satellite communications are restored. On the battlefield, Russia and Ukraine traded heavy barrages again, and First Lady Melania Trump said she helped broker a local cease-fire in a southern Ukrainian city.

In France, a gunman firing from a vehicle on Friday killed one person and wounded at least five more, including a twelve-year-old girl, in the Paris suburbs of Trappes and La Verrière. President Macron called it a racist terror attack. The suspect was arrested within minutes. Authorities have deployed some seventy thousand security personnel across the country for today's cost-of-living protests, hoping to avoid a repeat of last weekend's unrest, and Art Basel's Paris fair is closing early for safety reasons. Elsewhere in Asia, Kim Jong Un is in China on his first visit in a year.

In technology and business, the Journal reports that AI-powered hacking is overwhelming the internet, with a serious flaw fueling what is being called Black October. Separately, three OpenAI safety researchers were fired after the company suspected them of leaking details of its model designs, an episode that shows the growing tension over who should see inside powerful AI systems. And in China, Xinjiang, once the focus of international condemnation, has become a showcase for Beijing's commercial ambitions, with tourism alone bringing in about fifty billion dollars last year.

Finally, a few lighter notes. The Journal found a musician's goofy, high-pitched rap has become the unlikely anthem of the Brewers' playoff run. The World Series is under way, and tomorrow the Packers visit the Lions in what The New York Times calls the game of the week. And with fall settling in, The Times invites you to be a little less outdoorsy and a little more outsidey, and simply go for a walk among the leaves.

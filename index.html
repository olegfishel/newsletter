Morning Briefing

Tuesday, September 8, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front-page capture & 10-Point newsletter), The New York Times Morning newsletter, San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, supplemented by Reuters, AP, Washington Post, Bloomberg.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 8, 2026.

The easy days are over for investors. After a summer buoyed by strong corporate earnings and a surge in technology shares, Wall Street opened the fall season firmly in the red. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell more than six hundred points, or roughly one and a quarter percent, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq each slipped about half a percent. Rising oil prices — with crude pushing toward a hundred dollars a barrel — and the specter of another Federal Reserve interest-rate increase later this month rattled nerves. The Fed's next policy decision falls on September sixteenth, and with inflation still running hot, partly because of a shooting war in the Middle East, a rate hike is very much on the table. As the Wall Street Journal put it plainly: "The Fed and inflation are back in focus."

The Middle East conflict is a major driver of that inflation threat. Over the weekend, U.S. Navy vessels struck three Iranian oil tankers in retaliation for Iranian ballistic missile attacks on American warships, escalating a conflict that began in February when American and Israeli forces launched an air campaign against Iran. The U.S. naval blockade, now in its fifth month, has choked Tehran's oil exports down to roughly two hundred sixty thousand barrels a day — an eighty percent drop from a year ago. Brent crude is hovering near ninety-nine dollars a barrel. Iran, for its part, is threatening to establish a new exclusion zone near the Strait of Hormuz. The economic squeeze on Tehran is severe: the country's most important export earner is drying up, deepening a crisis that was already acute before the shooting started. Meanwhile, Israel stepped up strikes in southern Lebanon this week, killing twenty-seven people, and Houthi rebels in Yemen targeted civilian sites along the Saudi Red Sea coast, raising fears the conflict could widen further.

The U.S. trade war with Canada entered an ugly new chapter overnight. Canada's retaliatory tariffs — matching Washington's fifty-percent duties dollar for dollar — took effect at one minute past midnight. They cover about twenty billion dollars of American goods annually, hitting sectors including steel, dairy, agricultural equipment, paper, and electronics. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, whose country left the negotiating table last month declaring itself "at war" with the United States, is drawing strong public support: three-quarters of Canadians backed his decision to walk out. President Trump responded with social media threats to target Bombardier, the Canadian aircraft maker, and suggested he might halt bilateral trade outright. The political fallout could be significant at home: states like Michigan, Ohio, and Kentucky are particularly exposed to the Canadian counter-tariffs, and Democrats see the economic pain as a potential opening in the coming midterm elections. The New York Times notes that the dispute has united Canadians in a way that has little modern precedent — a remarkable development for neighbors who long prided themselves on the world's friendliest border.

In Europe, Germany's political center is in serious trouble. The far-right Alternative for Germany party won forty-four percent of the vote in Sunday's state election in Saxony-Anhalt — a historic result, and the AfD's strongest showing in any German state. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats collapsed to under eighteen percent. The result stops short of an absolute majority for the AfD, and Merz's party has ruled out any coalition with the far-right, but the political mathematics look grim. "This is the hardest defeat that the CDU has had for decades," Merz conceded. The AfD ran on restricting immigration, ending aid to Ukraine, and flirting with the idea of leaving the European Union — positions that would have been unthinkable on the mainstream German right just a decade ago. The question now is whether Merz can hold his government together at the federal level, or whether fresh elections are coming.

Elsewhere on the global chessboard, Belgium revealed that it had arrested a Chinese citizen on suspicion of stealing microchip technology, raising European anxiety about Beijing's quiet campaign for industrial secrets. Greenland, meanwhile, is adroitly playing both superpowers against each other: leveraging President Trump's well-documented threats to acquire the island, the Greenlandic government is drawing increased investment commitments from European allies — and the European Union just pledged two hundred thirty-two million dollars to underwrite Greenland's strategic position. Trump, for his part, also posted a social media map apparently suggesting the state of New Mexico be renamed "New America," his most expansive attempt yet to redraw the North American map in his own image.

On the domestic front, a Wall Street Journal investigation details how Corey Lewandowski, serving as a special government employee and adviser to then-DHS Secretary Kristi Noem, effectively controlled billions in agency spending while simultaneously pitching officials from Qatar and the United Arab Emirates to hire him privately for help with agency matters. An internal watchdog probe is scrutinizing potential improprieties including personal enrichment by senior officials. Lewandowski denies any wrongdoing. The story lands on the morning of the eighty-first United Nations General Assembly, which opens today in New York amid what the Journal calls "a record number of active global conflicts."

On a lighter economic note, the labor market continues to offer a striking paradox: Americans without college degrees are having one of the best job markets in years, with unemployment among younger non-college workers at historically low levels, while young college graduates are finding it unexpectedly hard to land an offer. The Journal also reports that Jaguar Land Rover will cut four thousand jobs as the automaker absorbs Trump's tariffs, a sales collapse in China, and the cost of its electric vehicle pivot. And in a sign of where retail investing is headed, AI agents are now being deployed by money managers to help run client portfolios — a development Wall Street executives predict will reshape the industry.

Finally, today marks a solemn day on the calendar. This Thursday will be the twenty-fifth anniversary of the September eleventh attacks. Events across San Diego County are already underway to mark the occasion. The New York Times notes that New York City's mayor is releasing more than a hundred seventy thousand pages of documents about environmental toxins in the months after the attacks, which suggest officials misled the public about air quality, including asbestos levels, near Ground Zero. And the Journal's Gerald Seib writes that while the attacks provoked a remarkable moment of national unity — one hard to imagine in today's political climate — that unity ultimately sowed the seeds of the partisanship that defines American life a generation later.

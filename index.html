Morning Briefing

Saturday, November 28, 2026

Sources: WSJ front page and The 10-Point; NYT front page and The Morning; San Diego Union-Tribune front page and e-Edition email. Web searches for today returned almost only older material, so web reporting added little beyond background on the Iran blockade.

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 28, 2026. We begin in the Gulf of Oman, where the U.S. military has struck and disabled a commercial vessel that was trying to run the American naval blockade of Iran.

The Journal's newsletter calls it the latest escalation in Iranian efforts to get around a blockade that has squeezed the country's economy, and it was among the Journal's most-read stories. The Union-Tribune carries a wire brief describing a ship disabled in a breach attempt. Background reporting shows this is a pattern: the blockade has been imposed, lifted and reimposed this year, and a similar strike on a cargo ship came in early November.

The standoff is also feeding oil markets. Prices were mixed Friday as traders weighed renewed threats to Middle East supplies ahead of a meeting of OPEC and its allies, which the Journal's newsletter places on Sunday.

Now to Ukraine. The Journal's front page explains how limits on Elon Musk's Starlink and Starshield satellite service force Kyiv to fly its drones with the brakes on. Fly too fast and the connection may cut out; fly too slow and the drone is easier to intercept. Ukraine faced such speed limits in two stretches this fall, and the newsletter warns they could weaken its mid-range strikes on Russia.

Also from the Journal: as Moscow keeps hitting critical infrastructure, millions of Ukrainians are weighing what red line would finally push them to leave. The Union-Tribune notes that President Zelenskyy is urging the United States to press Putin harder, a request he has made repeatedly through the year.

The Times adds two Ukraine-related angles. One reports that the war is devastating wildlife, from dolphins to moose and storks, because habitats are now full of anti-drone netting and minefields. The other says Russia's big ambitions in artificial intelligence are being undercut by the constraints of war.

On Gaza and Israel, the Journal reports that Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, a possible Democratic presidential candidate, said neither Biden nor Trump did enough to restrain Prime Minister Netanyahu during the war. Separately, an Israeli television investigation into soldiers' alleged use of Palestinians as human shields has provoked a nationalist backlash and a debate over military accountability. And the Times reports that Haaretz, Israel's liberal newspaper, is building up its U.S. presence as Israel moves to the right.

Turning to Europe and beyond. Spain holds a snap general election Sunday, with Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's future at stake. The Times profiles the leader of the far-right Vox party, who could end a taboo dating back to the death of Franco in 1975. Taiwan votes today in local elections and a referendum on reviving nuclear power.

In France, S&P Global cut its credit outlook to negative from stable, warning that mounting public debt threatens efforts to steady the country's finances. The Times has a feature on the deep crisis facing French bookstores. And the Journal says socialist-style policies, from nationalizations to price controls, are powering an electoral rebound for center-left parties across Europe.

In Britain, the Times reports that net migration has fallen to its lowest level since 1997, setting aside the pandemic years, and explains three things it has learned about how a friend of Jeffrey Epstein became Britain's ambassador to Washington. In Hungary, the opening of secret police files has set off a round of finger-pointing over who collaborated.

In Canada, the Supreme Court largely upheld a Quebec law that bars public-sector workers from wearing religious symbols on the job. The Times headline frames it as Quebec being allowed to keep its ban, while the Journal's brief emphasizes that the court largely, not fully, sided with the province.

A few more from around the world. Colombia's new president is turning the drug war into theater, the Journal says, as he steps up military operations against entrenched militias. The Times reports that Russia is erasing memories of the Gulag as part of a campaign to rehabilitate Stalin, and that Delhi residents are switching off refrigerators and leaving doors open to cope with unbearable hot nights.

The Times also reports on China's "female gaze" entertainment boom, from male dancers to hiking companions, which is testing the limits of Beijing's tolerance, and on a wedding on an Australian beach where antisemitic terror struck last year: two medics who treated shooting victims were married by an Orthodox rabbi, though they are not Jewish. The Times also looks at the disappearing Iranian meal, and the Journal at China's secondhand luxury boom, where resale sales rose more than 15% to over $4 billion even as the broader luxury market shrank.

Turning to U.S. politics. The Journal's front page says President Trump has staked much of his second-term legacy on artificial intelligence, and his loyalty to Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, including stepping in to reinstate him as CEO, is becoming a political cost. Republican lawmakers plan to investigate whether the administration and Nvidia skirted rules meant to keep advanced chips from China, while voters worry about lost jobs and insiders getting special deals.

The Times offers a counterpoint from the other side of the Pacific: Huawei, which Trump once tried to cripple, is now central to China's AI push.

The Times reports a new Trump tactic to restrict transit funding: rewriting the rules, which could make it harder for dozens of projects around the country to win money they have pursued for years. The Union-Tribune runs the story on its front page, adding that critics warn the changes could undermine investment in rail and bus service, and noting that the administration has awarded just one grant from a multibillion-dollar federal program.

Also from the Times: Walter Willett, a Harvard scientist behind the Mediterranean diet research, has had his grants frozen amid the administration's fight with Harvard, even though his field of diet and health is a priority for Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Another Times story reports that an FBI interview suggests the CIA waterboarded more than it admitted.

The Times also ran a look at the most detailed data yet on midterm voters, with five takeaways, and a report on how the fight between Congress and Trump may leave federal contractors as the losers. A Times investigation is probing the source of a roughly $300 million donation to Trump's Washington projects. The Journal's whimsical A-hed finds anarchy at a beloved public golf course in Washington, where Trump's plan to upgrade the East Potomac track has stalled. And on the opinion pages, the Times says goodbye to the Electoral College, while a Journal opinion piece warns that Democrats now control enough state governments to threaten it.

Turning to markets. Stocks rebounded to close a roller-coaster week. The Journal says the Nasdaq gained 6.7% for the week, its best since April, with the Dow up 5.5% and the S&P 500 up 5.6%, as tech shares jumped when investors took a break from worries about AI. The Times describes the same rally at the end of a week of wild swings.

The Journal's newsletter adds that more than 450 billion dollars has flowed into U.S. stock funds this year, even through November's selloff, and that stocks now make up over 40% of household financial assets, the highest share on record. That confidence supports the market, but it also means more people would feel a prolonged selloff.

In banking, a bookkeeping blunder in Citigroup's London hub, which the newsletter puts at 35 billion dollars, has prompted an internal shake-up and undermined the bank's efforts to repair its relationship with regulators around the world. Meanwhile, JPMorgan plans a private club for well-heeled clients in a renovated 1930s branch, with lounges, a dining room and a bar.

In other business news, a multiday tech outage at Allstate has left customers struggling to renew policies, file claims or summon roadside help during Thanksgiving week. In defense, BAE Systems said it has won 6 billion dollars in contracts to supply hundreds of combat vehicles to several European nations, while Rheinmetall lowered its sales and margin projections for the end of the decade after German cancellations and delays. And the Federal Trade Commission imposed strict limits on board representation for Italian gunmaker Beretta Holding and its minority stake in rival Sturm Ruger.

For consumers, the Times reports Americans are spending as much as ever despite rising prices, trading off rather than cutting back, and offers tips for avoiding buyer's remorse on holiday deals. It also says the energy chief argues the "age of electricity" is rolling on even though climate talks imploded, and asks whether Japan can cash in on the world's appetite for anime. The Journal notes a Michigan couple in their 80s face a 92% hike in long-term-care premiums, bringing their bill to nearly 11,000 dollars a year.

Turning to other national news. Andrea Stewart-Cousins, New York's first female state Senate majority leader, has died at 76 after 20 years in the chamber. Her sudden death leaves a leadership void among top state Democrats. The Times also reports that New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani had successful Achilles tendon surgery.

In sports business, the Journal tells of a family feud as big as Texas: after the death of Texans founder Bob McNair, his sons Cal and Cary are fighting over a 9.65 billion dollar empire that includes the Houston team. Cary alleges Cal exploited their mother's declining health, Cal's lawyers say Cary sought control himself, and the distrust is spilling into the front office.

The Times marks several deaths: Geese Ausbie, the Harlem Globetrotters star; Martha Firestone Ford, owner of the Detroit Lions, at 101; Paolo Bulgari, jewelry heir, at 89; Irish folk musician Donal Lunny, at 79; and a father who crusaded against steroids in sports.

A few other stories from the Times: a patient has marked one year living with a pig kidney, a transplant milestone; more American women are turning to natural family planning, a study shows; a family gathered for an unusual Thanksgiving after a detonation in Iowa; a city fears losing its pier after a section collapsed into the sea; and the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, postponed by an East Coast storm, steps off this morning. Sales of Grand Theft Auto VI have broken entertainment records.

In culture, Angelina Jolie and Brad Pitt have settled a lawsuit that aired abuse allegations, and the Met is scrambling for a tenor who can sing in Czech. Alanis Morissette has made a Christmas album, a Los Angeles bar manager has sued director Antoine Fuqua, and thousands were evacuated from a Don Toliver concert in England after reports of an unknown substance in the crowd. Disney's Bob Iger is the subject of a new biography the Times says depicts him as insecure and vindictive.

Here in San Diego, the Union-Tribune's lead story gives new details on the mosque shootings. Court documents allege that a half hour before two teens ambushed a San Diego mosque and killed three congregants, they were messaging a North Carolina girl about the planned attack, and one mounted a phone on a tactical helmet to livestream it.

Prosecutors say the girl, 17, was one of several people in contact with the teens, was involved in the plot's online dimension and helped shape their ideas. She was arrested in North Carolina and charged.

Also on the front page, more shoppers are turning to artificial intelligence for Black Friday hunts. Die-hards still lined up before dawn at stores like Best Buy in Mission Valley, but many used AI tools to track prices and find deals.

Another A1 story, from the Los Angeles Times, finds California paid about 190 billion dollars more in federal taxes than it got back, roughly 5,000 dollars per resident, making it the biggest donor state in America, a fact Governor Newsom has used in his fights with Washington.

Business on the front page: San Diego's life-science real estate market, which has been in a slump, may be starting a rebound, with a recent report noting stronger demand and tenants negotiating leases. Inside, the Union-Tribune has a guide to holiday events across the county, a new poet laureate in Oceanside, and word that a San Diego Zoo elephant is expected to fully recover. Heavy rain is forecast to replace the king tides.

The paper also reports that Mexico has filed a formal complaint over a DEA tip hotline. I could not independently confirm the details this morning.

San Diego State couldn't complete a rally in Las Vegas and now turns its attention to Washington State, then hosts UNLV. The Union-Tribune also has a "familiar face in bruin blue" feature and says its Thanksgiving poll delivered the biggest upset of the postseason.

UConn is struggling, and coach Geno Auriemma ripped into his players after a third loss, while top-ranked South Carolina is unbeaten. And the Journal's most-read story says "Bazooka Joe" just blew up the Dallas Cowboys' season.

Morning Briefing

Monday, November 2, 2026

Sources: The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental web reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, November 2, 2026. Americans vote tomorrow in midterm elections that will shape the last two years of President Trump's second term, and the final New York Times and Siena polls suggest Democrats have a real shot at winning the Senate, not just the House.

Republicans hold fifty-three Senate seats, so Democrats need to flip four to take the majority, since Vice President JD Vance would break a tie. The final polls give Democrats the edge in six of seven battleground states, and they even lead in Texas and Kansas, where voters haven't sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932. Just thirty-eight percent of likely voters in those states approve of the president's job performance, and Democrats hold a nine-point advantage on the cost of living, with gas prices that soared during the Iran war weighing on voters. The Times' own analysts caution that polls are not predictions. Republicans have the map and the money, with Trump's super PAC raising nearly four hundred fifty million dollars and Elon Musk contributing more than four hundred million, and voters who made up their minds in the last month are breaking toward Republicans by nine points. More than eleven billion dollars has been spent on advertising, a record for a midterm. Winning the House alone would let Democrats stall the president's agenda and gain subpoena power, while the Senate would give them a veto over judges and cabinet nominees.

Democratic unity, meanwhile, is fraying at the worst possible moment. In Michigan, Representative Haley Stevens, who lost the Senate primary to progressive Abdul El-Sayed, stunned her party less than forty-eight hours before Election Day by endorsing Republican Mike Rogers. The Wall Street Journal reports that allies of party leaders privately blame an insurgent left wing for draining time and money from races that should have been easier, while progressives say the establishment isn't doing everything it can to help them. Whatever happens Tuesday, the results are expected to accelerate a fight over the party's future. In other political news, Connecticut Senator Chris Murphy had a stroke over the weekend, and his team says he is awake, alert, and hopeful of a full recovery. And a federal judge in Alaska dismissed the Trump administration's lawsuit that pre-emptively challenged the results of the Senate race there, in which two candidates share the same name.

Overseas, the Iran war is in a diplomatic phase. Iranian negotiators left talks with Qatari mediators in Doha on Sunday, and an official says that was not because of trouble in the negotiations. A draft proposal is circulating, and a follow-up meeting is possible. Iran says the basis for the talks is a seven-step plan from September to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and end the U.S. naval blockade, and its foreign minister suggested the strait could open within two days rather than seven. President Trump rejected Iran's first proposal, but he says the talks have been productive and that the United States will not launch new attacks before the vote, though he hasn't ruled out more military action. Tehran warns that a renewed war could last longer than the last round. Hopes for a deal helped push crude oil down about four percent to just under a hundred dollars a barrel.

On the Russia and Ukraine front, the Journal reports exclusively that Washington is pushing to restore Russian natural-gas sales to Europe through pipelines that run across Ukraine, as part of talks aimed at an energy ceasefire before winter. The idea was floated by envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff on trips to Moscow and Kyiv in September. It would route the gas through a U.S. intermediary to get around European sanctions, and could open a lucrative opportunity for American investors, but it faces resistance in European capitals. Separately, a multi-day local ceasefire in the Russian-controlled Ukrainian city of Oleshky has ended with no reported violations.

On Wall Street, stock futures are higher, with the Dow up about four-tenths of a percent and the Nasdaq up closer to six-tenths, after a rough Friday for AI stocks. The big story is Apple, which passed Nvidia to become the world's most valuable public company, not because it soared but because it slipped only a little over one percent during Friday's selloff. Apple reports full-year results after the bell today, along with Palantir, and analysts expect roughly one dollar ninety-nine a share on about one hundred thirteen billion dollars in revenue. That AI anxiety runs through the Journal's front page, which reports that investors and even tech executives are hunting for insurance against the biggest market bet in history. OpenAI has raised less than half of the thirty billion dollars it targeted in its latest round, and lenders are demanding tougher terms from AI firms. The ISM manufacturing report arrives this morning, and the October jobs report is due Friday. The crypto industry, meanwhile, is still reeling from a six-point-three billion dollar Binance heist.

Elsewhere, a business jet crashed near a busy road in South Florida, killing all nine people on board. A federal judge dismissed Trump's defamation lawsuit over the Journal's article about a birthday note to Jeffrey Epstein. In Europe, stretched public finances and an aging population are straining the cradle-to-grave welfare model and stoking public anger. And China is carrying out one of the largest peacetime naval buildups in modern history, with as many as a hundred ships at sea on any given day, challenging American maritime dominance on a scale not seen since World War II. The Supreme Court opens its two-week November argument session today.

A few lighter notes to close. Sweden's Andreas Almgren won the New York City Marathon in two hours, six minutes, and one second, beating Italy's Yemaneberhan Crippa by thirteen seconds in his first competitive marathon, while Kenya's Sheila Chepkirui won the women's race for a second time. Italy's Kimi Antonelli became the youngest Formula One world champion with a third-place finish in Mexico. And back home in San Diego, the Chargers fell to 0 and 7 against the Rams, a local educator was chosen to compete for National Teacher of the Year, and the city is fighting for control of Liberty Station.

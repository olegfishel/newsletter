Good morning, it's Wednesday, July 29, 2026.

The dominant story today is the escalating conflict between the United States and Iran, now entering a dangerous new phase. Iran launched a surprise ballistic-missile attack on American forces stationed at a base in Jordan — the latest in a series of strikes that have grown both more frequent and more accurate over recent weeks. U.S. Central Command reported that all missiles were intercepted, but the attack carries heavy symbolic weight: it is Iran's first ballistic-missile strike since President Trump paused American air strikes last week to allow diplomats another attempt at a ceasefire deal. Iran's Revolutionary Guard appears to be signaling it will not sit idle while negotiations play out. At the same time, U.S. and Saudi forces conducted coordinated air strikes on Iranian-backed militias operating in Iraq — a response that underscores just how multi-front and complex this conflict has become.

Against that backdrop, President Trump welcomed Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu to the White House yesterday for their first in-person meeting since the two leaders jointly launched the war against Iran in February. The session lasted roughly ninety minutes and was described by both sides as productive. Netanyahu reportedly pressed Trump to maintain the military campaign and further degrade Iran's capabilities before any deal is struck; Trump, who has been nudging toward negotiations, declined to offer the explicit commitment Netanyahu sought. Still, both leaders left the Oval Office presenting a united front, with Netanyahu calling it one of the best conversations they had ever had. The meeting took place against the backdrop of Lindsey Graham's funeral in Washington, where Trump eulogized the longtime South Carolina senator as "one of the great figures in Senate history." Graham, who died this month at 71, drew world leaders to Washington National Cathedral, including Netanyahu and Ukrainian President Zelensky.

The broader Middle East picture grew more complicated overnight. The Houthis in Yemen claimed another attack on a Saudi tanker, extending their declared blockade of Red Sea shipping lanes. Iran also publicly rejected a proposal from Oman to evenly divide control of the Strait of Hormuz — a vital chokepoint for global energy markets. And in a grim footnote, Iran publicly executed two protesters who had participated in demonstrations in January, drawing international condemnation from human rights organizations.

Turning to domestic politics, the democratic socialist movement is making mainstream Democrats increasingly nervous, and today's Wall Street Journal 10-Point leads with the phenomenon. The Democratic Socialists of America now count more than 120,000 members, and DSA-backed candidates have been defeating incumbent Democrats in high-profile primaries, capitalizing on popular discontent with the party establishment. Observers are drawing uncomfortable comparisons to the Tea Party wave that roiled Republicans more than a decade ago. Congressional primaries today in Missouri and Michigan could add to the socialist column — or check its momentum, depending on turnout.

Today is also Federal Reserve day. The FOMC wraps up its two-day meeting this afternoon, with Chair Kevin Warsh set to announce the rate decision at 2 p.m. Eastern. Nearly every economist surveyed expects the Fed to hold rates steady at 3.5 to 3.75 percent for a fifth consecutive meeting. Warsh has kept his cards unusually close to the chest — making today's announcement harder to predict than it has been in recent cycles. Inflationary pressures remain elevated, crude oil is surging this morning by more than four and a half percent to nearly 83 dollars a barrel, and the 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.64 percent. S&P 500 futures are slightly positive ahead of the decision, which also includes a press conference at 2:30 p.m.

On the corporate front, Boeing posted another quarterly loss, though its deficit narrowed as the company devoted more resources to the new Air Force One jets and ramped up commercial aircraft production. The FAA separately ordered airlines to inspect hundreds of Boeing 737 MAX planes for improperly installed cabin seats. Meta priced a $12.5 billion bond offering to fund its data-center expansion, but had to pay a higher interest rate than it did on a similar deal last year — a sign that AI companies flooding the bond market are beginning to test investor appetite. Apple introduced a product-leasing program with Klarna, letting customers pay monthly for iPhones and other devices and then return, upgrade, or buy them outright. Visa reported higher quarterly revenue as consumers kept spending despite inflationary headwinds. Coca-Cola raised its full-year earnings outlook, crediting its World Cup marketing campaign with lifting volume.

In Europe, the climate story is growing impossible to ignore. Some of the largest wildfires in modern European history are sweeping through Spain and southwestern France, threatening the outskirts of Madrid and the ancient wine estates near Bordeaux — some dating to the fourteenth century. The New York Times reports this morning that 2026 is already running ahead of last year's record-breaking fire season in terms of acres burned. The fires had an unusual origin: an exceptionally wet winter that created dense fuel loads, which dried out rapidly under weeks of record heat. A new heat wave bearing down today — pushing temperatures above 104 degrees Fahrenheit in parts of Spain — threatens to intensify the blazes further. Spain's Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called the situation "a wake-up call." Thousands of new evacuations were ordered overnight in Castellón.

In Japan, a powerful earthquake struck the southwestern part of the country, killing at least thirteen people and triggering evacuation orders for more than 200,000 residents. Fires broke out and roads were damaged, with rescue operations ongoing. Back in Washington, the Senate confirmed Jay Clayton as the new director of national intelligence. And the Trump administration announced plans to end a subsidy program that has helped hold down Medicare prescription drug premiums, a move that could leave many seniors facing higher costs for coverage next year. Separately, the president stepped up pressure on Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to further restrict childhood vaccines, focusing on their alleged link to autism — even as White House advisers reportedly want to dial back the controversy ahead of the midterm elections.

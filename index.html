Morning Briefing

Tuesday, August 25, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal front-page capture and 10-Point newsletter (yesterday's edition, Aug. 24) • New York Times: The Morning (Aug. 25) • San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition (Aug. 25) • AP, Reuters, Bloomberg, Washington Post, NPR web reporting

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 25, 2026. The Trump administration has launched its most aggressive economic campaign yet against Iran, threatened to double auto tariffs on Canada, and won a preliminary Supreme Court battle over mail-in voting — all in the span of a single news day. It is a lot to sort through, so let's take it piece by piece.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stepped before cameras Monday to announce what the administration is calling "Operation Economic Outcast" — a sweeping new sanctions campaign designed, in Bessent's words, to "sever every economic lifeline that sustains this tyrannical regime until Tehran stands alone." The program targets nearly sixty individuals, entities, and vessels connected to Iran's nuclear and missile programs, cyberoperations, and oil revenues. It extends secondary sanctions into five sectors: aviation, digital assets, gold, shipping, and technology. The message to trading partners — particularly China, which accounts for roughly ninety percent of Iranian oil purchases — was direct: stop doing business with Tehran, or face consequences. Iranian Economy Minister Ali Madanizadeh was unbowed, declaring "You will fail this time too." The announcement added yet another dimension to the conflict that has already pulled the United States into a wartime posture in the region. Separately, the Wall Street Journal reports that independent trackers who count the world's oil shipments cannot verify U.S. claims about how much oil is actually flowing through the Strait of Hormuz, raising pointed questions about the administration's stated progress in squeezing Iran's energy revenue.

On the trade front, President Trump on Monday threatened to impose fifty percent tariffs on automobiles, trucks, auto parts, and steel from Canada beginning January first — a dramatic escalation of a tit-for-tat trade war that erupted over the weekend after bilateral talks collapsed. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who had walked away from what he called a "bad deal," pledged to retaliate dollar for dollar. The Journal's 10-Point newsletter noted that Canada still sends the vast majority of its exports to the United States, meaning the standoff carries substantial economic risk for both countries. The U.S. had already imposed fifty percent duties on twenty billion dollars in Canadian goods on Saturday; the auto escalation, set for January, represents a further doubling of pressure on an industry that is deeply integrated across the two countries' border.

In a significant legal development with direct ramifications for November's midterm elections, the Supreme Court handed the Trump administration a six-to-three victory Monday, clearing the way for a key piece of the president's mail-in voting executive order. The order directs the Department of Homeland Security to compile state-by-state lists of eligible citizens, which the Postal Service would then use to block mail ballots from reaching anyone not on those rolls. Critics argue the system is far more likely to disqualify legitimate voters than to catch fraud, noting that voter fraud is exceptionally rare. The three liberal justices dissented sharply. Justice Sonia Sotomayor wrote that the ruling says nothing about whether the president's directives are actually lawful — it merely delays adjudication. The decision comes just two months before the midterms, and several states that had sued to block the order warned of coming chaos. As a political backdrop, the New York Times notes that Democrats' prospects of retaking the Senate appear to be improving as Trump's approval ratings have slipped.

Another story rattling Washington: the Journal reports that President Trump personally intervened in the federal government's antitrust pursuit of Live Nation, calling a senior Justice Department official and instructing him to settle the case. The resulting deal preserved the concert giant intact, abandoning years of pressure to force a breakup that would have separated Ticketmaster from its parent company. Trump's call — which officials describe as striking even by the standards of an administration that has openly pressured law enforcement in other matters — speaks to a broader pattern. As the Journal notes, White House involvement in enforcement matters, once considered taboo, is now a regular feature of Trump's second term.

On Wall Street, all eyes are fixed on Nvidia, whose earnings call is set for Wednesday. Analysts project record revenue of approximately ninety-two billion dollars for the period, up from an estimate of seventy-eight billion at the start of the year. The Journal describes the coming report as a high-stakes test of investor faith in the artificial intelligence boom. Chip-maker stocks have been volatile in recent months, dragging on the broader tech indexes — on Monday, the S&P 500 fell three-tenths of a percent and the Nasdaq dropped eight-tenths, while the Dow edged up. Futures this morning are pointing modestly higher. In a related geopolitical thread, Taiwan prosecutors have charged nine people, including former employees of Nvidia and Super Micro, with helping to ship advanced AI servers to China.

There is a deeply troubling story out of the New York Times this morning about the toll professional football takes on the human brain. A major new study found that of 878 former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021, researchers were able to examine 235 brains — and 215 of them showed signs of chronic traumatic encephalopathy, the degenerative disease known as CTE. Applying that rate to today's active rosters suggests at least four hundred of the roughly sixteen hundred players currently on NFL teams will develop the condition. Researchers warn the actual rate may be far higher, since most of the remaining brains were never tested. CTE can be diagnosed only after death and produces memory loss, confusion, dramatic mood swings, and in some cases suicide. Times reporter John Branch, who has covered the subject for fifteen years, noted pointedly that OSHA essentially does not regulate professional football. The sport is simply too large — generating more than twenty-three billion dollars in revenue in 2024, with thirty-five of last year's top fifty U.S. telecasts — for government to want to confront it. One researcher told Branch that Russian roulette has better odds.

From the foreign policy desk: the United Kingdom and France are providing classified technology to Kyiv that will allow Ukraine to manufacture an Anglo-French cruise missile for use in its ongoing war with Russia. Ukraine's sovereignty day was marked with celebrations in Kyiv and among its allies. Meanwhile, the U.S. scrapped a large-scale amphibious exercise with South Korea scheduled for next month — the latest American military pullback in Asia as attention and resources shift toward the Iran conflict. The Trump administration also put forward a revised six-figure H-1B visa fee proposal, replacing an earlier hundred-thousand-dollar charge that courts had struck down.

And a lighter note to close the national canvas: Apollo Global Management chief Marc Rowan has for twelve years operated a seafood shack in Montauk that draws celebrities and influencers with sunset views, Dom Pérignon, and a ninety-seven-dollar lobster salad. The neighbors say the place simply shouldn't be there. After years of litigation, a judge has reset the entire dispute to what he described as "square one." Some battles cannot be won with money.

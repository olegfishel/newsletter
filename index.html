Good morning, it's Sunday, September 6, 2026.

The United States and Iran are locked in the most dangerous confrontation over the Strait of Hormuz in decades, and yesterday it escalated again. Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at an American aircraft carrier and destroyer, a brazen act that drew a swift and forceful American counterstrike. United States Central Command confirmed it "permanently disabled" two Iranian oil tankers and "completely destroyed" a third in response, carrying out what the Pentagon has been calling a "tanker-for-tanker" policy approved personally by President Trump. Neither of the targeted American warships was struck and no service members were injured, but the fact that an Iranian ballistic missile was fired at a carrier — the symbol of American naval supremacy — is a significant new threshold. CENTCOM chief Admiral Brad Cooper warned that the U.S. would not hesitate to defend American forces "and, if necessary, destroy Iran's limited and exposed oil fleet."

This confrontation is now more than six months old. It began in earnest on February 28, when the United States and Israel launched massive joint strikes against Iran after nuclear negotiations collapsed. Tehran responded by seizing effective control of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil flows. The U.S. has been guiding commercial vessels through the strait, but overall traffic remains severely depressed. Iran has used foreign front companies and other tactics to continue exporting oil — and, as the Wall Street Journal's 10-Point newsletter reveals this morning, some of that Iranian money has been flowing directly through American banks. The Treasury Department identified roughly nine billion dollars in Iranian funds passing through U.S. lenders in 2024 alone, hidden behind foreign partners and shell entities that make detection extraordinarily difficult. It turns out that while Washington has been waging a military and economic campaign against Tehran, Iran's own adversaries have been quietly exploiting the very financial system the sanctions regime is meant to defend.

The fuel market is feeling all of it. Diesel hit an average of five dollars and eighty-five cents a gallon on Friday, an all-time record that surpassed the previous high set during the supply disruptions of 2022. The current level is nearly sixty percent higher than a year ago. The war's disruption to Strait of Hormuz shipping, Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries, and Russia's extended diesel export ban have combined to squeeze global supplies to a critical low. Truckers and logistics companies are absorbing costs that will eventually pass to consumers, and the political pressure on the White House is building ahead of the November midterm elections.

Against that stormy backdrop, Friday's jobs report delivered a genuine bright spot. The American economy added 162,000 jobs in August, far outpacing the consensus forecast of just 56,000 — the strongest monthly gain since March. The unemployment rate held steady at 4.1 percent, a historically low level suggesting the labor market shook off its early-summer doldrums. The good news carries a complicated caveat: traders now put the odds of a September Federal Reserve rate hike at better than sixty percent. Stocks fell on the news — the Dow fell half a percent, the S&P 500 a touch less, and the Nasdaq slipped about a third of a percent — as investors priced in the prospect of tighter money.

On the diplomatic front, a rare glimmer of potential progress emerged in Ukraine. Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner flew from Moscow to Kyiv this morning, carrying the outlines of Russia's position after meeting with President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin on Saturday. Ukraine declared it was ready to pause strikes on Moscow through Monday and expected Russia to do the same regarding Kyiv — a three-day pause, fragile and narrow, but the first of its kind in months. The envoys are expected to brief Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky on what Russia may accept as terms for a broader political settlement. Zelensky's negotiators have been warned, however, that Ukraine's own military procurement system is riddled with mismanagement: internal audits reviewed by the Times reveal the country lost about 1.2 billion dollars to fraud and waste in 2024 alone.

In Germany, voters in the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt head to the polls today in an election that could produce the first far-right state government in the country since the end of the Nazi era. The outcome is being watched closely across Europe. Meanwhile, the Summer of 2026 is going into the history books as one of the most meteorologically brutal on record. The New York Times's climate correspondent David Gelles writes this morning about what he calls "Bummer Time" — a summer when a super El Niño amplified global warming's effects, canceling Fourth of July parades in Philadelphia because of a 103-degree heat index, blanketing Chicago in Canadian wildfire smoke, and flooding communities from Colorado to Massachusetts. July was the warmest month on record for the contiguous United States, per the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Scientists say the pattern is not an aberration but the new baseline.

Back home in domestic politics, the two parties are shaping up for a consequential midterm. According to new data cited by the Times, the share of Americans identifying as Democrats has grown significantly over the past year. Republicans face a difficult needle to thread: they need the Trump base but cannot afford a national referendum on a president whose approval is at a record low. Trump's super PAC is pouring ten million dollars into the Texas Senate race, backing Republican Ken Paxton against Democrat James Talarico. In Congress, Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania — a potential swing vote — is drawing fire from fellow Democrats who say he has shown little interest in his actual legislative duties, churning through staff and clashing with colleagues. The Trump administration, meanwhile, is advancing a proposal to extend childcare subsidies to married couples where one spouse stays home, a significant policy shift that would be felt most in Republican-leaning states.

A few other stories to note this Sunday. Federal regulators have opened a safety probe into Tesla's steering-wheel-free Cybercab. Adobe named insider Anil Chakravarthy as its next CEO, succeeding Shantanu Narayen. Windrose, the electric trucking startup touted as a Tesla rival, is running low on funds and shedding workers. And carmakers including Hyundai, Ford, and Jeep parent Stellantis are betting on a new category of vehicle — the extended-range electric vehicle — that runs on electricity but carries a small gas-powered generator as a range extender. One planned Ram pickup promises up to 690 miles, more than twice the typical EV range. For a country still sorting out its relationship with the electric vehicle, it may be the practical compromise that actually sells.

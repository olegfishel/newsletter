Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 10, 2026. The story dominating boardrooms and Washington alike this morning is the unraveling situation at Nvidia, the chipmaker whose products power the artificial intelligence boom. Over the weekend, chief executive Jensen Huang acknowledged an affair with Alice Huang, who is no relation, an executive at a Singapore-based data-center company called Megaspeed. U.S. intelligence officials have concluded she was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. The Wall Street Journal's 10-Point takes readers to a late-night gathering at a Taipei bar in 2024, where she told fellow executives that Jensen was a great friend, sent a text, and minutes later the Nvidia chief arrived for a whiskey. Around that time, Megaspeed was preparing to spend roughly two billion dollars on Nvidia chips, and American and Asian authorities began asking whether the firm was illegally diverting them to China.

Nvidia's board has now opened its own investigation, and the company says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. Huang remains in his job during the review. But the episode has exposed a striking governance gap: Nvidia has no chairman and no succession plan, Huang has some sixty executives reporting directly to him, and the board is filled with early investors and longtime business partners, which makes disciplining him a real challenge. The stock fell more than four percent on Monday, wiping out roughly two hundred billion dollars in market value, though it edged higher after hours. Beijing calls the allegations a smear. President Trump says Huang told him he had no idea she was a suspected spy, and that he believes him. Still, the Justice Department has taken over the federal probe into Megaspeed, and the Republican leaders of three House committees have asked Huang for a private briefing this month. There is a political layer too, since Huang persuaded the president last year to loosen part of the ban on chip sales to China.

Oddly, the broader market barely flinched. Stocks surged on Monday, with the S&P 500 up two point three percent, the Nasdaq up two point seven, and the Dow up two point two, retracing last week's wild swings and ending roughly where they began the prior week. This morning is less cheerful. The October consumer price report showed prices rising four tenths of a percent for the month, above forecasts, with core prices up three tenths, and that has revived talk of the Federal Reserve staying tough. Nasdaq futures are down about one percent and S&P futures about half a percent, with details of Anthropic's planned stock offering also drawing investor attention. Crude oil is down nearly three percent, near eighty-four dollars a barrel, gold is lower, and the ten-year Treasury yield sits just under five percent.

In Washington, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth holds a news conference this morning, his first since May, to fight the push to impeach him. Kentucky Republican Thomas Massie has introduced eight articles of impeachment accusing Hegseth of endangering troops and waging the Iran war without congressional approval. Hegseth calls it a farce driven by policy disagreements rather than high crimes. A House vote could come as soon as midweek, and with Republicans holding a razor-thin majority of two hundred eighteen to two hundred fourteen, only a handful of defections would matter, in a conference where many members blame the Iran war for their election losses. Meanwhile, Louisiana's Steve Scalise is challenging Ohio's Jim Jordan for the top House Republican post. After a landslide Democratic win driven by economic frustration, the Journal asks what the party can realistically do about affordability, even as Democrats are already moving to investigate some of corporate America's biggest names. California voters rejected a proposed billionaire tax, ending an expensive fight over what would have been the nation's first modern wealth tax. The Supreme Court allowed the administration to keep building a border wall along a three-hundred-mile stretch of southwest Texas, including inside a national park, and a federal judge ordered New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani to comply with a federal subpoena seeking help identifying immigrants with serious criminal histories.

Overseas, Russia, Ukraine and the United States opened two days of talks in Abu Dhabi, the first such meeting since February. Special envoy Jared Kushner says recent contacts have narrowed the gaps and that the president is committed to a deal, though the two sides remain far apart on territory and security guarantees. The talks come against the backdrop of the Iran war, which drained global oil stockpiles and has countries and companies racing to refill tanks and build new storage. The Journal reports that the U.S. military moved nine aerial refueling planes from England to Germany and Spain because of a threat from Iran, even as Mr. Trump says renewed military action is unlikely and the war may end soon. Canada reimposed sanctions on eleven Iranian entities, and American and French officials suspect Iran was behind a cyberattack that disabled refrigerators and freezers at European supermarkets. In Britain, comedian Russell Brand was sentenced to ten and a half years in prison for rape and sexual assault.

In business news, ExxonMobil signed a preliminary deal to explore oil investments in Venezuela, nearly two decades after leaving. Skydance replaced James Gunn and Peter Safran at the top of DC Studios with Jeremy Latcham. The Children's Place filed for bankruptcy, Goodyear's chief executive stepped down, and SiriusXM named Anjali Sud as its new chief executive. Regulators will require eye-condition warnings on GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Zepbound, and the Journal reports that America's cattle herd has shrunk to its smallest in seventy-five years, so expensive beef is likely here to stay.

To close on lighter notes, Phil Collins is heading into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, M. John Harrison became the first science fiction writer to win the Booker Prize, and a restored cut of the once-censored film "The Devils" is selling out theaters. Closer to home, the San Diego City Council approved the four-point-two-billion-dollar Midway Rising project, and San Diego State will stick with Saunders at quarterback as it hosts Utah State.

Business & Finance

Major U.S. stock indexes surged, retracing last week's wild swings to close just above where they stood a week ago, with the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow gaining 2.3%, 2.7% and 2.2%, respectively.

SiriusXM named board member Anjali Sud as its chief executive officer to succeed Jennifer Witz, who will serve in an advisory role until the end of next year.

Children's Place filed for chapter 11 after an effort by a Saudi family to turn the apparel retailer around failed to keep it out of bankruptcy court.

Goodyear Tire & Rubber said Mark Stewart stepped down as chief executive officer and president and from the company's board with immediate effect.

World-Wide

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's neglect of succession planning has become a risk for the company amid an investigation into reports that the tech leader was targeted by a suspected Chinese spy.

The House proceeded toward a vote on whether to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who plans to hold a news conference on Tuesday to oppose the effort.

Louisiana Rep. Steve Scalise is making a bid to become the top House Republican, squaring off against Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio for the minority leader post.

The Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to proceed with construction of a border wall along a 300-mile stretch in southwest Texas.

California voters rejected a proposed billionaire tax, ending a heated and expensive fight over what could have been the nation's first modern tax on wealth.

U.S. regulators required the makers of GLP-1 drugs such as Ozempic and Zepbound to include warnings of a risk of a rare eye condition that can cause permanent vision loss.

Actor and TV presenter Russell Brand was sentenced to 10 years and six months in prison after he was convicted of rape and sexual assault.

The U.S. and France suspect Iran is responsible for the cyberattack on European supermarket refrigerators and freezers that caused large financial and material losses.

Morning Briefing

Monday, July 27, 2026

Sources: Wall Street Journal (front page & 10-Point newsletter), The New York Times (The Morning), San Diego Union-Tribune (e-Edition), supplemented by Reuters, Bloomberg, AP, and Washington Post reporting.

Good morning, it's Monday, July 27, 2026. The story commanding the most attention right now is the pause in America's military campaign against Iran — now in its third day — with diplomacy quietly stirring on both sides and markets watching closely ahead of a week packed with big technology earnings and a Federal Reserve interest-rate decision. Meanwhile, a landmark moment in immigration policy arrives today as Temporary Protected Status expires for hundreds of thousands of people, most of them Haitian, who have lived and worked legally in the United States for years.

President Trump held off what officials described as a major escalation of the Iran campaign on Friday, ending nearly two weeks of consecutive nightly strikes. The pause followed a remarkable White House debate in which Vice President JD Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman General Dan Caine both raised concerns about the trajectory of the conflict. The top American military commander in the Middle East, Admiral Brad Cooper, warned that a broader offensive could dangerously deplete air-defense munitions stockpiles in the region and that the bombing had reached the limit of its effectiveness, with too few high-value targets remaining. At the same time, diplomats are pressing Iran to engage on reopening the Strait of Hormuz. A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran will hold its own retaliatory strikes as long as the American pause holds — a fragile but notable de-escalation. The Israeli prime minister is headed to Washington this week to attend the funeral of the late Senator Lindsey Graham and to meet with Trump at the White House. Separately, attacks between Saudi Arabia and Houthi rebels in Yemen are intensifying, threatening a four-year truce that had largely held.

On the technology front, the week begins with an extraordinary deal taking shape in the AI industry. Nvidia is in talks to provide a roughly $250 billion financial backstop for OpenAI as part of a massive data center project in Piketon, Ohio. The hub, a 10-gigawatt computing complex developed by SoftBank's energy subsidiary, is expected to cost over $500 billion in total once the chips inside are counted. The arrangement would help OpenAI lease infrastructure it would effectively control for the first time — reducing its reliance on Microsoft, Amazon, and Oracle — while locking in years of chip demand for Nvidia. The Journal describes it as one of the most ambitious financial transactions yet in America's AI boom, with the first phase expected to be ready by 2028. Meanwhile, Wall Street itself faces major tests this week: big tech earnings and the Fed's rate decision loom against a backdrop of two straight weeks of notable market declines. The Dow futures are up this morning — over 52,700 — and the VIX fear index has pulled back, but the WSJ's 10-Point newsletter captures what columnist Emma Tucker calls an "unsettled vibe" creeping through markets. There is one genuine bright spot: companies across tech, transportation, railroads, and defense are starting to hire again, defying widespread predictions that artificial intelligence would trigger a wave of job cuts. After a year of caution, firms say they need more workers to operate alongside AI, not fewer.

The New York Times leads this morning with what writer Sam Sifton calls the story of people "protected no more." Today marks the expiration of Temporary Protected Status for approximately 350,000 immigrants, the majority of them Haitian. The humanitarian program was created by Congress in 1990, designed to shield from deportation people whose home countries had been rendered unsafe by armed conflict or natural disaster. For Haitians, the trigger was the catastrophic earthquake of 2010. Last month the Supreme Court cleared the way for the Trump administration to end the program, with Homeland Security calling it an exercise in "upholding the rule of law." Critics and economists are watching the economic ripple effects with concern. A nonpartisan research organization warned that having tens of thousands of workers no longer able to legally work is going to be "a very big hit" for particular industries and regions. Hebrew SeniorLife, a nonprofit care organization in the Boston area, is preparing to lose 40 Haitian employees — housekeepers, cooks, dishwashers, nurses — after already losing 60 workers to earlier immigration policy changes this year. Salvadorans who have been in the TPS program since 1990 face their own expiration deadline in September, representing another 190,000 people.

Elsewhere in business and policy: United Airlines chief executive Scott Kirby last year called Delta's CEO to discuss a potential merger of what would have been the two most valuable carriers in the United States; those talks did not advance. Spirits maker Sazerac is pressing Brown-Forman shareholders to reconsider a $15 billion buyout that the board has rebuffed. Elon Musk's tunneling startup, the Boring Company, is in talks to raise new funding at around a $20 billion valuation. And in an uplifting quirk of the consumer economy, Hasbro's Magic: The Gathering card game is surging in popularity and bucking the broader decline in traditional toy sales. On the political calendar: former Maine state Senate President Troy Jackson has secured the Democratic nomination for the U.S. Senate after his rival Graham Platner dropped out amid a scandal. India's education minister resigned following months of youth-led protests that began as online memes — a sign of how digital organizing is reshaping politics in the world's most populous nation. Brazil denied entry visas to two Trump administration officials, citing concerns they intended to undermine public confidence in the country's electronic voting system. North Korea's Kim Jong Un called for "radical" upgrades to his country's military reconnaissance and intelligence capabilities. And in a lighthearted Journal aside: someone turned all 18 greens at the University of Michigan's golf course — designed by the same architect who shaped Augusta National — a brilliant shade of orange, and no one yet knows who did it.

In San Diego and California this morning: extreme heat and gusty winds are fueling new wildfires across the region. An audit finds that UC and CSU campuses have improved their response to sexual harassment complaints. A labor shortage is feared locally if refugee deportations continue to accelerate. The Padres host the Colorado Rockies this afternoon. And in international cycling, Tadej Pogacar has cemented his status as one of the sport's all-time greats with a dominant Tour de France victory.

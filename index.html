Morning Briefing

Thursday, October 29, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal (front page and The 10-Point), The New York Times (The Morning), the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and supplemental wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 29, 2026. The biggest story overnight is a shifting and somewhat contradictory picture on Iran. Vice President JD Vance has written to Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council and one of Tehran's most powerful hard-liners, proposing a restart of comprehensive peace negotiations. Officials say the letter suggests a meeting soon, with Vance again leading the American delegation as he did in June, and it insists that Iran's nuclear program be on the table from the start. The Wall Street Journal reported that President Trump directed the outreach, though Trump later described it as an effort to help Iran with medical supplies and other things. At the same time, the administration has warned Gulf states that a short, intense renewal of the war is possible after the midterms, and Qatar is working as a go-between, trying to persuade Tehran to admit United Nations inspectors to its bombed nuclear sites in exchange for some sanctions relief. Oil eased in Asia on word of the outreach, though crude is still hovering above one hundred dollars a barrel.

That energy price is part of why the Federal Reserve is so cautious. The central bank held interest rates steady yesterday, but this was no sign of comfort. Two officials, governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack, dissented because they wanted another increase. The Fed is buying time to decide whether more hikes are needed to keep high energy costs and an artificial intelligence building boom from seeping into broader inflation. Stocks rose after the decision, with the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq each gaining about three tenths of a percent and the Dow up two tenths. This morning, though, futures point lower, with Nasdaq futures down more than one percent, and the reason is Big Tech earnings. Alphabet reported revenue growth of twenty-five percent, powered by its cloud business, but growth in search advertising slowed, and market reports have its shares down roughly ten percent in premarket trading. Microsoft, by contrast, saw its cloud growth accelerate again, lifting profit, and its shares are higher. The ten-year Treasury yield is sitting right around five percent. Later today we get the first estimate of third-quarter economic growth and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, along with earnings from Amazon, Eli Lilly, Intel, Mastercard and Starbucks.

In corporate news, one of the most storied names in American law is going away. Leaders at Weil, Gotshal and Manges say they plan to dissolve the firm after a string of high-profile defections and failed merger talks left it with few options. Nelson Peltz's Trian has offered roughly one point four billion dollars, or seven dollars and thirty-five cents a share, to take Wendy's private, arguing the burger chain would be better off out of the public markets. Ford reported lower quarterly profit, weighed down by losses in its electric vehicle unit. And the Journal's lead story in The 10-Point is a reversal: for years, Apple squeezed flash-memory suppliers for deep discounts, but the AI boom has sent flash prices up roughly fivefold. Apple is on track to spend about twenty-five billion dollars on flash this year, up from six billion last year for roughly the same volume, and Tim Cook has likened it to a hundred-year flood.

Turning to politics abroad, Israel's election has produced a familiar result. With most of the vote counted, Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud is headed for the largest number of seats, ahead of an opposition bloc that had made the election a referendum on the prime minister. But his bloc appears to fall short of an outright majority, so the coalition math will matter, and The New York Times says he still has a good chance of clinging to power. In Brazil, Flávio Bolsonaro, who lost the presidential election, slipped out of Brasília in a convoy of decoy cars and flew to Miami, helped by Trump allies, senior American officials and Argentina's president, despite a Brazilian court order barring him from leaving. The Journal reports Trump personally approved admitting the family.

At home, the midterms are now days away, and the news is dominated by dirty-tricks allegations. The Times reports that Democratic operatives worked behind the scenes to put a second Alaskan named Dan Sullivan on the Senate ballot, hoping to confuse voters and siphon support from the Republican incumbent of the same name, a plan the Peltola campaign denies being part of. Alaska's governor has ordered an investigation, and Trump has demanded that Democrat Mary Peltola be prosecuted and removed from the ballot. The Justice Department, meanwhile, has launched a flurry of legal moves to advance Trump's demands to prosecute his political foes. Elsewhere, a judge rejected an administration attempt to make California set aside ballots from people it suspects are noncitizens, and Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. temporarily allowed six unapproved peptides onto the market despite scientists' warnings that the evidence is thin. The United States also expelled a Cuban diplomat after a shouting match at the United Nations.

Overseas, Spain is observing three days of mourning after a high-speed train collision near Urda, in the province of Toledo, killed at least sixty-eight people. In Ukraine, a rare local peace deal announced by First Lady Melania Trump has so far brought a brief respite to the besieged city of Kherson, and a ceasefire there is holding.

Closer to home, the San Diego Union-Tribune reports on a plan to shrink Hodges Dam over flood concerns and local counties pushing back on federal demands for voter information. In sports, the Milwaukee Brewers won their first World Series, beating the Tampa Bay Rays in five games, and the WNBA Finals could be decided this afternoon if the Golden State Valkyries close out the Atlanta Dream.

And a last note: Dolores Huerta, the labor leader who co-founded what became the United Farm Workers, has died at ninety-six.

Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 15, 2026.

The Federal Reserve opens its two-day policy meeting today with the markets holding their breath. Chairman Kevin Warsh — the man President Trump personally chose after years of demanding that his predecessors cut rates — is now widely expected to do the opposite: raise them. At his Jackson Hole appearance in late August, Warsh said inflation is still too sticky and that the Fed may have "work to do," sending the odds of a September hike from roughly one in three to better than two in three almost overnight. The 10-year Treasury yield has already climbed above five percent, a psychological threshold that rattled bond markets on Monday and dragged major stock indexes lower, with the S&P 500 off half a percent, the Nasdaq down six-tenths, and the Dow falling three-tenths of a percent. Adding a further edge to the bond market is a structural shift that the New York Fed has been quietly probing: hedge funds now hold roughly seven percent of the entire Treasury market, having stepped in as traditional pension funds pulled back. Their "basis trades" — borrowing to exploit tiny pricing gaps between bonds and futures — amplify gains in calm markets, but they leave precious little cushion if conditions turn.

Energy markets are another live wire. American oil executives have been warning for months that the Houthi seizure of the Strait of Hormuz — a stunning offensive that began in late February when the U.S. and Israel launched air strikes on Iran — would eventually produce a genuine fuel crisis. They now say it is here. The strait, which normally carries roughly twenty percent of the world's crude, is still shipping well under a quarter of its pre-war volume, and oil settled Monday at $102 a barrel, with Goldman Sachs warning of $120 if the disruption persists. Crude Oil futures ticked up again this morning. The ripple effects are arriving on Main Street: mortgage rates are closing in on seven percent for the first time since early last year, threatening to snuff out what had been expected to be the housing market's recovery year.

The technology debate dominated headlines in parallel, and President Trump was at the center of it. Despite a chorus of leading AI researchers and company founders calling for a slowdown over safety fears — and warnings from OpenAI and Anthropic that their own systems could eventually escape human control — the president rejected any federal guardrails. Writing on social media, Trump dismissed safety concerns as a "hoax" that would hand China a competitive edge and leave American companies in financial ruin, insisting the industry already has everything it needs: "a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT." Wall Street appeared to agree with his timeline if not his reasoning: hyperscaler stocks rose even as the broader market fell, as investors bet that neither safety summits nor congressional hand-wringing would slow the AI buildout. The WSJ reported that OpenAI and Anthropic are quietly racing each other and China toward initial public offerings, even as they privately acknowledge they may not be able to guarantee control of the technology they are releasing. OpenAI, meanwhile, made a smaller move: it quietly acquired Glass Imaging, a startup using artificial intelligence to improve smartphone cameras. And Apple brought its own AI story to consumers — iOS 27 is now available, featuring a substantially rebuilt Siri powered by Apple Intelligence. China's top spy chief offered a remarkable counterpoint to Trump's dismissiveness, saying that AI poses a genuine threat to Communist Party rule — suggesting that concerns about powerful AI are not merely a Western regulatory pretense.

The Supreme Court handed the Trump administration a significant legal defeat on the election front. In a six-to-one-to-two ruling — with Justices Alito and Thomas dissenting — the court declined to revive Trump's executive order directing the U.S. Postal Service to block delivery of mail ballots to voters not on pre-submitted state lists. State and local election officials, who had warned that implementing the new system weeks before the November midterms would have caused chaos, cheered the decision. Justice Kavanaugh wrote a cautious concurrence, suggesting the Postal Service might have the statutory authority to act, but that forcing the change on states now would be arbitrary. The legal fight will continue after the midterms. The ruling arrives as a Times/Siena poll shows Democrats holding a sizable edge over Republicans heading into November, powered largely by Trump's underwater approval ratings.

The immigration crackdown drew pointed coverage from the New York Times today, which detailed the slow-motion economic collapse unfolding in Key West since the administration ended Temporary Protected Status for Haitians in July. Of Key West's roughly 25,000 residents, an estimated 5,000 are Haitian — hotel housekeepers, restaurant cooks, Uber drivers, supermarket workers. Many have been detained; others simply stopped coming in to work. The snowbird season hasn't started yet; the real labor pinch is still weeks away. Separately, an inspector general's report found that the now-closed "Alligator Alcatraz" detention facility in the Florida Everglades failed national standards for medical care, food, hygiene, and recreation, and used small metal cages — about the size of a restroom stall — called "calming areas." New York City's mayor announced a coalition of cities has sued to challenge federal rules that would make it easier to deny green cards to immigrants who use public benefits. And a federal court in New York began hearing a lawsuit by a group of pro-Palestinian students, including Mahmoud Khalil, accusing Columbia University of failing to protect them from harassment.

In other news worth noting: Senator Mitch McConnell appeared on the Senate floor to vote for the first time in three months after a fall that had raised serious questions about whether he would return. The Trump-appointed board of the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts is reportedly moving toward closing the storied Washington venue as soon as today amid financial strain. Ukraine's President Zelenskyy said Kyiv is ready to halt strikes on Russian territory if the conditions are right, while NATO pledged additional support. Fashion designer Bob Mackie, whose sequined and feathered creations adorned Cher, Carol Burnett, and a generation of Hollywood icons, has died at age eighty-seven. And in a story that could become a parable for every trade negotiation of the past decade: a Chinese court ruled that Driscoll's, the California berry giant, had its patent-protected blueberry varieties stolen after it brought the fruit to China hoping to cultivate a new market fifteen years ago. China's blueberry industry boomed — largely at Driscoll's expense.

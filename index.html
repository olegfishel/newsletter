Morning Briefing

Wednesday, November 4, 2026

Compiled from The Wall Street Journal front page and 10-Point, The New York Times Morning newsletter, the San Diego Union-Tribune e-Edition, and current wire and web reporting.

Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 4, 2026. Democrats woke up today in control of both chambers of Congress, after a midterm election that amounted to a sweeping rebuke of President Trump. The Associated Press called the House for Democrats in the early hours of this morning, and the Senate had already tipped their way on Tuesday night, ending two years of unified Republican control in Washington.

The scale of the win surprised even optimistic Democrats. The New York Times counts 228 House seats for the party so far, with that majority expected to grow, and estimates Democrats are on track to win the national House vote by about thirteen points, which would be the largest margin for either party in fifty years. In the Senate, Democrats needed to flip four seats and cleared that bar early in the evening. They have now taken seven Republican-held seats, including Texas, where state legislator James Talarico won, Maine, where Susan Collins lost her seat to Troy Jackson, Ohio, where former senator Sherrod Brown returned, Kansas, South Carolina, Iowa, and Michigan, where Abdul El-Sayed became the first Muslim elected to the Senate. Mississippi is still separated by only a few votes, and Alaska is still counting.

The governors' races told the same story. Gina Hinojosa upset Greg Abbott in Texas, ending decades of Republican dominance there. Amy Acton defeated Vivek Ramaswamy in Ohio, and Keisha Lance Bottoms won in Georgia. Here at home, Xavier Becerra will succeed Gavin Newsom in California. Analysts point to the same ingredients everywhere: anger over high fuel and food prices, turnout that looked more like a presidential year, and a swing of roughly fourteen points toward Democrats in counties dominated by white working-class voters. Republican gerrymanders in seven states largely failed to deliver, and a big Republican spending advantage did not save them.

President Trump, for his part, sounded subdued overnight. In an exclusive interview with the Journal, he acknowledged that the war with Iran was a piece of the Republican losses, though he said he does not regret launching it. He told reporters he was willing to accept the results and work with Democrats, but he also stressed his veto power, and he posted online that he would have won in a landslide had he been on the ballot. The Journal's 10-Point notes that Democrats now have real leverage over his nominees and over government spending, but that their promised action on health care and household costs runs straight into that veto pen. Expect investigations instead, into the early mass firings and the business dealings of the president's family and cabinet. ABC News reports that Speaker Mike Johnson will not seek the post of House minority leader, and Hakeem Jeffries is expected to be elected speaker.

The Iran war is the other great story hanging over all of this. The conflict began in late February and has sharply cut traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, which normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil and gas. U.S. forces struck Iran's Qeshm Island on Monday night, in what officials called defensive action to protect commercial ships, and Brent crude has been trading above a hundred dollars a barrel. The Journal reports that Tehran has turned to guerrilla tactics, using explosive undersea drones, rocket-propelled grenades and limpet mines against the vessels that ferry oil out to waiting tankers, a shadow war aimed at pressuring Washington while negotiations remain deadlocked. Meanwhile, Israel has told American officials that Iran is weighing the use of chemical weapons, and President Trump said the United States will end roughly four billion dollars in annual military aid to Israel at the end of the year, a cutoff that may come far sooner than the gradual phaseout the two countries had discussed.

In Ukraine, rescuers are still digging through the rubble of a school in the Zaporizhzhia region that was being used to hand out humanitarian aid when Russian glide bombs hit it. At least thirty-one people were reported killed at first, and President Zelenskyy now puts the toll at forty, with dozens more missing. The Times reports that the Trump administration says U.S., Ukrainian and Russian officials will resume peace talks early next week. Separately, the Journal reports NATO is drafting a response doctrine that could include strikes deep inside Russia if Moscow attacks alliance territory, and North Korea has stepped up its missile tests while rejecting American diplomatic overtures.

On Wall Street, stocks finished mixed on Tuesday as investors waited for the results, with the S&P 500 and Nasdaq up and the Dow slightly lower. This morning futures have bounced sharply, with Dow futures up more than one and a half percent, as the Democratic sweep and the president's willingness to accept it eased worries about uncertainty. The ten-year Treasury yield is hovering near four point nine percent. In corporate news, CVS Health will split in two, separating its retail pharmacies from its insurance and pharmacy-benefit businesses in the first half of 2028. 3M agreed to pay about seven point three five billion dollars over nine years to settle forever-chemical claims. AMD beat expectations even as its OpenAI deal weighed on profit, and Shopify slid nearly nine percent on lukewarm results. Job openings rose to 7.5 million in September, and today brings ADP's private payrolls report, the ISM services index, and earnings from Uber, McDonald's, Qualcomm, CVS and Novo Nordisk.

A few other stories to know. Investigators say pieces of the tail of the Bahamas-bound jet that crashed in a Florida street were found miles from the impact site, suggesting part of the plane broke away before it hit the ground. The Supreme Court appeared inclined to side with Colorado Catholic preschools seeking public funds without enrolling children of same-sex parents. In San Diego, county voters appear narrowly split on a sales tax measure, the House race has Democrat von Wilpert in the lead, and forecasters warn a heat wave and coastal flooding may arrive together.

Business & Finance

U.S. stocks finished mixed on Tuesday amid investor nerves ahead of the midterm results, with the S&P 500 up 0.4 percent, the Nasdaq up 0.8 percent, and the Dow down 0.4 percent.

Elliott Investment Management compared the current state of financial markets to the Roaring Twenties that preceded the Great Depression.

CVS Health will split into two publicly traded companies, separating its retail pharmacies from its insurance and pharmacy-benefit operations in the first half of 2028.

3M agreed to settle long-running litigation brought by thousands of people who say they were harmed by PFAS, the so-called forever chemicals.

China told its biggest securities firms and banks to slow their work on IPOs and concentrate on supporting the struggling stock market.

U.S. job openings rose to 7.5 million in September from an upwardly revised 7.2 million in August, while hiring held fairly steady.

AMD's sales beat Wall Street expectations, though a deal it struck last year with OpenAI weighed on its third-quarter net income.

Unilever aims to save the equivalent of about 1.12 billion dollars and secure steady sales growth as it narrows its focus to a slimmer core of nonfood products.

Keurig Dr Pepper reported higher profit and sales for the quarter, even though its coffee business is taking longer than expected to recover.

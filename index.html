Good morning, it's Friday, August 14, 2026.

The aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln is finally heading home — but the circumstances of its relief have turned into a mounting national controversy. The Lincoln has been at sea for more than 250 days without a meaningful port call, conducting combat operations against Iran under Operation Epic Fury since late February, with five thousand sailors and Marines aboard. Senate Democrats are pressing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth for answers after reports emerged of moldy showers, chronic shortages of soap, toothpaste, and deodorant, and at least two sailors who attempted to jump overboard in apparent mental health crises. The Wall Street Journal confirmed that the USS George Washington is now steaming toward the Indian Ocean to provide relief, and President Trump's team quietly arranged the rotation after lawmakers from both parties raised alarms. Iran's government dismissed Trump's claims of controlling the Strait of Hormuz as "lies," insisting the waterway remains firmly under its control. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent added to the pressure this week, warning that the United States would pursue economic isolation of Tehran on a scale the world has never seen.

The Lincoln story has local resonance here in San Diego, where the ship is home-ported. The San Diego Union-Tribune reports this morning that a new vessel is expected to relieve the Lincoln in the Mideast — offering families along this coastline their first real hope of reunion after what has become one of the longest unbroken carrier deployments in modern Navy history.

In a related and striking military story, the Journal reports that President Trump has ordered the Navy to rip out the electromagnetic aircraft launch system — known as EMALS — from its new carriers and return to older steam catapults, a decision Navy officials have resisted for years and one that is expected to cost billions of dollars to execute. Trump cited reliability concerns with the newer technology, though the Navy has long maintained that EMALS, despite a troubled early record, represents a generational leap in how quickly aircraft can be launched.

On the political front, the Journal's 10-Point newsletter leads with a deep investigation into how President Trump and Elon Musk quietly rebuilt their alliance after a very public falling out. A year ago, Musk torched the relationship on social media, floated the idea of a third political party, and walked away from the White House. What followed was months of back-channel diplomacy largely steered by Vice President JD Vance, culminating in a conversation on a flight to Beijing where Musk told the White House he plans to spend at least one hundred million dollars — and perhaps as much as a hundred and twenty million — to help Republicans hold the House and Senate this November. His America PAC is targeting Senate races in eight states, including Michigan, Ohio, Maine, and Alaska. Musk spent more than two hundred and sixty million dollars to help elect Trump in 2024, making him the largest single political donor in American history, and both men have much at stake if Democrats regain control of Congress.

Markets are heading into the weekend in good spirits. Two consecutive days of benign inflation data — consumer prices rose just a tenth of a percent in July, and wholesale prices came in flat — have lifted the S&P 500 to a fresh record above 7,800, its best close ever. The Nasdaq gained eight tenths of a percent on the week, and the Dow added a tenth. Tariff refunds are providing an unexpected tailwind to corporate earnings: Apple, Nike, and FedEx are among the major companies recovering significant sums relatively quickly, adding to what analysts are calling a better-than-expected earnings season. Separately, gains on private investments in companies like Anthropic are inflating earnings at some of the biggest tech firms — a quirk that the Journal's Jonathan Weil warns investors to scrutinize carefully.

In a Manhattan federal courtroom today, Luigi Mangione — accused in the December 2024 killing of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson — is expected to enter a guilty plea to federal stalking charges. The case captivated the country last year and today's proceeding marks a significant milestone in the federal prosecution.

The New York Times Morning newsletter devotes its lead story to a sobering question heading into the fall midterm season: can we trust political polls? Chief political analyst Nate Cohn walks through two stunning survey misses from recent Democratic primaries. In Michigan, polls showed Abdul El-Sayed leading his Senate primary by double digits — he won by a single percentage point. In Wisconsin, Francesca Hong carried a more than twenty-point polling lead for governor — she lost outright, with David Crowley, a moderate, surging as establishment candidates consolidated behind him in the final days. Cohn raises the possibility that a phenomenon called "nonresponse bias" — where highly educated and politically engaged voters are far more likely to answer a survey than others — may be systematically distorting primary polls in ways pollsters haven't yet corrected for.

Elsewhere in the news: a federal judge in Boston dismissed the Trump administration's antisemitism lawsuit against Harvard, ruling that the cited incidents were "isolated and episodic" and insufficient to sustain the case. The Kennedy Center's board of trustees voted to close the performing-arts venue for two years and add Trump's name to its facade, according to Representative Joyce Beatty and her attorneys. Flock Safety, whose license-plate surveillance cameras are widely deployed in San Diego and other cities, announced safeguards after a backlash over potential law enforcement abuses. And Tropical Storm Lala is tracking toward Hawaii, where forecasters say it could become the first hurricane to make landfall in more than thirty years.

In business news: China's SMIC posted sharply higher profit and revenue on robust demand for legacy semiconductors. Lenovo kicked off its fiscal year with record sales, expecting to hit its hundred-billion-dollar annual revenue goal ahead of schedule. Danish shipping giant A.P. Moller-Maersk raised its full-year guidance for the second time in under three months, citing higher freight rates and strong Asian demand. Tyson Foods will shutter a major beef-processing plant and sell another — the latest sign of the beef industry's structural troubles. And Tapestry's Coach brand posted a fifteen-percent sales surge, more than offsetting weakness at Kate Spade.
